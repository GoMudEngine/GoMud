## Chapter Twenty — *[Maren]*
### The Restricted Collection

---

The hoop-set cost four marks, which was robbery for bent iron and a handful of rivets. Maren paid without comment. The apprentice at the counter — sixteen, maybe seventeen, with the permanently reddened hands of someone who spent his days around steam — took the coins and nodded her toward the back without looking up from his ledger.

She'd been told the nod was the signal. Davan had explained it twice, the second time with the patient repetition of someone who understood that details kept people alive. Buy the hoop-set. Wait for the nod. Go through the door behind the stave rack. Down.

Davan went first. The stairs were narrow, stone-edged, built for moving barrels not people. The air changed three steps down — oak shavings and vinegar replaced by mineral cold, the particular dampness of stone that never saw sun. She catalogued it automatically: one exit behind her, none visible ahead, walls close enough to touch both sides with her elbows bent.

The cooperage sat two streets from the river, on a lane where the smell of the workshop covered everything else. She'd noted the approaches on the walk over. Three ways out of the lane. Vane was outside, near the gate, doing whatever Vane did when she waited — which was stand very still in a place where she could see a long way and think about angles. Maren had not asked her to come. Vane had simply been there when she left, and had walked beside her without explaining, and had stopped at the gate with a nod that meant *I'll be here*.

The basement opened up at the bottom of the stairs. Low ceiling, stone floor dark at the edges where the damp crept in. Two lamps on a table in the center, their light reaching about as far as the first ring of chairs and failing after that. The corners were suggestions. The room smelled like tallow and cold earth and, faintly, the chemical bite of tanning solution — someone's clothes, not the room itself.

Six people. She counted them before she counted anything else.

---

A woman at the head of the table, seated, both hands flat on the wood. Fifty or near it. Gray threaded through dark hair pulled back with no concession to style. She had the posture of someone who had spent years bending over texts and had eventually decided to stop bending — spine straight, chin level, the overcorrection become habit. That would be Renner. Davan had described her as *the one who runs it*, which Maren had understood to mean *the one nobody has successfully argued down*.

To Renner's left, a woman with the forearms of someone who worked with heat. Glassblower's arms — ropey, scarred at the wrists where the radiant burn caught you if you leaned wrong. Thirty, maybe younger. She sat with her hands in her lap, which was a choice. People who worked with their hands and then sat on them were making a point about patience. Asha.

Across the table, a square-built man with chemical stains worked into the creases of his knuckles that no amount of washing would remove. Tanner. Corvel. In front of him on the table sat an object Maren's gaze caught on and held: a smooth gray container, forearm-length, the surface so uniformly dark it looked less like a thing and more like the absence of a thing. The lamplight reached it and stopped. Did not reflect. Did not slide off. Simply ended.

She made herself look past it.

An older man in the far chair — sixties, weathered in the way of someone who spent time outdoors but thought indoors. Ink on his fingers, but old ink, in the cuticles. Mapmaker's hands. Edvar. He watched her come down the stairs with an expression that gave nothing and took everything in, and she recognized it because it was the expression she wore in rooms full of strangers. Professional assessment.

A young woman beside Edvar, also with ink stains — fresher ones, on the side of her writing hand where the heel dragged. Cartographer's apprentice, or assistant, or whatever the relationship was.

And Pip. She knew Pip already, or knew of him — Davan's friend from the docks district, the one who found things. Twenty-three and built like a question mark, all forward lean and nervous energy. He gave her a nod when she reached the bottom of the stairs. It was the most welcoming gesture she'd received so far.

Davan stepped to the side, giving the room a clear view of her. "This is Maren," he said. "She's the one I told you about."

The silence that followed was not hostile. It was evaluative. Maren stood in it the way she stood in all silences — with her weight even, her hands at her sides, her face offering exactly as much as she chose to offer. Three years of performing a version of herself that wasn't quite true had given her a very precise understanding of what her face did when she let it do nothing. Nothing was usually the right choice.

"Sit," Renner said. Not an invitation. An allocation of resources — there was a chair, Maren was standing, the inefficiency offended her.

Maren sat.

---

The questioning began without preamble.

"Davan says you have an object," Renner said. "Davan says a number of things. I'd like to hear what you say."

"I have an object," Maren said.

Renner's mouth pressed into something almost approving and almost impatient — the mouth of someone who had been used primarily for precision and had lost the knack of signaling anything softer.

"He also says you're hollow."

The word landed on the table between them like a stone dropped from a modest height. Not thrown. Just released. Maren noted the reactions without turning her head: Asha's hands shifted in her lap. Corvel looked at the object in front of him, then back at Maren. The young cartographer's pen stopped moving. Edvar did nothing, which told her Edvar already knew.

"Yes," Maren said.

"How long?"

"Always. I'm unchanged."

"And you've been concealing it for —"

"Three years."

Renner processed this — the slight narrowing of focus as she fitted the new piece into an existing framework and discovered the framework needed to flex. "Three years in a city that tests for the Chrysalis at employment, at housing registration, at —"

"Yes."

"How?"

"I'm very good at not being tested."

Another silence. This one had a different texture — not evaluative but recalibrating. Maren had just told a room full of strangers that she'd committed what amounted to three years of continuous civic fraud. The fact that she said it without drama, without invitation to elaborate, just the fact laid down like a stone in a wall — it registered differently with each of them. Pip looked impressed. Asha looked thoughtful. Renner looked like she was revising an estimate upward.

"Show me the object," Renner said.

Maren didn't reach for it yet. "I want to understand what I'm showing it to."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning Davan told me what this group studies. Founding-era material. The gap between what the bloodline claims and what the evidence supports. I want to know what question you're trying to answer before I show you what I might be the answer to."

The room shifted. Not physically — no one moved — but the quality of attention changed. Maren had just demonstrated that she'd listened to Davan, processed what he'd told her, and arrived at an inference that took most of them to the edge of comfortable territory. She hadn't said *I have something valuable*. She'd said *I understand the shape of the problem you're working on, and I know where I fit in it*.

Renner leaned back in her chair. The wood creaked. "All right," she said, and for the first time her voice carried something other than interrogation. Something that sounded, distantly, like the beginning of respect.

---

Renner talked. She talked the way she apparently always talked — in complete sentences, sourced, with the faint impatience of someone who'd had this argument before and won it every time and was tired of winning because winning hadn't moved anything forward.

The bloodline held that Founding-era objects responded to Chrysalis-touched physiology. That the change written into every person by the moons' influence was also a key, a compatibility layer — a *worthiness*, in the word the temple used. The objects left behind were meant for the changed, and only the changed.

"And the evidence for this," Renner said, "is that every person who has ever successfully operated a Founding-era mechanism has been Chrysalis-touched."

"Which is everyone," Asha said, from her chair. Quiet, but with the timing of someone who'd placed this particular stone in this particular wall before. "Everyone is Chrysalis-touched. So that evidence is —"

"Circular," Renner said. "Yes. Thank you, Asha, I was getting there."

"You were getting there slowly."

"I was getting there *thoroughly*." Renner turned back to Maren. "No unchanged person has ever been in a position to try. Not part of civic life. Not given access to archives. Not brought into contact with recovered objects. The test case doesn't exist."

"Hasn't existed," Asha corrected. Her eyes were on Maren.

The correction sat there. Renner did not look at Asha. She looked, for a fraction of a second longer than the argument required, at the dark surface of Corvel's container. Maren caught it. Renner was not just arguing the bloodline's epistemology; she was arguing herself. Thirty years of archival work inside the temple. Four years of nights and cooperage meetings after. If the test worked, Renner would not only be vindicated. She would be one of the people who had helped the story hold. The lamplight caught a muscle in her jaw and let it go.

"Four years ago," Renner continued, and her voice was the same voice, but something else moved underneath it now, "I found a margin notation in a pre-crash navigational text. The standard astronomical tables list four major orbital bodies. The notation — in what appears to be a contemporary hand, not a later addition — marks the number as wrong. Changes four to three."

"Which is heresy," Corvel said. It was the first time he'd spoken. His voice was lower than Maren expected, and more measured. A man who weighed words before spending them.

"Which is heresy in a text that predates the concept of heresy by approximately two centuries," Renner said. "The notation also includes a symbol. Small, easily missed." She looked at Maren with the directness of someone who had stopped apologizing for knowing more than everyone else in any given room. "Davan tells me you've seen the same symbol."

"On the disc," Maren said.

"On the disc."

The room held its breath. The collective suspension, the moment where a group that had been arguing in theory encountered the possibility of arguing in practice.

"Describe the symbol," Renner said.

"A curve, broken, with a line through the break. It's on the edge of the disc. It matches the inner orbital path on pre-crash charts."

Renner looked at Edvar. Something passed between them — a glance with years of shorthand in it. Edvar nodded, a small motion, almost invisible. Whatever the symbol was, Maren had described it correctly.

Somewhere in the cold floor beneath her boots the stone had started to pull the warmth out of her feet. The tallow smell thickened as the lamps burned down; one wick had begun to gutter.

---

"Can I ask you something directly?" Asha said.

"You've been asking indirectly for the last five minutes," Maren said. "Go ahead."

Craftsperson's directness met something it recognized. "Has the disc ever responded to you? Warmth, vibration, light, anything that suggests active function rather than inert material?"

The room went very still.

Maren considered the question. She considered what answering it honestly would mean — not for her, because she'd already walked down the stairs and sat in the chair and answered *yes* to *are you hollow*, which meant she'd already placed her bet. But for the argument. For the balance of power between Renner's insistence on evidence-before-testing and Asha's insistence that the absence of evidence was itself the evidence.

"It's warm," she said. "It pulses. The frequency has increased since I arrived in New Plymouth."

Nobody spoke.

"It started as once every few days. Now it's several times a day. The symbol on the edge — when it pulses, the symbol is warmer than the rest of the surface."

Corvel looked at his container. The gray surface sat there absorbing light, inert, silent, untouched by fourteen pairs of Chrysalis-touched hands over the eight years he'd had it. He looked at Maren.

Renner's expression had gone very flat. The flatness of someone whose framework was flexing further than she'd anticipated and who was deciding in real time whether to resist or accommodate. "That's —" she started.

"Reported experience," Asha said. "Not evidence. I know. But it's reported experience from an unchanged person handling Founding-era material, which is exactly the test case you said didn't exist."

"I said *hasn't* existed."

"Past tense. Yes. That's rather the point."

---

Outside, somewhere on the workshop floor above, a stave rack was being loaded; the distant knock of wood on wood came down through the floorboards. The lamps guttered again. Pip got up to trim the wicks, and for a moment the light contracted, pulling the room smaller, pushing the corners further into dark. Maren watched him work. His hands were steady. Dock worker's hands, broad across the palm, but he handled the wick-trimmer with the delicacy of someone who found things through sensitivity rather than force. The light steadied, expanded, reached the table again.

The disc was in her coat, in the inner pocket, where it had been since she'd fled north. She could feel it against her ribs — the warmth, the faint rhythm, the pulse that matched nothing she could identify. Not her heartbeat. Not her breathing. Something else. Something that had its own schedule and was keeping to it with increasing urgency.

Her father: he had carried this disc. He had gone east. He had not come back.

Vane at the gate, leaning against it in the dark with her arms crossed and her voice stripped to its operational register. *They need more information before heading east. We need to know what this group knows. But we're running out of room, Maren. Horst is in the city.*

Ulla's letter, her father's words filtered through Ulla's handwriting: the hill in the eastern highlands. Something there. Something the disc was for.

The recess on the disc's underside — the shaped depression that was clearly designed to receive something specific, something that fit, something that would complete a mechanism she didn't understand.

And Corvel's container on the table: the light-swallowing dark of it, the fourteen pairs of changed hands that had tried, the one pair of unchanged hands that hadn't.

The argument was still going. Renner and Asha, orbiting each other with the practiced gravity of people who had done this many times — Renner pulling inward toward rigor, Asha pushing outward toward action, neither of them wrong, both of them incomplete, and the incompleteness was the thing that kept them circling. They needed something to land on.

Edvar watched. He had been watching the entire time with the patience of a man who made maps and understood that the territory did not care what you drew on the paper. The young cartographer beside him had stopped pretending to take notes and was looking at Maren with open curiosity.

Davan sat near the door. He had vouched for her and then gone quiet, which was the right thing to do. He understood that the vouching was his job and the rest was hers. She noted it. Filed it under *competent*.

She looked down once at her own hands, resting on her knees. Unchanged, as they had always been. A little cold.

Maren reached into her coat.

---

*Vane, at the gate, had turned slightly toward the mouth of the lane. The light off the cooperage yard caught the angle of her cheek, and her right hand had moved without seeming to move, from her side to inside her coat. A figure at the far end of the street — man, heavy build, not moving the way a passing tradesman moved. He was watching the cooperage. Not the door. The approach.*

*Vane did not lift her head. She only shifted her weight so that the gatepost covered her outline from that angle. The figure turned, eventually, and walked on.*

---

She set the disc on the table.

No preamble. No announcement. Her hand went in, came out, set it down. A motion as plain as putting a cup beside a plate.

The disc sat on the wood between the two lamps. It was smaller than the container — palm-sized, dark, the same light-swallowing material. The symbol on its edge caught the lamplight and held it for a moment before the surface drank it down. Beside Corvel's container, the two objects looked like they belonged to the same sentence — different words, same language, same voice that had stopped speaking a very long time ago.

The room went silent.

Not the evaluative silence of her arrival. Not the recalibrating silence after she'd said *unchanged*. This was the silence of a room full of people who had been having an argument for years and had just watched the argument become unnecessary. The theoretical had become material. The gap in the evidence was sitting in a chair. The untested assumption was on the table.

Renner looked at the disc. She looked at the container. She looked at Maren.

Asha's hands came out of her lap and rested on the table, palms down, the first unguarded gesture Maren had seen from her. Corvel had gone very still, trying not to disturb something fragile. Edvar leaned forward — just slightly, just enough for his lamp-shadow to shift on the wall behind him.

Pip, standing by the lamps with the wick-trimmer still in his hand, did not move at all.

The disc pulsed. Once. Faintly. A warmth that Maren felt even with her hand no longer on it, the way you feel heat from a stone that has been sitting in the sun — residual, patient, indifferent to whether anyone was paying attention.

Every person in the room saw it. Not a glow — the material didn't glow. But something shifted in the surface, a deepening, as though the dark became briefly more itself. The container didn't respond. It sat beside the disc the same way it had sat for eight years: inert, sealed, silent under fourteen pairs of hands that were, perhaps, the wrong hands.

Renner went still — not because it had pulsed, but because the pulse was *specific*, and her framework did not account for specificity. Four years of argument had been built on a trait: that any unchanged hands would answer, that the key was the absence itself, distributed wherever the absence fell. The disc had not answered the room. It had answered the person. Her face did not fall — it rearranged, slowly, in the small public way of someone who would do her grieving later.

Renner opened her mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Well," she said. The word came out stripped of everything she usually armored her words with — precision, sourcing, the faint impatience. Just the word. Bare.

The disc on the table. The container beside it. The faces around the room, all of them turned toward her.

The disc was not a keepsake. It had never been a keepsake. Her father had not left it for her because it was precious to him, a sentimental object to remember him by. He had left it for her because she was the only person in his life whose hands had not been changed. Because the disc was a key, or part of a key, or the first word of a question that required unchanged hands to ask.

She had carried it for years thinking she was carrying grief.

She was carrying a test.

And these people — Renner with her four years of argument and her thirty years inside the institution that had managed the forgetting, Asha with her circular evidence, Corvel with his sealed container and his silence, Edvar with his maps and his face that Davan had seen and not spoken of, Pip with his intuition, the young cartographer with her ink-stained hands, Davan by the door who had brought her here — these people had been asking the question she was the answer to. Not the disc. Her. The hollow woman who had walked through three years of fraud and come out the other side holding an object that responded to unchanged hands in a world that had decided unchanged hands were empty.

The disc sat on the table. The container sat beside it. The lamplight failed to catch either surface. The room waited.

She looked at her own hands, resting on her knees. Unchanged. Untouched. The hands the moons had passed over, or missed — hands that a world ten thousand years old had not written its story across. She did not know yet whether that was mercy or accident or something with a name she had not been given. She let the not-knowing stand.

She did not say anything. There was nothing to say. The disc was on the table. She was in the room. The argument was over.

The test had not yet begun.
