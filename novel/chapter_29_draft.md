## Chapter Twenty-Nine — *Aldric*
### The Record

---

Davan's finger-pad was on the one kept thing his finger had chosen.

Aldric read the younger man's back the way he had read the backs of
novices at prayer — not the head, not the shoulder, but the set of
things below the shoulder blade where a body admitted the weight it
was carrying. Davan had gone past admitting. He was *in* the weight,
steady, his pad against the object and the object against the
material of the shelf, and the tension of the contact was a courtesy.

The courtesy had been offered.

He rose from the far shelf. The knees did what they did. He placed
his palms flat on the tops of his thighs, and the gesture arrived in
two languages at once — the hands-flat of a man approaching a book
whose cover had not been opened in his lifetime, and the hands-flat
of a priest lowering himself before the blessed sacrament. He let
them both be. He was tired of keeping them in different rooms.

He crossed the floor of the storage.

The dark material did not ring under his boot. His step made the
dense small sound of a floor made, at its making, to answer back
the way the floor of a library answered back — not more, not less.
He knew the sound of a floor that had been made for reading. This
was a reading floor.

He stopped a pace short of Davan and looked at the object under the
finger-pad.

It sat at the same height as its neighbors: flat on the upper side,
curved beneath. Small notation along one edge in a script Aldric did
not know. *Did not know* was the honest phrase — the letters, if
they were letters, were at the boundary where his eye reported
*written* without reporting *in what*. Not wholly foreign: he had
seen that quality once before, on the lintel-stone above the
undercroft doorway, in light he had been holding with the wrong hand.

"Davan."

"Here."

"May I."

"Yes."

The pad did not lift. The invitation had been there since before
Aldric had asked for it, and the asking had been a thing he needed
to do for himself — the last gesture of a man trained to ask before
he opened. The training was not wrong. It was, this once, not the
instrument the room required. But the gesture gave him the edge of
the page to put his hand on, and the edge was, for a man of his
vocation, the place reading began.

He put his palms at the sides of the object.

He lifted it.

The weight was the weight a maker chose when the maker had wanted a
thing carried without noting the carrying. Hand-weight. He brought
it a pace back so the wall-light fell across the upper face without
shadow of his own body, and he turned it so the edge-notation faced
him, and he waited for his eye to do what his eye did.

His eye did what his eye did. Not all at once. Of the seven
languages he had at different depths, none of them was this. But
the shapes along the edge were doing something familiar in an
unfamiliar alphabet. They were doing *numbering*.

A catalog number.

The word settled. Not a prayer. A catalog number — the mark a body
of kept material put on each of its items so the order could be
known without reading. He had spent thirty years in rooms where the
first act of respect for a shelf was to acknowledge its catalog
before picking up a single book.

He had begun to read.

---

He sat down on the floor.

Not with the dignity of a Keeper — with the practicality of a man
whose knees had been on the cold material of the far shelf longer
than the knees had asked to be. He sat cross-legged so that his eye
and the upper face of the object were at the same height. He put
his hands flat on his thighs.

The upper face was not blank. As his eye settled it resolved into
what he understood as *written* — dense, the way a page of close-set
type was dense, the letters in a careful hand running in lines. He
was not yet trying to read the lines. He was looking at their
arrangement. Paragraphs. A marker every fifteenth line that stood
out as a different sort of symbol, the way a scribe marked, in the
margin of a chronicle, the year.

This was a log. A record-in-sequence. The shape a document took
when its purpose was *to note, with date, what was true*, and not
to persuade any reader of any thing at all.

At the top of the upper face, set off from the body by the white
space a careful hand gave a heading, were two shapes. The inner-
orbit figure. And a mark he did not know.

He looked at the second mark for a long moment. The scholar did not
place it. But the body gave him a register the scholar had not
thought of: the mark was a *seal*. The shape above the opening of a
document when the document wished to announce the authority under
which it had been written.

The seal was not a church's seal. The Keepers' order had a seal; he
knew the weight of it. This was not that weight. This was the stamp
of an *office* — an enterprise. It had left this record under the
stamp of its enterprise, ten thousand seasons ago, and the stamp
had waited here to be seen.

He lowered his eye to the body of the text. He did not try to read
it. He noted that a four-letter unit appeared repeatedly, the first
letter of which was the inner-orbit figure itself, doing the work
of a letter the way a figure did the work of a letter when a
language had begun in a pictogram and moved on from the pictogram
without forgetting that the pictogram had once been the word.

*The word had once been the thing.*

He set the object back in its catalog position. He stood. The knee
protested; he let it.

He walked the shelf, establishing order. This long wall, written
registers. The opposite wall, at the same catalog distance — the
same register at different delivery. Voiced. Small indentations on
the upper faces, the sort of surface that lit when a hand settled
on it. The written on the left, the voiced on the right, at the
same dates, in the same hand.

An archive under two registers.

He did not cross to the voiced wall yet. He was not ready. He did
not want to hear a voice before he had read a document. The order
mattered to him — the written before the voiced — because the
written was the form his thirty years had prepared him for.

He chose a second object further along the first shelf.

---

He sat again.

The upper face was divided by ruling into a grid he recognized
across languages. A manifest grid: items left, quantities middle,
disposition right. His eye had looked at a thousand such grids in
estate inventories, temple-stock ledgers, pilgrim-supply accounts.
He read the columns by what the column did, not what it said.

First column: *items*. Second column: *number*. Third column:
*where assigned*.

The *where assigned* column had four distinct symbols, repeated
down the column in different combinations. Three were variants of
each other — small changes on a common shape. The fourth was a
shape apart.

He looked at the fourth symbol for a long time.

An oblong body, with a small notation at one extremity that resolved,
when his eye adjusted, into the figure of the descent line — the
drift of the fourth arc from the carving on the hillside outside,
rendered here at the scale of a column entry, doing the work of a
location-marker. The fourth symbol was a place.

The place was where he was sitting.

He counted the column. Most entries carried one of the three
variants. The remainder — a small percentage — had the fourth.
*Three of four, assigned to the three.* *One of four, assigned to
the lander.* Including, in the rows whose first-column entries he
could partly parse, rows that appeared to list people.

Crew.

The manifest was a crew manifest. It allocated crew to four
destinations. Three were orbital stations. One was the surface. The
count on the stations was greater than the count on the surface.

*Three for station, one for descent.*

He said the phrase in his head in English-of-the-country. The phrase
was what the manifest said, and the manifest was the source, and the
source was in his hand, and his hand was steady.

He had come to the mission profile.

He held it for a long breath. He had inferred this shape from the
diagrams, from the faces, from Brennan's *for unchanged hands*, from
Reth's *four hundred meters, no grain*, from the four arcs on the
hillside and what the light had done at the crest of the ridge three
mornings running. Inference had been the instrument his life had
built. But inference was always subject to the possibility that the
data were partial and the pattern, though coherent, was not the
pattern. This was not inference. The office had filed its own
manifest. The manifest said what the pattern had said.

A quieter sentence arrived under the first.

*The three that remained were not survivors. They were on post.*

He let it stand. He had been carrying, without exactly carrying, a
framing of the three moons as *the ones that made it* — the shape
the country had given itself over ten thousand years, because the
shape had the consolations a story made for the living wanted.
*Four fires in the sky. One came down to die.*

It was not the shape of the document.

The document did not say *the three survived*. The document said
*three for station*. The three had been tasked. They had gone to
their assigned places and had stayed there because staying was
their work. The work had not ended because the surface contingent
had not arrived to mark it ended. They were *performing protocol*.
They had been performing protocol for ten thousand seasons. The
protocol had held.

He closed his eyes a moment. Not prayer. The small motion of a man
accommodating a scale he had not built a room in his head for yet.
When he opened them, the manifest was still the manifest, and the
column was still the column.

His hand went inside his robe for the lamp-cord. It did not. The
hand started and stopped at the robe's shoulder and came back down
to his thigh, and he did not see the hand. His eye had gone back to
the manifest.

---

The third object was on the second shelf.

He crossed on his knees — the body had admitted its preference, and
the Keeper accepted — and chose the object at the second shelf's
start. A different format: not log, not manifest. Panels on the
upper face, each holding a diagram. Cross-sectional. The interior of
a vessel, seen from the side, compartments indicated.

He knew what he was looking at before the scholar named it. A ship.
The same shape the face in the undercroft had rendered as the
fourth of the four — the lander — but here intact, at original
design, before the damage. The compartments carried labels he could
not read. But some carried the stamp of the seal. Some carried the
four-arc figure. And at the ship's stern —

His breath caught.

A compartment label there carried a figure he had seen in his own
sealed chamber, on the inward-facing curve of the four smooth
containers. He had recorded it in his journal two months ago. He
had thought it a house-mark, a maker's sigil, a decoration.

Now it was a compartment label.

The containers in his sealed chamber had come from *that
compartment*. The mark on the containers was the mark of their
origin hold. Four storage holds at the ship's stern, each labeled
with its sub-figure — three of a family, one apart, the same grid
of differentiation as the manifest's destinations — and the fourth
hold had held the containers the Confluence temple had been built
over.

He sat with this. He had not assembled the last step before. He had
approached it — the temple-built-atop, the debris-field-under-the-
floor — in his own chamber, in a dry-stone fold, riding east. But
he had not put the containers on the manifest. He had not understood
that the objects he had knelt before for two months were
*inventoried*. That an office had filed them. That his life had been
spent tending a building that was standing on top of an inventoried
cargo hold.

He was not angry. He tested the anger; it was not there. Anger
required that someone had done something to him, and the office
whose stamp sat at the top of the first object had not done anything
to him specifically. The office had filed its documents. The office
had assumed its colony would arrive. The colony had not arrived. The
documents had stayed where they were filed. Two centuries of
Keepers at a time had done what their hands had been given them
for, without knowing that the hands were tending a floor built above
an archive.

His life had been what his life had been.

The register was — he tried the word and the word fit — *quiet*. He
was very quiet. He sat in the quiet, and the quiet was not peace;
it was the quiet of a long thing landing.

---

He stood.

He crossed to the voiced wall.

He did not yet lift one. He looked at the spacing of them. The
archive kept its forms in the forms that befit them. The written
were the written and the voiced were the voiced, at the same dates,
in the same hand. The voiced were what he had been afraid of. He
was still the Keeper whose life had been mostly text. A voice from
the office would make the office a *people*, and people, even dead
for ten thousand years, entered a room as people entered a room.

He took one register from the shelf.

Carefully. Hand-weight. The upper face carried a single panel of
indentation, and his thumb, held clear of it, felt the cool-precise
difference between panel and surrounding surface — the area that
invited contact. He was carrying the object to the floor where he
could set it down and begin, and the carrying itself was the
thinking he was doing about whether to make the contact.

He sat in the middle of the floor.

He set it down. He thought of Davan, still at the far wall, still
with his pad at the one object he had chosen. He thought of Maren
at the recess outside — the hum through the material of the hill,
the whole hillside held open because of the pressure of one finger
against a small mark. He thought of Brennan in Greenford, whose
eight-page letter had said *for unchanged hands* and had not, in
the letter, explained the phrase because the phrase had not needed
explaining between them. Brennan would have to be told. Aldric did
not know yet by what channel. The channels he had been trained to
use would suppress what he sent through them. The channel would
have to be elsewhere.

He set his palm on the panel.

---

A voice began.

He did not understand the words.

The voice was a man's. Middle-register. Tired. Not performing. Not
preaching. Not reporting to a superior who expected brevity. The
voice spoke at the cadence of a man who had sat down at the end of
a day he had not expected to have, saying what had to be said
because no one else would say it. The vowels were long. Some of the
consonants were sounds Aldric's language did not use. But the tone
was a tone Aldric had heard thirty years' worth of — in the cells
of dying pilgrims, at the bedsides of men setting down their work.
It was the tone of ending.

He closed his eyes.

He let the voice finish.

He understood nothing of the words. But in the middle, the same
four-letter unit he had read on the first object — the figure-word,
the inner-orbit figure doing the work of a letter — came up in the
man's speech as a name. The man said it with the care a man used
when saying a name. It was the name of the mission, or the ship, or
the office; he could not tell which. Saying it was something the
man had decided he was going to do before the ending.

After the name, the voice became quieter. A pause. Then a single
word with the shape of a sign-off.

The voice was finished.

Aldric lifted his palm.

He had never believed, even in his worst scholarly years, that the
people who had made the figure on the door of the Confluence had
been anything other than people. But he had held them as figures
from an *office* — a faceless enterprise, the sort of thing a
theology used to put scale under its story. The voice corrected
the scale. The office had been staffed by a man, among other men.
The man had been tired at a certain hour. The man had said what he
had said. The man had probably not known what would follow the
ending he was at. The man had done the work of speaking the name.

He put the voiced register back.

---

He crossed again to the written shelf and picked up the ship
diagram and carried it back to the manifest, and he reached for a
third written object further down the shelf — its upper face
glimpsed from the catalog, the format of a *study*, a report on a
subject. He carried that one back too and laid it beside the other
two. He was triangulating. He was doing what a Keeper did when the
documents had begun to talk to each other.

The third object.

The subject-heading used a word he had learned by its shape in the
first log's body: *native*. The body was denser here — paragraphs,
tables, diagrams at the page-foot. He took a long moment to
understand the diagrams as cellular, because he had never actually
seen a diagram of a cell, only descriptions of them in the natural-
philosophical treatises of the Greenford library. But these were
cells. The cells held the same kind of structure in both versions
of the side-by-side panel, and the difference between the two was
the difference he had been trying not to see.

The left version had a small figure inside the cell that the right
version did not.

The figure was the inner-orbit figure, rendered in the small.

He looked at it for a long time.

The office had used the same figure for many things. It had been,
for ten thousand years on Gaius, the emblem of the mission. It had
become, by a distance, the emblem of theology. It had become, by a
further distance, a carving on the doors of a temple, a mark on the
edge of a disc, the shape of an obsessive young man's knife in
pine. Here, at its earliest, it was the office's stamp for *mission-
related* — the notation an office put in a corner of a diagram to
say *this is ours*. The left version of the cell had been marked
*ours*. The right had not.

The left was the version the Chrysalis had made.

The right was the version before.

The cells were not different species. They were the same species at
two states, and the states were before-and-after contact with a
subject, and the subject was named in the heading by the word
*native*, and the native was the organism the report was on.

He laid the report down.

He did not move for a breath. The floor of the storage held him the
way a floor held a man, and he let it.

The Chrysalis was not a gift.

The Chrysalis was not a sacrament.

The Chrysalis had been waiting on Gaius when the colony ship came
down through the sky it had no business being in, and the Chrysalis
had entered the surviving colonists through soil and air and water.
The Chrysalis had not *chosen* them. The Chrysalis had done what
the Chrysalis did. Those who had not died of it had changed. The
ones who had not changed had been the *unchanged*. The office had a
word for them. The word was on the right version of the cell:
*native-resistant baseline*. He did not know the alphabet but he
knew the grammar and he knew the shape of a technical phrase.

*Native-resistant baseline.*

He said the phrase in English.

He said it in the Keepers' Latin.

He said it in the old ritual tongue of the Confluence, in which the
Chrysalis Rite's oldest prayers were chanted — and the phrase in
that tongue did not fit. The ritual tongue did not have a word for
*baseline*. The ritual tongue had a word for *chosen*.

*Chosen* had always been how the bloodline described itself. He had
never trusted the word; the word had always rung to him like a word
put into a theology by its beneficiaries. But he had trusted the
structure the word stood in — that the bloodline had a *capacity*
the other lines did not have, and the capacity was the foundation
of ten thousand years of doctrine about the administration of
Founding-era objects.

The capacity was in the right-hand cell.

The capacity was: the cell did not admit the organism. The cell was
unchanged. An unchanged body was the body the ship's systems had
been designed to recognize, because the ship's systems had been
designed before the colony had encountered the organism. An
unchanged body was the baseline the office had assumed. An
unchanged body was the body for whom the disc functioned as a crew
ID, because the disc had been made for the body that remained in
the condition the office had cataloged it in.

The bloodline were unchanged.

They had not been chosen. They had been immune.

Immune was a smaller word than chosen. Immune was a smaller word
than *gifted*. Immune described a property. The property was a
genetic trait the descendants had carried forward, and the trait
had been the key to the pre-crash tools, and the access to the
tools had built the whole theology their descendants' descendants
believed.

He thought about the Autumnal Rite. He had sat at the Confluence's
high altar for thirty Autumnal Rites and watched the participants
approach for the mark. He had named each mark by its coming. He had
read the readings and sung the canticles and invoked the gift-
givers. The gift-givers had never given anything. The gift-givers
had been a category a minority had built to describe their own
immunity as a blessing, so that they could live with having access
to the tools of the ship and with the rest of the population not
having that access.

The temple had been built over a debris-field. The altar had been
laid over the bloodline's immunity. The Rite he had administered
for thirty years had celebrated, as a gift, a biological event that
had killed most of the colonists and had changed the rest.

He did not feel the anger now either.

What arrived in its place he could only name as *tenderness*. Not
for the office.
Not for the bloodline. For the long slow shape of a story a people
had made out of their own biology because making the story had been
how those people had lived with their biology. The Chrysalis had
come. The Chrysalis had done what it did. The unchanged had held
the key and the key had worked, and the tools had worked for them
and not for their neighbors, and the unchanged had done what human
beings had always done when they had access and other people did
not — they had built a story in which their access was legitimate,
and the story had got more beautiful over time, and eventually the
liturgy had been what the country understood about itself. The
liturgy had been beautiful. He had loved it. He had *loved* it. He
did not now un-love it. He looked at it across the distance the
right-hand cell had put him on the other side of, and he saw it the
way he saw, at a remove, a thing he had been given as a child and
had carried a long way and was now setting down because the object
under his hand required both hands.

He set the study back.

---

Maren spoke from the passage.

"Aldric. Davan."

Low. Placing them.

"Here," Aldric said.

"Here," Davan said.

"A voice," she said. The word was careful. She had heard something,
or she had felt it through the material. "I felt it under my hand.
The pulse went shorter for a count."

"A voice," Aldric said. "The register carried. I did not think the
carrying would reach you."

"It did not reach. The hill answered something. I read the answer."

"Yes."

A pause. Then: "Is it bad."

He considered.

"No," he said. "It is true."

"All right," she said. The word arrived in her two-word register.
Something had settled at depth. He heard it settle.

"I will be longer," he said.

"Yes."

---

He reached for a fourth object.

The next in the row. Its catalog figure next in sequence. He
suspected a study — the format, from a distance, was the study-
format. He brought it to his lap and set it with the other three he
had laid out on the floor. Four objects in a line now, from four
shelf-positions. He had assembled in an hour the shape he had not
been able to see from the outside of the hull.

Its heading used two words he had learned.

*For the colony.*

And a third word he had not yet seen. A heading-word: centered,
larger, its letters spaced the way a title was spaced. The heading
said, if he had read it right — and he thought he had read it
enough right — *archive, colonial-genetic, Mission Office,
Hold-IV*.

He had stopped breathing for a moment. The body caught itself and
began again, because the body did what the body did, and he let it.

The object he held was the study-document whose subject was the
contents of the four smooth containers in his undercroft.

Hold IV. The hold at the stern of the lander whose four containers
had been found intact, at the Confluence, in the secondary debris
scatter he had not known until this month was a debris scatter. The
hold had carried an *archive*.

He read what he could read of the body. A description — short,
because a document describing a genetic archive did not need to be
long; the archive was the archive. He could not read most of the
caption-words, but one recurring figure was the figure for
*preserved*. Another was *viable*. A third was *human*. A fourth he
guessed from position as a unit-word for the kind of thing a biology
archive was made of — *sample*, or *material*.

Preserved. Viable. Human.

The four containers held preserved, viable, human material.

Material from the unchanged baseline.

Material the mission had brought with it to seed the colony's long-
term genetic health from, before the mission had encountered the
Chrysalis, before the encounter had made baseline-cells into
something else across most of the colonists. The colony had carried
with it the old version of itself, cryopreserved in holds, bound
for a thousand-year health plan whose plan-makers had not
anticipated a native organism that would make the plan a different
kind of plan by breakfast.

The plan had survived. In the holds. In the containers. Under the
floor of his temple.

A further entry near the bottom caught his eye. Smaller print. A
footnote — his eye knew the shape of a footnote across languages —
bracketed, formatted as a contingency clause. He did not try to
read the text word-by-word. He saw the figure-words he had been
collecting: *native*, *resistance*, *baseline*. And a further figure
whose position in the clause was the position of a *requires*.

He read the footnote as best he could.

The footnote said — in the shape he was certain of, even if the
grammar was not — that opening the archive containers required a
condition. The condition was the absence of the native organism
from the opener's biology. The requirement was absolute. The
archive had been sealed with a biological lock that recognized
baseline. A body carrying the organism could not open the hold. The
lock had been designed, in office-of-the-mission before-the-crash,
to guard against contamination of the preserved material by foreign
biology. Against contamination in the opener's direction — the lock
made no distinction.

The holds could not be opened by any living person on Gaius, because
every living person on Gaius carried the Chrysalis integrated into
their cell-structure, even those — the bloodline, Maren — whose
immune systems had kept the active organism out. The organism had
been present across the population for ten thousand years; the
bloodline's descendants were not the same pre-organism baseline
their ancestors had been, even if those descendants were immune.
The lock was set to the pre-organism baseline. The population the
lock had been designed for did not exist on this planet any more.

Opening the holds required making a person who was the pre-organism
baseline.

Undoing the Chrysalis in them first.

He said the word in his mind. He said *undoing*. He said it in
three languages. It sat in each of them like a stone — a stone a
man had lifted out of a stream-bed and had set on the bank. The
stream moved on. The stone was on the bank. The stone was now a
choice.

He did not yet have the choice in his hand.

He was at the threshold of the choice. He was not yet across it.

Across it was the next Keeper's ground. The next Keeper was going
to be the Keeper who took the floor up, which he had said to
himself three nights ago and which was, now, not a sentence he had
said but a description of the work in front of him. The floor was
already partially up. He was sitting on the floor of the storage
that was under the floor of the chapel. He had not *taken it up* in
the sense of a decision-taken; he had *walked through* it in the
sense of a route-walked. The taking-up would be the coming-back-
out, and the going-back-to-the-Confluence, and the saying-of-what-
had-been-found, and the channel-built-by-hand for saying it in.

That work was tomorrow's work. This hour's work was to receive what
was in front of him.

He received.

He set the archive-document back at its catalog position — the
others too, in order, respecting the row — though he did not yet
stand. He remained seated. He put his hands flat on his thighs. He
let his breath even.

He thought about the next object. He thought he might cross to the
voiced-register wall and let a second voice into the room. He was
composing his next motion in his head the way he composed the
order of a vigil — what came first, what came second.

---

Davan spoke.

"Aldric."

Low. The carrying register Davan used when he had something he did
not want to carry across the floor at volume. Aldric turned his
head. Davan was still at his shelf. He had not moved his pad. But
his face was at the passage.

"Vane."

Aldric had heard nothing at the passage. He listened and heard
nothing still, and then the scrape of a boot on the material at the
threshold that was not the scrape of a boot he knew yet, and then
Vane's voice, dry, at the mouth of the passage.

"Aldric. Davan. Come to the angle."

The voice a hunter used when a hunter was not yet telling you to
move fast. It was telling him to stop what he had been doing.

He stood. The knee protested; he let it protest.

"How near," he said.

"Near enough. His boot is on the slope, above the cart. He is going
quiet. He has not seen Edvar yet — Edvar is flat in the southern
lee and knows. One man. The only scent on the slope. The other
four are not here today."

"All right."

He heard his own voice in the storage's register and heard it carry
the way the material carried — close, warm, the voice held a hand's-
breadth by the wall before the wall gave it back. His voice
sounded older to him than he had heard it sound in some while. It
was the voice of a man who had just read what he had read and had
his hands flat on his thighs a minute ago and was going to walk
out because a follower of Horst's was at the breach.

He looked once at the shelves. He had put the four objects back in
their catalog places. The voiced register was back on its opposite
shelf. The row he had made on the floor was gone. A library paid
the courtesy of leaving a shelf as it found it. The courtesy he
owed was that courtesy, because he did not yet know whether he

He turned toward the passage.

Davan had come off the shelf. His pad had lifted at a rate slower
than a lift — he had asked his pad to leave the surface and the
pad had done it, and the departure had been a courtesy. Davan's
color was low. His wrists lit faintly at the new register; the
peak was past.

"Ready," Davan said.

"Yes."

They crossed to the mouth of the passage together.

Aldric set his hand on the wall of the passage as he went out. He
had not touched it on the way in; he had not had permission yet. On
the way out, the wall gave a cool to his palm without his asking —
a cool that did not travel. The palm carried it the length of two
steps and then the palm was his palm again. The giving had been the
final beat of the reading. The archive, in the register it had, had
acknowledged that he had read.

The passage was narrow at top, widening at chest-height, narrow
again at the bottom, as it had been coming in, and the air moved
past him toward the outside. Ahead, a pale square of morning at the
end of the passage. Maren's silhouette at the recess, her thumb on
the disc, her back not turned, her hand not moving. She had not
moved in the time he had been inside. The hill was being held open
under her pad. The held-open-ness had been the condition of his
reading. It was still the condition.

Beyond Maren's knee, on the slope above the cart, a second
silhouette moved along the north shoulder — small at that distance,
the shape of a man who did not know he was visible yet, coming down
through the low scrub with the quiet of a professional. Edvar,
beyond Maren, was not visible from the passage angle; but a thin
line of horse-breath steamed up from the lee of the stone where the
cart was, and the breath was even, the breath of a horse being held
at its halter by a hand that knew the horse.

Aldric stopped at the angle Vane had named.

He did not step further. He did not step back.

Behind him the storage held. Ahead of him was the hillside with the
disc in its recess and Maren's thumb on the disc, and the follower
on the slope, and the morning, and the work of the day which had
not yet been done.

The reading was interrupted.

A boot scraped on scree above the cart. Quietly, not quietly enough.
He heard it at the angle the way he would have heard a footfall in
a chapel aisle during a vigil — the small sound that said the vigil
was no longer alone. Vane, invisible at the shoulder below, made no
sound at all. Davan, at his shoulder inside the passage, was
holding the new color low at his wrists.

The boot came down on the scree again. One pace nearer.

He breathed in. He breathed out.

The reading had not finished. The world had come in anyway.
