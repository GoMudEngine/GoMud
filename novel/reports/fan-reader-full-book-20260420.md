# Fan Reader Response — Full Manuscript
*What the Moons Keep* — Complete Read-Through
Report Date: 2026-04-20
Reader persona: first-time, knows only "literary science-fantasy, slow-burn
reveal, four POVs"

---

## METHOD

Read cover to cover, in prescribed order: main manuscript (Chs. 1–16),
then chapters 17–30 in draft order. Tracking was done while reading — not
reconstructed after. This report reflects the sequence of actual response,
not a tidy retrospective.

---

## SECTION ONE: FIRST IMPRESSIONS AND EARLY ENGAGEMENT

**Prologue:** An immediate signal that this is a different kind of book.
Eight fragments of the same story, including a children's skipping rhyme
and a condemned text and a waypoint shrine inscription. The shrine line
stopped me cold: *The moons are not moons. Ask your blood what it
remembers. Then go east. There is a hill shaped wrong.* I did not know
what any of this meant. I wanted to.

**Chapter 1 [Maren]:** The coal-pouch trick is a small thing rendered
with precision — I understood the stakes before the stakes were explained.
The word "hollow" arrives with the weight of a slur someone has just
discovered applies to them. Then: *the bloodline has asked to be told.*
Someone is being looked for. Maren is leaving. I'm with her.

**Chapter 2 [Davan]:** Bioluminescent empathy mutation, traveling north,
carving a figure with one arm held close without knowing why. I asked: why
does he carve it? The question lingered. I did not expect the answer to
take twenty-eight chapters.

**Chapter 3 [Aldric]:** The sealed room. The "face." A dying woman
whispering *the moons are not moons, they are boats with the dead still
inside.* I felt the book's architecture click into place here. The prologue
had been a promise; this was the first payment.

**Chapter 4 [Vane]:** The olfactory mutation. The Bloom. The contract.
Vane is introduced as the most morally compromised of the four, and it is
immediately interesting. Her nose for status, her clinical detachment — and
underneath it, the first hints that she knows what she is doing and dislikes
herself for it.

**Early engagement overall:** High. I read the first four chapters without
putting them down (metaphorically). The mystery was good — not a puzzle to
solve but a gravitational pull. I wanted to know what was east. I wanted to
know why Maren's blood remembered something she didn't.

## SECTION TWO: MID-BOOK TRACKING

### Questions raised in early chapters:

- What are the moons, actually? (Raised: Prologue, Ch. 3)
- What is "hollow"? Why does the bloodline care? (Ch. 1)
- What does the disc do? What is it? (Ch. 1)
- Why does Davan carve the one-arm figure? (Ch. 2)
- What is Bloom, really? (Ch. 4 — I had suspicions)
- Who is looking for Maren, and why? (Ch. 1)
- What is east? (Shrine inscription)

### Questions answered (and when):

- Bloom's source: Ch. 14 (full revelation) — I had suspected it from Ch. 4
  but the confirmation hit harder than the suspicion. Good pacing.
- Disc's nature: gradually, Ch. 6 (first pulse) → Ch. 16 (cooperage, the
  decisive pulse) → Ch. 27 (disc in recess)
- What's east: Ch. 17 (Reth describes the hill) → Ch. 27 (they see it) →
  Ch. 28 (inside)
- The moons: Chs. 19, 24, 25 (the two faces, different positions, same
  event) → Ch. 30 (the captain's voice; the moons are on station, not
  drifting, not dead)
- Why Davan carves the one-arm figure: never stated explicitly, but by Ch.
  24 I understood. His mutation was reaching for a shape his conscious mind
  had not yet named. The carving *was* the name.
- Why the bloodline matters: Ch. 29 (the archive document, left-cell vs.
  right-cell, *native-resistant baseline*). This was the reveal I did not
  see coming.

### Drift moments (where I lost purchase):

**Chapters 11–14 (New Plymouth, rooms):** A slight drag. The cooperage
group is being assembled and the political texture is important, but I
wanted the plot to move. A few scenes felt like they were holding the
novel in place while characters arrived. Specifically: the third or fourth
room-scene where Vane and Maren discuss the Bloom supply chain again felt
like ground already covered. Not bad, not fatal, but I registered it.

**Nowhere else.** The pacing after Ch. 15 is clean.

### Character engagement over time:

**Vane** became the most surprising to care about. She started as the
morally compromised professional. By Ch. 18 (seeing the hollow woman on
the pallet, walking away, the two words: *I'm stopping*), she became the
most emotionally complex person in the book. The withdrawal chapters (26
and 30) are the bravest writing in the manuscript — rendered without
sentimentality or any bid for sympathy. *Nose, working. Mine.* That
sentence.

**Maren** is the spine of the book. She is not always the most vivid person
on the page, but she is always the right one. The hill chapter (27) is the
strongest single chapter in the manuscript. Finding the coat. Sitting
beside it without weeping because the weeping country was a country she
could not afford today. *She had been carrying grief. The cooperage had
taught her she was carrying a test. The hill taught her what she had not
had the words for, which was that she had been carrying both.* That is a
structural thesis of the book rendered in a character's body, and it lands.

**Aldric** has the most complex interiority of the four, and the lamp-wick
habit is the single best sustained character detail in the manuscript. A
man who has spent thirty years trimming other people's lights. The moment
in Ch. 25 when he blows out the small lamp — *with his mouth, not his
fingers* — without deciding to. The hand staying on his knee, open, palm
up. That is the whole arc in one gesture.

**Davan** is the warmest. The carving hand. The skin that reads what he
has not yet named. His interiority in Ch. 28 — the room of held things,
the courtesy of not picking up, the new color — is extraordinary. The
"attending without attender" formulation. He understands the room because
he is made of the same register.

## SECTION THREE: SURPRISES

**Landed hard:**

1. The bloodline reveal (Ch. 29). I expected the bloodline to be villains,
   or to be chosen, or to be a lineage with a secret power. The actual
   answer — *native-resistant baseline*, immune, not chosen — is smaller and
   more devastating than any of those. Aldric sitting with the word *tenderness*
   for the people who had built a beautiful story out of their own biology
   because that was how they had survived it. I did not expect that word, and
   it was right.

2. Finding the father's coat (Ch. 27). I had expected the father to be
   alive, or the father to have sent a message, or the father to have left
   the disc with someone. He died four paces short. His hands pointing at
   the recess. The key had been in Ashwick, in the infant who was not yet
   named Maren.

3. Vane's withdrawal being rendered with the precision of a logbook, and
   the logbook being the entire form of her interiority. *Day three symptom
   per Cade's old chart. Attention fragments. Compensate: extend the circuit.*

4. The captain's voice (Ch. 30). The whole book has been building to an
   encounter with Founding-era data. I expected something cold, something
   archival, something at the register of a geological discovery. Instead: a
   tired man at the end of a day he had not expected to have. *I am told
   there is a hillside to the east that moves in the light. It is our own
   hull. Imagine. I am going to go look at it.* A man getting up from a desk.

**Did not surprise me:**

The Bloom revelation (Ch. 14), because the earlier chapters had been laying
the groundwork with enough care that I was already afraid of the answer
before it arrived. Which is the right way to do it — not a surprise but a
confirmation of the worst suspicion.

## SECTION FOUR: PROSE ASSESSMENT

**Distinctive: yes.**

The book has a syntax that is doing a specific thing. Short declarative
sentences, then longer clauses, then a rest. The withholding is structural.
The verb "held" is used throughout with precision — for rooms, for
characters, for the material of the ship, for the hill itself. By the end,
*held* has accumulated enough meaning that when the last section uses it
for the moons (*holding their stations, holding what they keep*), the word
carries the whole book.

The physical details earn their weight. The lamp-wick habit is not a
detail; it is a life. Davan touching wheel-hubs in the morning because the
cold of the iron tells him something. Vane's cuff-motion.

Standout prose:

- *She had been carrying grief. The cooperage had taught her she was
  carrying a test. The hill taught her what she had not had the words for,
  which was that she had been carrying both, and both had been true, and the
  reframe had not been an exchange. It had been an addition.*

- The entire *attending without attender* passage (Ch. 28).

- *He blew. The flame went. The cold came in at the space where the flame
  had been. The hand he had not used to put it out stayed on his knee, open,
  palm up, the pads of the thumb and forefinger resting on air.* (Ch. 25)

- Maren's final section in Ch. 30: *the moons did not look back, because
  the moons were not in the business of looking. They were at their stations.
  They were keeping.*

**One note on range:** The emotional register holds at a sustained low heat
for most of the book. This is appropriate and controlled. But the Maren
section of Ch. 30 risks feeling like it earns more than it has paid for, in
the sense that the most explicit emotional statement of the book arrives at
the end of a journey that has been deliberately understated. The captain's
voice is the hinge. If that lands (it did, for me), the Maren payoff lands
with it. If it doesn't land for a reader who needed more earlier, the ending
may feel earned-by-structure-alone. Worth knowing.

## SECTION FIVE: STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT

**Four POVs, rotating.** The rotation is clean. Aldric's chapters carry the
intellectual architecture of the book. Davan's chapters carry the emotional
register. Maren's chapters carry the plot spine. Vane's chapters carry the
moral complexity and the physical world. No POV is redundant.

**The prologue's eight fragments work.** By the end, I had returned to each
of them in my head and they had all been resolved or opened further. The
skipping rhyme (*Four fires in the sky, one came down to die, three stayed
up to cry*) is not accurate by the end — the three didn't cry; they held
their stations — and that discrepancy is exactly right. Children's songs
are not accurate. They are emotionally true. The book honors the difference.

**The ending is not a resolution. It is a beginning.**

The four characters are going to disagree. The archive is not going to be
opened today, or next month, or in this book. The decision about *what to
do* with what they found is the next generation's problem, possibly the
next century's. This is structurally correct for the book's thematic
concerns (scale of history, weight of inheritance, patience of the held
thing), but it will frustrate readers who wanted a plot conclusion. I did
not find it frustrating. I found it honest. The book has been telling us
for thirty chapters that the moons have been holding their stations for ten
thousand years without needing anyone to tell them what to do next.

**The antagonist (Crane's faction, Horst's contract)** is handled at the
right distance. They are present as pressure, not as plot. The follower on
the slope is resolved by Vane with cord, not a chase. This is a book about
what is inside the hill, not about a race to get there.

## SECTION SIX: EMOTIONAL RESPONSE LOG (CHRONOLOGICAL)

*These are the moments I registered something visceral, in order:*

1. Shrine inscription in Prologue: *Ask your blood what it remembers.*
   (Curious. Pulled forward.)
2. Ch. 3: Sella's dying words. (The first full-body chill.)
3. Ch. 11: The disc's first clear pulse for Maren alone. (The book's first
   unambiguous "this is about her specifically.")
4. Ch. 14: Bloom revelation. (Dread confirmed. The book asked me to feel
   sick and I did.)
5. Ch. 16: The disc on the cooperage table, pulsing for Maren and no one
   else. (Recognition. Relief. The mystery locating its center.)
6. Ch. 21: Aldric's hand stopping one span from the disc. *The span between
   his hand and the disc was the exact width of the sentence he had come a
   thousand miles to begin.* (This sentence is the book's emotional peak for
   Aldric's arc, and it happens three-quarters through.)
7. Ch. 25: Aldric blowing out the small lamp. (Unexpectedly moved.)
8. Ch. 26: *Nose, working. Mine.* (Vane's entire arc compressed to two words.)
9. Ch. 27: The coat. The hand-line pointing at the recess. (The cost of the
   book made visible. Did not cry. Felt like crying would have been rude to
   the prose.)
10. Ch. 29: *Native-resistant baseline.* Aldric's *tenderness*. (The reveal
    I didn't see coming, and the emotional response I didn't expect.)
11. Ch. 30: *I am going to go look at it.* (The captain as a tired man. The
    whole scale of the book in a single breath.)
12. Ch. 30: Maren looking up at the three moons. *She let them keep.*
    (Arrived. Stayed.)

## SECTION SEVEN: RECOMMENDATION PROFILE

**Who this is for:**
- Readers who finished *The Left Hand of Darkness* and were glad it didn't
  explain the biology.
- Readers who read *Housekeeping* for the prose and didn't want more plot.
- SF readers who are tired of chosen-one structures and want a book where
  the main character's significance is historical, not heroic.
- Literary readers who will accept science-fantasy framing if the interior
  lives are real.
- Anyone who has read *The Road* and thought: I want this precision, but I
  want it pointed at something that isn't despair.

**Who this is not for:**
- Readers who need external conflict as the primary engine.
- Readers who want a complete plot resolution.
- Readers who need to understand the science quickly. The book withholds
  its mechanics on purpose. Readers who chase the mechanics will be ahead
  of the book and restless.

## SECTION EIGHT: THE SIX-MONTH TEST

In six months, what I will remember:

The lamp-wick. A priest who has spent thirty years trimming other people's
lights, reaching for a wick that isn't there, and finally, at the end,
blowing one out with his mouth.

And Maren's father, lying four paces short of the lock, his hands pointing
at the recess. The key had been in Ashwick all along. Both of these things
true at once.

---

## SECTION NINE: THE HONEST NOTE

The book is exceptional and I mean that without the usual softening. The
four POVs are the most controlled four-way narration I have read in genre-
adjacent fiction in several years. The prose is doing something original
with the language of withholding. The structure honors its own scale — it
does not pretend that ten thousand years of history resolves in a five-day
walk east.

The honest note is this: the middle of the book (approximately Chs. 11–14)
will lose a percentage of readers who needed a different kind of momentum
to stay with the understated approach. The assembly of the cooperage group
is necessary and the politics of it are real, but the section moves at a
pace that the early chapters have not prepared us for. The early chapters
are driven by Maren's road north, which has physical urgency. The cooperage
section is driven by information and character positioning, which is a
different kind of page-turning.

The fix is not to add action. The fix is to find, in those chapters, one
moment as visceral as the lamp-wick or the coat or *I'm stopping* — a
moment that pays the emotional register forward before the reader has had
to wait too long for one. The cooperage table scene when the disc pulses
(Ch. 16) works this way, and it may be enough. But read those chapters
with the question: *is there a reader who gets here and puts the book
down?* And if so, what does that reader need in Ch. 12 that isn't there?

Everything else: keep.
