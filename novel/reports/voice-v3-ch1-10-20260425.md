# Prose & Voice Audit V3 (Vee) — Prologue + Ch 1-10

---

## 1. POV Voice Drift

### Maren

Maren's voice signature is compressed, watchful, dry-edged, analytically
self-aware. It holds across most of this section. The Chapter 9 shelter
monologue — reportedly compressed in a prior pass — is now the strongest
sustained Maren passage in the whole block. The one wobble is in Chapter 5:

**Minor — Maren's Ch. 5 reflection at the boundary stone shifts into a
more lyrical, abstract register than she usually permits herself.**

> "Whoever decided she was hollow was wrong, or lying, or both. And their
> being wrong or lying about that meant she should be considerably more
> skeptical about whatever else they'd decided about her."

The structure of that second sentence — "their being wrong or lying about
that meant she should be considerably more skeptical" — is not Maren. It
is the sentence of someone pausing to be clever. Maren's analytical mode
produces short declarative conclusions, not qualified subordinate chains
with hedged verbs. The thought is right; the sentence shape isn't. Compare
the surrounding prose: "Two words. *Hollow* said empty. *Unblooded* said
it had never been there." Those are Maren. The skepticism sentence is the
author smoothing the idea for the reader.

The paragraph beginning "She was not hollow. The hollow was not in her. /
The hollow was in someone's plan for her." is excellent Maren — abrupt,
internal, not decorated. It underscores how faintly off the skepticism
sentence is by contrast.

**Minor — Maren's closing lines of Ch. 10 drift slightly toward poetic
register.**

> "She could see them at the edge of her vision, and that was enough. /
> She had road to cover."

"She had road to cover" is perfect Maren. But "She could see them at the
edge of her vision, and that was enough" is not quite right — the "and
that was enough" construction is a narrator's consoling summary of a
feeling, not Maren's own voice assessing her situation. She would think
something more like: *she wasn't looking at them. She didn't need to.* The
current line tells us what Maren makes of the moons being visible. Maren
would more likely just register it and move.

### Davan

Davan's voice — open-grained, processing by circling, noticing specifics
without forcing conclusions — holds well throughout Chapters 2 and 6.

**Minor — Davan's river-barge philosophy dialogue (Ch. 6) is slightly too
tidy on both sides.**

> "A simplification that lets you move through the territory without
> getting lost. The getting-lost or not is the point, not the map."

Lera is a well-realized character and the scene is good. The issue is that
the philosophical exchange is too resolved — both Davan's objections and
Lera's responses land cleanly, with each beat of argument fully formed.
Davan's voice signature is half-thoughts he doesn't force to conclusion. In
dialogue, that should mean incomplete sentences, reformulations, a response
to an earlier version of Lera's point rather than the current one. The
scene as written gives us two people whose arguments arrive pre-edited.
Davan's interruption — "But if the simplification leaves out something
load-bearing—" — is the most natural beat in the exchange; the rest feel
slightly groomed.

This is Minor because the scene's thematic content is doing necessary work
and the overall Davan voice is secure.

---

### Aldric

The Story Bible's voice note for Aldric calls for two languages in
open conflict — scholarship and priesthood stumbling against each other,
liturgical vocabulary arriving where institutional vocabulary used to sit.
The Bible itself admits this hasn't landed consistently through Chapter 13.

**Major — Aldric's voice in Chapters 3 and 7 reads as thoughtful-scholar
with liturgical flavoring rather than a man whose professional framework
is cracking under the weight of something that destabilizes it.**

The Chapter 7 lamp-pinching scene — "A compulsion that did not feel like
compulsion. A question the body had already answered while the mind was
still formulating its objections" — is the clearest failure point. The
prose is exactly right for a reflective, self-aware 53-year-old scholar.
It is insufficiently wrong. Aldric at this point has been secretly visiting
a sealed room for three weeks, lying to his archivist about institutional
integrity, and lying to Prioress Crane's face. The prose should show
someone who has lost traction on his own self-concept — sentences that
start with one word and correct to another, formal constructions that
break before they complete. Instead we get: "A vigil, he thought, and
the word arrived liturgical — the wrong language for a man drawn back to
a sealed room against his own decision, and the only language he had."

That's an excellent observation. It's Aldric *noticing* that he's using
the wrong language. The Bible wants Aldric actually *doing* it — using
*providence* where *procedure* belongs, not catching himself and noting
the misuse. There's a difference between a character who is self-aware
about his collapse and one who is *in* it. The audit Bible note — "a man
of the cloth realizing his god is a shipwreck" — requires more active
friction than currently present.

**Minor — Ch. 7's Crane confrontation is very good but Aldric recovers
his institutional voice too cleanly.**

The *down* slip — Aldric hearing himself say "down" when he means the
lower storage, a fraction too late — is a fine touch. But it's the only
one. For a man conducting secret research while a doctrinal reviewer is
working through his archive, the scene could use one more place where
Aldric's language slips before he catches it. Currently the error arrives
and is managed; it needs a second smaller one that doesn't get caught.

---

### Vane

Vane's voice — clipped noir, world-as-cost/yield, interiority presented
as professional analysis even when it is feeling — is the most consistent
POV voice in this block. Chapter 8 is the strongest single POV chapter in
Chs. 1-10.

**One Minor flag: Ch. 4's Bloom-high passage is slightly over-explained.**

> "This lasted some portion of the night. She lost track of time in the
> usual way."

"The usual way" is very Vane. But the paragraph before it — "Not dissolved
— she was still herself, still Vane, still in a chair..." — goes on for
six lines to explain the experience from inside it. Vane's interior
passages are professional analysis even when they're feeling; a character
who would write "the usual way" would not also give us three careful
sentences locating herself in space during the experience. The Bloom high
is the one moment where Vane's prose should go slightly less organized,
not more. The second Bloom scene in Chapter 8 handles this better, staying
at the working level and letting the altered state show in the work rather
than in self-description.

## 2. Narrator Leak / Authorial Intrusion

**Major — "the color the whole community had agreed to love" (Ch. 1,
Hett's Blooming scene)**

> "the firelight caught the membrane-thin patches at the boy's throat and
> made them glow a wet pink that Maren understood, watching, was the color
> the whole community had agreed to love."

Previously flagged in the sample review and still present. This is the
one definitive narrator-intrusion in the block. The observation requires
Maren to have had a sociological insight she has not been shown formulating
— "the whole community had agreed to love" is a cultural critic's framing,
not a seventeen-year-old watching a Blooming in real time. Maren would
register the community's *response* — the glad sound, the weeping, the
faces — and draw her own conclusion about it, in her own spare terms.

The sentence could be fixed by cutting the "agreed to love" construction
and letting the description of the communal reaction do the work Maren
herself is already doing: "Maren understood what she was pretending to be
part of." The current line tells us what the scene means. The scene already
shows it.

**Minor — Davan's Ch. 6 market observation is momentarily over-explained.**

> "the crowd was responding to that absence the way a body responds to a
> splinter — with a local inflammation, a focused rejection, an effort to
> push out the thing that didn't belong."

The simile is arresting and justified by Davan's voice — he circles and
notices, he'd reach for this. But "an effort to push out the thing that
didn't belong" is the simile explaining itself. The first two elements
(local inflammation, focused rejection) are the image; the third is the
author making sure we got it. Cut the third element and the image lands
harder.

## 3. Sensory Filter Verbs

The "she watched" / "she heard" / "she felt" problem is substantially
reduced in this draft. Two residual instances worth noting:

**Minor — Ch. 5, Maren at the patrol rise:**

> "She sat down in the scrub and her hands were shaking."

Clean. No filter verb.

> "She made herself watch."

Also clean. But fifty words earlier: "She could make out even at distance"
— which is technically fine but runs close.

The systematic scrub held. The few remaining filter constructions are where
they belong: in moments where Maren is specifically noting her own state of
attention ("she made herself watch," "she caught herself"). This is
appropriate — the filter is doing psychological work.

**The one location where filter verbs still linger unnecessarily is Vane's
Ch. 4 closing, pre-bed:**

> "she thought about what she kept finding in the girl's traces: that same
> quality of almost nothing."

"She thought about" is weak framing for what is presented as Vane's
professional analysis. "That same quality of almost nothing" should just
arrive, without the announcement. This is Minor.

## 4. Telling Where Showing Was Available

**Minor — Ch. 3, Aldric's interior:**

> "He found himself thinking of it as a text. An unread scripture — and
> then he caught the word and set it aside, because that was the wrong
> register..."

"He found himself thinking of it as a text" is telling: we are told he
has this thought, then told he noticed he had it. The catching should be
the scene — show the word *scripture* arrive in his notes and then get
crossed out. It has more force that way. The journal entry he writes at
the end of Chapter 3 does this better ("He crossed out the last sentence.
He wrote it again. He left it.") — that scene shows the ambivalence rather
than describing it.

**Minor — Davan's Ch. 2 closing:**

> "That might be the loneliest thing he could imagine."

This is the sentence Davan uses to close his reflection on the Hollow girl.
It's clearly true to his character. But it tells us his emotional
conclusion rather than sitting with the incompleteness that is his actual
voice signature. His voice reaches toward things without landing. "That
might be the loneliest thing he could imagine" *lands* — it resolves what
should stay slightly open. Something like the paragraph simply ending with
the image of walking into morning after would be truer to how Davan
processes.

## 5. Repetition & Echo Phrases

The verbal tic sweep has done its work. I found zero instances of "the
specific X," "the particular X," and "in the way of" that felt like the
old ambient density. The remaining instances are genuinely doing something:

> "the look that said *I am not going to say what I'm thinking about*. It
> was a warm look. It had a lot of weight underneath it."

"It had a lot of weight underneath it" echoes several similar constructions
across the chapters but earns it each time because weight-beneath-surface
is doing thematic work, not filling space.

**One remaining tic that escaped the sweep:**

> "the way X does when Y" construction

It appears 11-14 times across Chs. 1-10 in various forms: "the way you
sit at the Rite," "the way she did when she was frightened," "the way you
do in a sealed underground room," "the way a word you've known all your
life suddenly means what it says." Each individual instance is fine. The
aggregate density starts to feel like the author's prose habit rather than
a character's observation pattern, especially because it spans all four
POVs. A 30% reduction would be invisible and improve the reading.

## 6. Cliché, Adverb Cruft, Beat-Sheet Sentences

**Minor — "the body's verdict arriving before the mind's" (Ch. 6)**

This construction — the body knowing before the mind — appears three times
in this block in different forms. It's a real observation and it's earnt in
each case (Ch. 2 with Davan stopping on the road, Ch. 6 with the market
gap, Ch. 10 with Maren at the road's edge). Three uses in ten chapters
tips it from observation into tic.

**Minor — Beat-sheet sentence in Ch. 8, Vane:**

> "She had a job. The job had its own momentum, one of the things she
> valued about jobs. Simple machines: input, process, output. You located
> the target and you delivered the target and you collected the fee and
> the job closed and you moved to the next one. The mechanism was clean.
> She had run it for ten years without serious difficulty."

This is very good Vane voice. But the final sentence — "She had run it for
ten years without serious difficulty" — is beat-sheet: it is telling us
the contrast the scene is about to provide (she is now having serious
difficulty). Vane would not name the contrast. She would state the
mechanism and stop. The paragraph is stronger without the last sentence.

**No clichés found.** The prose is, throughout, alert to cliché and
navigates past it. This is not a problem in this draft.

**Adverbs:** Clean. The sweep is visible here. Not a structural note.

## 7. Dialogue Ring

**Found nothing significant.** Dialogue across all four POVs is
differentiated and functional. The Lera-Davan exchange in Chapter 6 is
slightly formal on both sides (noted under Davan above), but this is a
voice question not a ring question. The Vane-Maren first contact in
Chapter 10 — brief, charged, information-dense — is the best dialogue in
the block.

One small note: Davan's exchange with the farmer in Ch. 2 ("Neither have
I") is a clean beat. The farmer's lines occasionally tip into exposition
delivery — "Looking for someone. Young woman, dark hair, wouldn't show the
change" — but this is structural (Davan needs the information) and not
egregious.

## 8. Paragraphs That Earn Their Page Space

**First: the Hett Orval Blooming, Ch. 1**

> "He was crying. He was smiling while he cried, the two things on his
> face simultaneously in the way Maren had always read as wrong on people
> until she'd understood that it was not wrong, it was only honest, because
> the Chrysalis did not consult the body about what it was prepared to
> bear."

This is the best single sentence in ten chapters. It does everything the
novel needs to do in one construction: it shows the Chrysalis as real,
painful, and not malicious; it shows Maren's emotional complexity (she
doesn't hate these people); and it shows her analytical nature, catching
herself in an old judgment and revising it, mid-sentence, in front of the
reader. The Blooming scene as a whole earns its length.

**Second: Vane tracking Maren's absence in Ch. 8**

> "It wasn't there. The girl smelled like a person and nothing else, and
> Vane had been tracking people long enough that she only now realized
> she'd been filtering for that baseline warmth unconsciously, using it as
> part of her overall read on a subject. Now that it wasn't there the
> absence was something her nose kept returning to with the confused
> insistence of a tongue finding the gap where a tooth used to be."

This earns its page. The tooth-gap simile is the novel's best simile —
precise, physical, unexpected, available to Vane's sensory POV without
feeling labored. It also recontextualizes Maren's absence as a *discovery*
rather than a confirmation, which is exactly right for the scene's dramatic
function.

**Third: Aldric's liturgy-shift in Ch. 7**

> "*They do not leave. They have never left.* He had spoken those words
> ten thousand times. They had always meant faithfulness. The Witnesses,
> constant, devoted, keeping their vigil over the world they had gifted. He
> heard them now and the meaning shifted under him like a floor settling —
> *They do not leave.* Not faithfulness. Grief. The stubborn circling of
> something that would not abandon what it had lost. *They have never left*
> — not because they chose to stay, but because they could not bring
> themselves to go."

This is the novel's best theological passage. The liturgy-as-trap is the
exact register the Aldric voice should be in, and the reread of the
familiar phrase across three lines is structurally controlled. It does what
the Aldric voice should always be doing — the same words in two registers
simultaneously — and it does it at full power.

## 9. Verdict

This is a substantially cleaner manuscript than V1-V2 would have required.
The verbal tic sweep held. The major structural problems (Maren Ch. 9
monologue, Aldric Ch. 3 functional-theology, Davan Ch. 2 empathic-sense
exposition dump) are resolved. What remains is a set of fine-grained
concerns: Aldric's voice conflict is described more than enacted, Maren
slips into slightly abstract register at two chapter-closing moments, and
one narrator-intrusion sentence ("the color the whole community had agreed
to love") is still present and still the clearest authorial intrusion in
the text. The Prologue is among the novel's most effective material —
seven distinct voices, none of which sound like each other, doing
worldbuilding through testimony rather than exposition. The manuscript is
approaching the state where sentence-level work and chapter-level
structural decisions are the remaining work, not character-voice
reconstruction. Diminishing returns is the honest headline here.
