## 1. Non-Smile-Mouth Tic Inventory

The bible and line editor flag this as spreading from a Vane-specific
characterization to an author-level structural habit. Full inventory below.

**Ch. 8 (Vane POV):**
> "Betta's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile."
— Attributed to Betta. First appearance of the tic on a secondary character
outside Vane. Vane observing it.

**Ch. 10 (Maren/Vane POV), Scene 1 — Vane in ditch-grass:**
> "Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile.
> An acknowledgment."
— Vane's mouth; narrated by Maren POV. Canonical Vane use.

**Ch. 10 (Maren/Vane POV), Scene 2 — after "You're the one they're looking for":**
> "The corner of Vane's mouth moved again. This time it made it to something
> that was, briefly, a smile. It changed her face considerably."
— Second Vane use in the same chapter. The two uses in Ch. 10 in close
succession signal over-reliance.

**Ch. 14 (Maren POV):**
> "Vane's expression didn't change, but she turned her head slightly — the
> fraction of movement that meant her nose was working..."
— Not a mouth tic, but structural equivalent: Vane's reaction cued by a
partial physical tell rather than a stated emotion. CLEAN variant; flag only
for comparison.

**Ch. 16 (Maren POV) — Maren herself:**
> "Maren's mouth moved, a brief compression at one corner that she let pass
> without committing to."
— The tic is now on the protagonist, narrated in her own POV. This is the
most significant spread. Maren is not Vane; the gesture belongs to Vane's
register.

**Summary by character (Chs. 1-16):**

| Character | Chapter | Variant | Notes |
|-----------|---------|---------|-------|
| Betta | Ch. 8 | "mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile" | Secondary char; Vane POV |
| Vane | Ch. 10 | "corner of the woman's mouth... not quite a smile" | Canonical use |
| Vane | Ch. 10 | "corner of Vane's mouth moved again... briefly, a smile" | Second use, same chapter |
| Maren | Ch. 16 | "mouth moved, a brief compression at one corner" | MOST URGENT: protagonist |

**Cross-reference line editor findings (Chs. 17-20):**
- Ch. 17: Reth's mouth (corner movement)
- Ch. 18: Asha's mouth twitch (subtler variant)
- Ch. 20: Renner's mouth "did something that might have been the ghost
  of approval"

**Total across Chs. 1-20: 7 confirmed instances + at least 2 near-variants
across 6 different characters.** The tic has fully escaped Vane's voice and
is now the author's default gesture for suppressed emotion in any character.

**Priority fix:** Ch. 16, Maren instance (highest urgency — protagonist
cross-contamination). Ch. 10 double-use (reduce to one). Ch. 8 Betta variant
(give Betta a more specific physical tell).

## 2. Filter-Word Summary (Per-Chapter Density + Samples)

Methodology mirrors the line editor's Ch. 17-20 analysis. "Felt," "noticed,"
"realized," "saw," "heard," "thought" as openers to observations the prose
could render directly.

### Chapter 1 (Maren)
Count: **4 clear instances**

- "she felt it before she saw it, the slight change in how the linen sat"
  — The exact example cited in moons_feedback.md (FB-7). Should be the
  disc's first appearance as a filter-free physical event.
- "She told herself that a man from New Plymouth would ask questions about
  everything as a matter of professional habit. She told herself this was
  not specific."
  — "she told herself" repeated; mild but doubles the filter.
- "She heard the footsteps of the deacon's allotted guest-room, overhead"
  — "She heard" as opener; the footsteps can arrive without announcement.
- "She heard, an hour after the house had settled, the low sound of voices"
  — Second "she heard" in the same scene, same function.

### Chapter 2 (Davan)
Count: **3 instances**

- "He was not trying to listen to the merchants. They were just the loudest
  thing in the room." — The sentence-pair is a filter framing of what could
  simply be the merchants' conversation arriving.
- "He became aware of someone nearby." — Classic filter opener.
- "He had always felt things in rooms." — Acceptable; retrospective
  characterization, less urgent than in-scene filters.

### Chapter 3 (Aldric)
Count: **5 instances** (highest in Act One)

- "He had sat with Sella because she had asked..."
  — Reasonable; expository.
- "He looked at the seven options for a long time." — The looking is
  announced rather than shown through what appears to him as he looks.
- "He found it remarkable now, in the way you find something remarkable
  when you look at it from a direction you haven't looked from before."
  — Filter + meta-commentary on the filter. Remove the bracket.
- "He became aware, in the way you become aware of a thing you have
  already done, that his right hand was raised..." — The "became aware"
  construction is the Aldric-specific filter pattern. This is the first
  of several.
- "He saw it now." — Minimal filter but present; the symbol could simply
  be described.

**Ch. 3 note:** Aldric's POV has more filter constructions than the others,
partly because his voice operates through a layer of institutional
self-narration ("he told himself," "he understood," "he had always found").
Some of these are voice-appropriate (a man observing himself lying); others
are mechanical. Density is higher than Chs. 1-2 and noticeably higher than
Chs. 5, 9 (the cleanest chapters).

### Chapter 4 (Vane)
Count: **2 instances** (lowest in Act One alongside Ch. 5)

- "She noted the stumble and went around to the yard." — "Noted" as filter;
  but this is Vane's mode and functions as character voice. BORDERLINE.
- "She caught it." (re: Maren's chemical trace) — The catching can be
  rendered directly. Low priority.

**Ch. 4 note:** Consistent with the line editor's finding that Vane's clipped
style naturally reduces filter density. Her "catalogued/noted" constructions
are borderline — they read as in-voice, not as filters, most of the time.

### Chapter 5 (Maren)
Count: **2 instances** (joint cleanest Act One chapter)

- "She felt, for the first time since leaving Ashwick, genuinely stupid."
  — Direct, in-voice; borderline. The next sentence could stand alone
  without the "she felt" announcement.
- "she felt a specific interior lurch: the lie from before, still in her
  mouth" — "she felt" as setup for an image that carries itself. Cut opener.

**Ch. 5 note:** One of the two cleanest chapters in Act One. Maren's
cataloguing mode tends to eliminate filters because her observations are
already in the first-person register that bypasses them.

### Chapter 6 (Davan)
Count: **4 instances**

- "He looked at all of it." — Three words of filter before the emotional
  weather that follows.
- "he felt the other thing" — Filter opener on significant empathic event.
- "He watched this from three stalls away and the compound feeling was
  clearer now." — "he watched" + "feeling was clearer now" stacked.
- "He was not sure how he felt about being legible." — This is CLEAN;
  it's Davan's voice, the quality of his self-observation is the point.

### Chapter 7 (Aldric)
Count: **6 instances** (highest chapter count in manuscript)

- "he sat with the particular flat discomfort of a man discovering..."
  — "sat with" as processing metaphor + filter (see Section 3).
- "He heard confessions and granted blessings and settled two disputes"
  — Summary filter; acceptable in this expository context.
- "She had come to him on the second week with a question" / "she wanted
  his guidance because she believed..." — The embedded focalization on
  Petra is filter-heavy; the scene is partly in Aldric's perception of her.
- "He looked at the glass for another long moment." — Weak filter before
  the complex observation that follows.
- "He became aware of a thing he had not noticed" re: the pencil.
  — "Became aware" is the signature Aldric filter form; occurs three times
  in Ch. 7 alone.
- "He could not be certain." — Borderline; this is Aldric's epistemology,
  not a filter. CLEAN.

**Ch. 7 note:** Highest filter density in the manuscript. Aldric's voice
of formal self-observation structurally produces more filters. The "became
aware" construction is used 3x in this chapter; needs systematic reduction.
The Petra scene is the most acute problem — double-filter ("she believed /
she had said") softens a moment that should be sharper.

### Chapter 8 (Vane)
Count: **2 instances** — consistent with Ch. 4 pattern.

- "She stood in the doorway for another moment and revised her working
  model of the girl." — "revised" is fine; what follows could stand
  without announcement.
- "She noted the shape of what he'd left out and walked on." — "noted"
  as Vane mode; borderline acceptable.

### Chapter 9 (Maren)
Count: **1 instance** — cleanest chapter in manuscript.

- "She felt, for the first time with her full attention rather than with the
  part of her that was managing the fact of it." — The reframe scene at the
  boundary stone; the "she felt" opener somewhat weakens the moment. Otherwise

**Ch. 9 note:** The woodcutter's shelter chapter is the prose high-water mark
for Maren's voice. Almost no filters. The shelter's internal scene where she

- "She did not know what to make of this." — Mild filter; borderline

### Chapter 11 (Vane)

- "She could smell it from ten feet" re: Maren's emotional state.

### Chapter 12 (Davan)
Count: **4 instances**

  — Functional filter; acceptable in establishing shot.
- "He almost left. He was halfway to standing when his hand found the piece

### Chapter 13 (Aldric)

### Chapter 15 (Davan)

- "He sat against the wall and listened and tried to map the shape of what

- "Davan's skin-light pulsed once under his sleeves — a warm amber flare

## 8. Voice-Blur Passages (Aldric/Vane and Others)

Per FB-6, Aldric and Vane both operate in an "analytical/detached" register
that can make their internal monologues indistinguishable without chapter
headers. Candidate passages are passages of 100+ words in either POV where
swapping the chapter header would not immediately alert a careful reader.

### Aldric/Vane blur (most acute cases):

**Ch. 7 (Aldric), the Crane encounter in the corridor:**
> "She had not asked him directly. She was waiting for him to make a mistake,
> or to decide to trust her, and she appeared to be comfortable waiting for
> either. He went to bed and lay awake until the early hours..."

This could be Vane assessing a threat. Aldric's institutional vocabulary
("Prioress," "Keeper") is what anchors it; remove those proper nouns and
the register is indistinguishable from Vane's professional-threat analysis.
The passage lacks any liturgical or theological coloring.

**Ch. 7 (Aldric), stopping himself from filing the report:**
> "He was a careful man. Careful his entire adult life, with a robust
> appreciation for the ways in which the human mind found patterns when
> it very much wanted patterns to be there."

This could be Vane's "I'm a professional, professionals identify their
difficulties" mode. Swap "careful man" for "professional" and it belongs
in Ch. 8.

**Ch. 13 (Aldric), reasoning around the conclusion:**
> "He thought about Crane. Not as an obstacle — he had been thinking of her
> that way for weeks, the careful adversary navigating the same corridors..."
> (extends ~150 words)

Aldric contemplating Crane using the language of operational tactics. This
is the clearest Aldric/Vane blur in the manuscript. A reader stripped of
the chapter header would assign it to either, equally.

**Maren/Davan blur (mild, one case):**

**Ch. 16 (Maren), observing Davan's arrival in the cooperage yard:**
> "He didn't speak immediately. He stood in the doorway and let the yard
> settle around him — the sound of it, the smell of it, her in it — and
> Maren recognized the quality because it was one she shared: the need to
> arrive in a place fully before you did anything in it."

Maren is here assigning herself and Davan the same observational quality.
This is not a blur, but it is a flattening — both characters are described
as sharing the same mode of entering a space, which reduces their
distinctiveness in the reader's memory of the scene.

**Summary:** Aldric/Vane blur is real and appears in at least 3 passages
in Chs. 7 and 13. Bible fix (FB-6) recommendation of "liturgical register
more strongly" is correct. Specifically: the Crane-as-adversary analysis
in Ch. 13 should have more theological guilt in it — Aldric isn't assessing
a tactical problem, he's assessing a fellow servant of an institution he
is betraying. That guilt should be audible.

## 9. Single-Tic Propping Per Character

Assessment of whether any character's distinctiveness is carried by a single
physical tic or phrase at the expense of broader voice development.

**Aldric — lamp tic:**
The bible explicitly tracks the lamp-pinching tic and its arc. In Chs. 1-16
it appears in Chs. 3 (introduction), 7 (twice), 13, and has bible notes for
Chs. 17, 25, 29. The tic does real work: it establishes the body acting
below the mind's level of awareness, which is Aldric's thematic situation.
VERDICT: The tic is necessary and well-used, but Chs. 7 and 13 lean on it
heavily (two appearances per chapter). When it appears without variation,
it becomes a stage direction. The Ch. 7 moment where he catches himself
about to extinguish his only light source in a sealed underground room is
the best use; subsequent appearances should be less prominent. Not yet
fully propping — but at risk by Ch. 17+.

**Vane — "filed it" / compartmentalization:**
Vane's professional compartmentalization language ("she filed it in the
category of things that were the supply chain's business rather than hers")
appears in Chs. 4, 8, 11, and 14 with nearly identical phrasing. This is
more of a risk than the lamp tic because it replaces interiority rather than
revealing it. Aldric's lamp tic shows us something; Vane's "filing"
metaphor explains that she's suppressing something without showing what the
suppression costs. The Ch. 11 moment where the compartment "opens the way
a sealed room opens when the mortar fails" is excellent. Before Ch. 11,
every "filed it" should be examined: can the thing she's filing be shown
leaking through instead?

**Davan — bioluminescent skin:**
Skin-light is Davan's second narrator, visible to readers but not to him.
Used well: amber-fear at the market (Ch. 6), blue-calm on the barge (Ch. 6),
the involuntary amber flare in the cooperage (Ch. 15), the green-gold
(Ch. 19, not yet in scope). However, Davan's voice beyond the skin-light
is relatively thin in Chs. 2 and 6 — his circling mode is present, but
the specific language of his interiority (the half-thoughts he doesn't
force) is less distinctive than the bible promises. He becomes more himself
in Chs. 12 and 15. The carving habit serves him better than the skin-light
as a voice marker because it's active not reactive.

**Maren — coal-pouch arm / cataloguing exits:**
These are her twin tics and both are appropriate. The arm she holds close
is the most economical piece of physical characterization in the manuscript:
it does character, backstory, and theme simultaneously. The exit-cataloguing
mode ("she catalogued it automatically: one exit behind her") is in-voice
and doesn't prop — it actively characterizes. Neither of these is
over-relied upon. Maren's voice is the most consistently distinct in the
manuscript, likely because her tics are behavioral rather than physical.

## 10. Feedback-Item Traces (FB-3, FB-4, FB-6, FB-9, FB-10)

### FB-3: Hollow Stigma Underweighted

**Where the concern IS present in the manuscript:**

- **Ch. 1** (Maren, Autumnal Rite): The Rite scene shows belonging-through-
  change as warm, real, joyful — "the particular comfort of not feeling it
  alone." Maren's exclusion is felt as loss, but not as oppression. The

- **Ch. 6** (Davan, Confluence market): "...the crowd around him was

  Hollow menace. The persecution feels general (fugitive logic) rather than

**Candidate passages for targeted addition/revision:**

- The Autumnal Rite scene (Ch. 1) could include one moment of Hollow-specific
  social texture: a glance Maren reads from another villager who suspects,
  an exclusion from the post-Rite gathering that is casually structural rather
  than openly cruel. The material is there; one sentence could deepen it.

  moment later in the same chapter — Davan himself being read by the empathic
  sense as clearly NOT hollow, a brief flash of comfort at being recognized

### FB-4: Vane's Bloom Sacrifice Underearned

**Where the concern IS present in the manuscript:**

- **Ch. 4**: The Bloom use is described as a tool for professional clarity.
  The cost (bark spreading, dependency) is acknowledged but not demonstrated

  but controlled.

- **Ch. 11-14**: The progression of withdrawal — episodes worsening, the

  — is well-staged. The PROBLEM is that the progression begins after Vane
  has already walked away from the contract. The reader sees withdrawal but
  has not seen pre-withdrawal failure to benchmark against.

**Candidate for targeted addition/revision:**

- **Ch. 8, road section after the first vision**: This is the most fixable
  location. Extend the intrusion of the vision by 1-2 paragraphs. Let Vane
  lose the trail for a period — not dramatically, but enough that she has
  to backtrack, and she knows she would not have lost it on Bloom. The
  current version has her immediately resuming tracking at full capacity.
  One moment of actual tracking failure would establish what the Bloom
  costs to lose before Ch. 11 establishes that she is losing it.

### FB-6: Aldric/Vane Voice Blur

**Specific passages documented under Section 8.** Additional observations:

  Both use the word unselfconsciously to mean self-defining professional

### FB-9: Crane as Physical Threat

## 11. Additional Patterns Discovered

### A. "Going nowhere and going there with great dedication" / Moons Observation Refrain

This phrase (or close variants) appears **three times** across Chs. 1-16:

- **Prologue I**: "patient beyond the understanding of any living thing"
  (thematic statement, not the phrase)
- **Ch. 6** (Davan): "going nowhere and going there with great dedication"
  (the moons' orbits, explicit)
- **Ch. 9** (Maren): "moved in their slow and patient arcs, going nowhere
  in particular and doing it with great dedication" (near-identical)
- **Ch. 10** (Maren): "moving in their slow and patient arcs above her,
  going nowhere in particular and going there with great dedication"
  (same again)

This is a notable tic: the author's formulation for the moons' orbital
patience repeats nearly verbatim across three chapters in two different POVs.
It likely started as a beautiful line in Ch. 6 and was unconsciously repeated
because it felt right. It's a signal to the reader of thematic weight; the
repetition is counterproductive because it underlines the author's hand rather
than the characters' perspective.

**Fix**: Keep one instance (Ch. 6 is the best deployment because it's Davan
reading the symbol on the temple doorway). Replace Ch. 9 and Ch. 10 uses
with unique formulations that land the same thematic note from Maren's
distinct angle.

### B. "He/she has the quality of..." construction

A persistent characterization template across all four POVs, producing
descriptions of the form: "[person X] had the quality of [person type who
does Y]."

Sample instances:
- "the quality of someone who had solved so many practical problems that
  solving them had become invisible" (Betta, Ch. 5)
- "the quality of someone who is accustomed to having his questions answered"
  (lead rider, Ch. 2)
- "the quality of someone for whom the professional and the personal were
  not separate territories" (Maren re: Vane, Ch. 10)
- "a quality Davan associated with people who worked with their hands"
  (re: Asha, Ch. 15)
- "the quality of someone who has found use for what she had" (Vane re:
  her mutation, Ch. 4)

Count: at least 15 instances across 16 chapters. The template is even more
prevalent than "the particular [noun]." It's the author's preferred mode for
rapid characterization of secondary figures and for POV characters reading
each other. Some uses are economical and effective; many are rote. Needs the
same global pass as "the particular [noun]."

### C. The "not X, more like Y" clarification construction

Appears frequently as a two-step characterization:
- "Not loneliness. She had made her peace with solitude long ago..." (Vane,
  Ch. 4)
- "Not the full experience, nothing so organized — just the leading edge of
  it" (Vane, Ch. 8)
- "Not anger — he'd learned to recognize anger's specific spike. Something
  lower and wider..." (Davan, Ch. 6)
- "Not a wrongness exactly — more like hearing a familiar song with a note
  missing" (Davan re: Maren's absence, Ch. 12)
- "Not frightened, not angry. Something more like someone revising a map
  in real time" (Vane re: Maren after letter, Ch. 11)

Count: at least 10 instances. This is a tic of the epistemic-uncertainty
grammar documented in Section 7 but distinct from the mouth-tic: it's
a correction structure, redefining an emotion by negating one term and
substituting another. Each individual use is elegant; at 10+ uses across
the manuscript it becomes the author's preferred mode for describing
interior states that resist categorization. Some characters (Davan,
appropriately) should use it more; Vane (who categorizes rather than
refuses categories) should use it less.

### D. "Smelled like" / "smelled of" as scene-entry device

Every chapter entry or major scene shift in Chs. 1-8 uses smell as its
opening sensory anchor, often with the identical structure:
"[place] smelled of [items in list]."
- Ch. 1: "The herb woman's kitchen smelled of drying things — fennel and
  coltsfoot..."
- Ch. 4: "The docks district smelled of its usual compound — brine and tar
  and..."
- Ch. 6: "The city announced itself by smell before it came into view."

This is a Vane-specific technique (her nose is her dominant sense) but it
bleeds into all four POVs in Act One. By Act Two, the pattern is more
controlled: Chs. 12-16 open with visual or action anchors more often.
The bleeding in Act One is worth noting but may be acceptable as an
establishing-shot choice.

### E. "He/she walked on" as chapter/section close

Several sections end with "[character] walked on" or close variants:
- Ch. 1: "She walked north." (closing line)
- Ch. 2: "He walked. The road curved north..."
- Ch. 5: "She walked it." (closing line)
- Ch. 6: (various walking closes on sections)
- Ch. 9: "She walked." (closing line)
- Ch. 10: "She walked north." / "She kept walking"
- Ch. 11: "She walked anyway." (closing line)

"She walked" as a chapter-ending beat recurs across six chapters in Chs.
1-11, almost all Maren or Vane chapters. It is thematically appropriate
for characters defined by movement through uncertainty. But the identical
structure repeated six times across eleven chapters reduces its power each
time. Closing lines should do more differentiated work.

## 13. Bible/Manuscript Voice-Signature Mismatches

### Aldric: Bible says "liturgical register more strongly" — manuscript doesn't deliver it yet

The bible (Section 2) and the feedback (FB-6) both call for Aldric's internal
monologue to carry more liturgical language — "a man of the cloth realizing
his god is a shipwreck." In Chs. 3, 7, and 13, this tone is gestured at but
not delivered. Aldric thinks in the language of a scholar and administrator,
not a priest. His theological training should produce involuntary liturgical
reference points when he encounters the undercroft objects — he should be
reaching for scripture and finding it inadequate, not reaching for epistemology.

**Bible/manuscript mismatch:** The bible says "institutional vocabulary
deployed with increasing awareness of its inadequacy." The manuscript shows
the inadequacy (he can't complete the report form, he keeps going back to
the chamber) but doesn't show the vocabulary. The voice is already working
toward the right characterization; the liturgical register is the missing
piece.

**Recommended bible note:** The Aldric voice signature in the bible should
specify: "Involuntary liturgical language appears when confronted with the
undercroft — his scholarship finds the object, his priesthood finds the
meaning, and the two languages are in conflict." Currently the bible doesn't
distinguish these two modes.

### Davan: Bible says "half-thoughts he doesn't force to conclusion" — manuscript partially confirms

The bible is accurate about Davan's circling mode, and the Ch. 15 and Ch. 16
Davan scenes deliver on it. However, Chs. 2 and 6 are less in-voice: Davan
in these chapters is more explicitly interpretive, more willing to name what
he's experiencing ("This was not a thought he'd decided to think"). The
half-thought quality the bible describes is more present in the later chapters
than in the earlier ones, suggesting the author found the voice during drafting.
Chs. 2 and 6 may benefit from a light revision toward less completion.

**This is a case where the bible should note the drafting evolution** rather
than treating the voice as uniform across the manuscript.

### Vane: "Clipped noir register" — confirmed by manuscript, but "almost no interiority" is too strong

The bible says "Interiority rationed to almost nothing except what bleeds
through action." The manuscript Vane chapters actually contain more explicit
interiority than this suggests — Ch. 4's Bloom meditation, Ch. 8's extended
compartmentalization passage, Ch. 11's decision scene with the wafer in her
hand are all clearly interior. What IS true is that Vane's interiority reads
as justified professional analysis rather than feeling. The bible's description
is not wrong about the result but slightly mischaracterizes the mechanism.

**Recommended bible update:** "Interiority presented as professional analysis
even when it is feeling — the categories keep things clean, or used to."
This more accurately describes what the manuscript actually does with Vane
and would better guide future drafters.

## 14. Priority List: If the Author Had 4 Hours for Manuscript-Level Prose Work

**1. Filter-word pass on Chs. 7 and 13 (Aldric chapters)**
Approximately 45 minutes. Chs. 7 and 13 carry the highest filter density and
the worst voice-blur. The Petra scene in Ch. 7 (filter-reported hypocrisy
moment) and the "he thought about Crane" passage in Ch. 13 (200-word Vane-
register analysis) are the two highest-impact single fixes in the entire
manuscript. Cutting the filters in these two chapters alone will visibly
improve the prose quality and reduce the Aldric/Vane blur without requiring
structural changes.

**2. "The particular [noun]" and "had the quality of" global pass**
Approximately 60 minutes. Both templates appear 15-25 times each. A
search-and-assess pass eliminating the weakest 50% of each would sharpen
every chapter without touching the story. These are the two most pervasive
prose tics and the easiest to address mechanically. Cutting one per page
will not be noticed by any reader; leaving them all is what produces the
sensation of a homogenized multi-POV voice.

**3. Non-smile mouth tic: address the Ch. 16 Maren instance and the Ch. 10
double-use**
Approximately 20 minutes. The Ch. 16 Maren instance (protagonist's mouth
doing the Vane gesture) is the single most urgent fix in the manuscript.
It takes one sentence to fix. The Ch. 10 double-use can be resolved by
cutting the first (less specific) instance. Low effort, high payoff.

**4. Add one line of physical description for the inner orbit symbol
(Ch. 5 or Ch. 9)**
Approximately 20 minutes. FB-10 is fixable with a single sentence added
to the disc-examination scene in Ch. 9, where Maren is already looking
at the disc's edge and the markings. "Three markings around the edge"
becomes something with enough geometry for the reader to track across
the remaining chapters. This closes a reader-experience gap at minimal
cost.

**5. Extend the Ch. 8 Bloom vision to create one clear tracking failure**
Approximately 45 minutes. This is the FB-4 fix — the most structurally
important revision in the manuscript because it affects how the reader
experiences Vane's sacrifice in Chs. 11-14. Adding a paragraph or two
to the vision in Ch. 8, letting the trail go cold for an hour before
Vane recovers, establishes the before-state for the sacrifice. Without
it, the reader sees withdrawal happening but hasn't seen what Vane was
protecting against.
