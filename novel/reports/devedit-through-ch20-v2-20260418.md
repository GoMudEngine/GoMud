# Editorial Letter — through-ch20-v2

Dear Author,

I've read the prologue, Chapters 1-16 of the revised manuscript, and the v2 drafts of Chapters 17-20. I read the outline and the story bible first for orientation, then the text cold as a first-time reader. What follows is the honest assessment you asked for. I will not perform encouragement; the manuscript is interesting enough to deserve a real editor's notes rather than cheerleading, and your willingness to cut deep means we can talk about structural moves rather than line-level polish.

## The Book You're Writing

You are writing a literary science-fantasy novel whose defining move is the refusal to treat its science-fictional armature as a twist. The reveal — the moons are ships, the Chrysalis is a native organism, the bloodline's monopoly is a circular proof — is not withheld from the reader. By the prologue's fourth fragment the Tidemark harbor-master has already told us. By Chapter 7 Aldric has counted four shapes. The book's premise is not "what is the truth?" but "what do people do inside a story that has become the wrong shape for the world?" That is a mature, specific, unfashionable project. The closest analogue in its bones is the family of novels that treat cosmology-as-sociology — Le Guin's colony books, Michelle West's slower institutional fantasies — rather than Ship-falls-to-Earth science fiction, which tends to resolve its mysteries with a twist.

This is the book's greatest strength and its greatest commercial liability, and we'll come back to that. What's working most clearly: the prose has a settled adult voice, the world's theological apparatus is specific and worked-in, the Aldric thread is exquisite, and you have one genuinely load-bearing thematic idea — *the map is useful but wrong, the question is whether it's wrong enough to send you off a cliff* — that is doing work at every level of the book from the premise down to individual character arcs. Do not cut Lera's speech in Chapter 6. It is the novel's thesis statement and it lands without strain because she is not laboring to deliver it.

## Where It Lives on the Shelf

The comp-title question is genuinely hard, and I want to be honest about that rather than name books that don't fit. The honest answer: this is a weird shelf-sibling of several things that do not often ride together.

**Plausible comps:**
- **Rebecca Roanhorse's *Black Sun* (2020, Saga/Gallery)** — multi-POV literary fantasy with weight on cosmological mystery and a slow build toward a convergence. Roanhorse is your best commercial register: braided POVs, mythic stakes, prose that is considered rather than propulsive. Your book is quieter than hers, but that's the shelf.
- **Emily Tesh's *Some Desperate Glory* (2023, Tordotcom)** — literary SF where the reveal (the received history is a lie) lands early and the back half is about living inside the new truth. Different milieu, but structurally this is the closest working analogue for "the mystery is solved halfway through and the novel keeps going."
- **Tasha Suri's *The Jasmine Throne* (2021, Orbit)** — braided POV, slow-build political-mystical fantasy, dense prose, literary voice. Commercially adjacent.

Less ideal but sometimes-invoked: Katherine Arden, Jesse Ball's *Census*, anything in the "quiet weird fiction" lane that magazines like *The Magazine of F&SF* publish at novel length and Farrar, Straus occasionally buys.

**Who I would NOT comp this to:** Any recent Ship/Colony SF (Gareth Powell, Adrian Tchaikovsky) — the tone is wrong and you'll mis-shelve yourself. Not Le Guin, not Wolfe, not Miéville — every agent will tell you not to compare yourself to giants. Not *Piranesi*, which is everyone's default "literary weird" comp right now and so saturated it's become a red flag.

**Audience:** The reader is in their late twenties through fifties, has read literary fiction and occasionally dips into genre, wants science fiction that reads like a novel rather than a puzzle-box, and is willing to accept a slow burn. This is a demanding reader but a real one, and this is a book specifically for them. Do not try to broaden the aperture. The moment you add ticking-clock urgency or streamline for pace you will lose the reader who is here for *your* particular slowness.

Commercial viability: mid-list debut, literary-SFF crossover imprint (Tordotcom, Saga, Orbit US's literary line, FSG Originals, Erewhon). A strong agent can sell this. It is not a lead-title book, it will not go to auction for a big number, and that is fine — the book is trying to be a book, not a commodity. Plan accordingly.

## The Promise of the Opening

The prologue is doing enormous work, and doing most of it well. Eight fragments, none of them completely right, establishes the epistemology of the entire novel before a single character speaks. The Tidemark telling alone is worth the price of admission — the old patient grief that doesn't belong to you personally but is yours anyway. That is the book the rest of the manuscript should be writing toward. That sentence is the novel's emotional thesis.

Chapter 1 is working harder than it was, I gather from context, and the hollow-stigma has been notched up. It still reads a little quiet to me on a first pass. The Autumnal Rite scene is beautifully observed and the coal-pouch exposure is crisply staged; what's thinner is the *social weight* of what the deacon has seen. Maren overhears Delia crying through a wall. That's intimate, but it is intimacy in a register the reader cannot yet weigh — we don't know yet whether *hollow* is a mild stigma or an abduction warrant. The deacon saying "the bloodline has asked to be told" does considerable work, but the reader is being asked to infer the stakes from a single overheard phrase. One way to strengthen this without rewriting: seed in Chapter 1 a glimpsed Blooming that is grotesque-but-celebrated, as the earlier feedback suggested. I agree with that note; it is the single highest-leverage revision available for the opening. Maren watching Jory and Bram is tender, but a body doing something unwelcome-yet-revered would do more work in three sentences than the overheard conversation does in three pages.

Does the opening promise the book that follows? Mostly. The opening promises slow-burn literary SF with mystical weight, a protagonist who has been performing an identity for years, and an institution that is interested in her for reasons she does not understand. The book delivers all of that. What the opening does not promise — and what the book eventually supplies — is the braided four-POV structure and the Aldric institutional thread. That's not a broken promise, but it is a widening of scope, and the reader should feel the widening as gathering momentum rather than distraction. Right now, the jump from Maren to Davan in Chapter 2 is almost too smooth — the voices are distinct, the situation is parallel (young person, road north, carving/coal-pouch), and the reader doesn't yet sense the braid. By Chapter 4 (Vane) the shape is clear. I'd keep it.

## Macro Structure

This is a three-act book in its bones, but the braided POV disguises the acts and blurs their turn points. Let me grade them:

- **Act I (Chs. 1-10):** Works. Four separate ignitions, each character moved from stasis into the road. The convergence beat — Maren and Vane meeting in Chapter 10 — is the Act I turn, and it's earned. It's the best single chapter in the manuscript through Ch. 16 because it has a human-scale moment (the whittled bird, "you could have walked past me") carrying the entire novel's themes without preaching.

- **Act II (Chs. 11-22, Chs. 11-20 written/v2):** Here is where the architecture strains. Act II's job in a braid like this is to bring the pieces into contact and complicate the question, not re-state it. The manuscript does both, but the balance tips toward re-statement. Chapter 13 (Aldric + Brennan's letter) and Chapter 15 (the cooperage meeting) deliver most of the second-act information dump, and by Chapter 16 the reader has been told the answer at least four times from four directions. The Ch. 15 cooperage meeting specifically reads as the scene where the book puts its thesis on the table for review rather than lived. The v2 Chapter 19 (Davan, Edvar, the face) is doing the same beat a third time.

- **Act III (Chs. 23-30, unwritten):** On the outline, this is the destination act — the journey east, the disc authenticates, the record plays, the four POVs disagree about the archive. I'll address below whether the setup can carry the promise.

The midpoint. In a braid this wide, the midpoint should be the moment when the four threads understand they are one thread. The manuscript currently places that recognition around Chapters 15-16 (Davan and Maren meet properly, the cooperage knows about the disc). That timing is serviceable, but it leaves Chapters 17-22 as aftermath rather than second-half momentum. The feedback that reveals arrive too early by Chapter 15 — I agree with that note, with an adjustment I'll come to.

## The Four POVs

Let me take them one at a time because this is the book's most consequential structural question and you asked me to tell you which one is expendable.

**Maren** is the book's spine. Her voice is the most consistent and her arc has the clearest through-line. The "hollow → unblooded → test-not-keepsake" progression is the most satisfying single character arc across the manuscript. The Chapter 9 reframe (woodcutter's shelter) is load-bearing; the Chapter 20 "she had carried it for years thinking she was carrying grief. She was carrying a test." is the emotional payoff the book has been building toward. Keep her. Do not compress her. She is the most expensive POV per page — her chapters run longer than the others — and she earns every word.

**Davan** is your voice experiment and it is largely working. His observational register is distinct from the other three, and the bioluminescent-skin-as-second-narrator is a genuinely novel POV device. His relationship with Edvar in the v2 Chapter 19 is the most emotionally tender scene in the drafts — *the recognition of company after a very long time alone* is doing a lot of work with little noise. But: he is also the POV most at risk of redundancy, because what he discovers (the orbital count, the four-to-three transition, the material identity of face and container) is what Aldric discovers from the other direction. If I had to compress a POV, it would be Davan's middle — Chapters 12 and 15 could probably merge, and Chapter 6 (barge to Confluence with Lera) is luxuriant in a way that I would defend but another editor would question.

**Aldric** is the most fully realized character in the book. His voice is the most distinct, his interiority is the most textured, and his chapters have the highest density of moment-to-moment craft. Chapter 7 is a structural jewel — the lying-as-habit observation, the Crane encounter, the "I do not know what I am looking at" entry in the journal. Chapter 13 is more uneven; the Brennan-letter exposition sags in the middle (four pages of summary is a lot of summary), but the ending — Crane in his rooms, the pencil on the wrong side, the accumulation rather than confrontation — recovers it. The v2 Chapter 17 (Reth) is strong. Aldric is not expendable and should not be compressed. He is also, per the feedback's voice-blur concern, no longer blurring with Vane in my read — the revised register (formal clauses stumbling into liturgical vocabulary: *providence / wreckage / institution*) is audible in the v2 drafts and starting to reach back into the earlier chapters via revision. Keep going on that sharpening.

**Vane** is the POV that asks the hardest question. She is the most commercially interesting character in the book — the noir hunter with a secret biological cost, the professional-dissolving-inward arc, the Bloom-supply-chain irony — and she is also the thread where the writing is least surefooted. Her voice is good, sometimes excellent (Chapter 8's lapse in the trail, the compound attention, *"two absences. One in her pocket. One she was following north."* is one of the best single sentences in the manuscript). But her arc moves fast — she turns around on the road within 20 pages of joining the hunt, and her Chapter 22 liberation is signaled but unwritten. The v2 Chapter 18 is doing something ambitious with compression, and I want to be specific about whether it works: it works at the level of voice, but the decision to have Vane discover the source, turn around, and walk away without freeing the captive or even buying a wafer — all within one compressed chapter — asks the reader to do a lot of imputing. The scene in the underground room reads more like a report about a scene than the scene itself. I would argue for either expanding the confrontation (let Vane stand in that doorway for longer, let the bark-skin itch become a focus, let the woman on the pallet be a person rather than an anonymized *hollow harvested body*) or moving more of its content to Chapter 22 and letting Chapter 18 be about the *decision* rather than the *discovery*. Right now it is trying to do both and under-delivers both.

**Is any POV expendable?** Honestly, no. Four is the right number for what this book is trying to do — three would collapse the institutional-thread-plus-hunter-thread into an overloaded secondary, and two would lose the book's core conceptual move (four arcs converging on a four-ship mission; the structural mirror is too important). But Davan is the POV most vulnerable to a reader deciding to skim, and the highest-leverage craft move for the revision is to give his chapters a sharper per-scene stake. He currently observes more than he contends.

## Voice

Is the voice marketable? Yes, but narrowly.

The voice is distinctive. I want to be specific about what's distinctive: it's a voice that does not hurry, that treats perception as itself a form of narrative event, that earns emotional weight by compression rather than amplification, and that understands silence as load-bearing rather than absence. *"When she says 'All right' it means something has settled, not that she is fine"* — that's the whole book's ars poetica in one character note. A reader picking up two pages in a bookstore could recognize this as a specific writer's work, and that is what voice-marketability means. It is not a voice that will light up BookTok, but it is a voice that will earn back-of-book blurbs and reviews that refer to your prose rather than your plot.

The risk: the voice is so unified across the four POVs that in the early manuscript (before the v2 revisions started sharpening Aldric's liturgical register and leaving Vane's clipped-noir to carry itself) they blur. Vane and Aldric are the two that risked blurring — both analytical, both observational, both interior. The v2 drafts are addressing this. Keep pushing. One concrete move: Vane's sentences should be shorter on average than any other POV's, and her paragraphs should be shorter too. Aldric's should be longest. Right now Vane's paragraphs are often the same shape as Maren's. That is the easiest single lever for audible-voice separation.

One specific craft note worth flagging at this altitude: the manuscript is slightly over-fond of the construction *"not X, Y"* ("not unhappy, not the way of a man who has stopped asking"; "not frightened, stupid"; "not slow, not passive, but paced"). I counted a dozen in the first 500 pages. It is a good move once; it is a tic three times; it is a tell four times. Flag it for the line-edit pass.

## The Middle Problem

There is a middle problem, and I want to name it precisely because the earlier feedback named it imprecisely.

The feedback said reveals arrive too early by Ch. 15. I'd refine that. The *reveals* arrive on schedule. What arrives too early is *confirmation across POVs*. By Chapter 15 all four characters (and the reader) know: the moons were four and are three, the fourth came down, there is a crash site in the east, the bloodline's claim is circular, the disc is a key. Each character learned this from their own direction, which is good braided-POV work. What's weak is that the four threads *corroborate* rather than *complicate* one another. Every piece one POV brings fits cleanly into the emerging composite. Nothing contradicts. Nothing is wrong in a way that matters.

The highest-leverage fix is this: give one POV wrong information for an extended stretch, and make the reader know it's wrong. Candidates: Aldric believes Brennan's *Chrysalis came from the fourth ship* inversion (the outline says this is intentional and gets corrected in Act III — that is exactly the right move, and I'd make Aldric operate under that belief *longer*, let him carry it into his Chapter 21 arrival, so that a reader knowing the true cosmology watches Aldric trying to interpret the evidence through an inverted frame). Or: let Renner's cooperage group have a specific wrong model — not just "we don't know for sure, but we suspect" but an articulated theory with load-bearing errors that Maren's disc disproves in real time. The v2 Chapter 20 gestures at this (the disc pulses, the container does not) but Renner's capitulation happens too fast. Let her resist the evidence for longer. The most honorable model for a character like Renner is not grudging acceptance but *grief* — thirty years inside the institution that managed the forgetting, four years working the astronomical argument, and what she was right about is smaller than what she was wrong about. Make her pay for the revision.

Related: the cooperage scene in v2 Chapter 20 is the weakest of the v2 drafts. Too much of the scene is *characters having the argument in front of the protagonist* rather than *the protagonist causing the argument to reach a new state*. Maren puts the disc on the table at line 196. Up to that moment she is passive recipient. After that moment the scene ends. The entire mechanical function of Chapter 20 — establishing that Maren's unchanged hands are the test case the cooperage has been theorizing for years — is delivered in a narrated interior passage (*She had carried it for years thinking she was carrying grief. She was carrying a test*) rather than dramatized. This is the most important interior beat in the book, and it is currently being told rather than earned scene-by-scene. The scene's bones are sound; the scene's weight is landing on a paragraph of interior summary. Put that weight on Renner's face, or on Asha's correction to past tense, or on the disc's single visible pulse — you have the elements; trust them.

## The Reveal Sequence and Act III

On whether the book recovers tension for Act III after the reveals have landed by v2 Ch. 19-20: this is the question I was most worried about going in, and my answer is cautiously yes, but only if Act III is *not about discovery*. If the hill-arrival, the disc-activation, and the ship's records are the climax, the book will read flat because the reader has already made those connections. What must be the climax is the *Confluence containers* payoff — specifically, the choice the bible has locked: one message container, three biologically locked genetic archive containers, the Chrysalis incompatibility making the choice-to-open a choice-to-unmake-ourselves.

I think this is a genuinely great ending premise. It is the thematic payoff the braided-POV architecture has been building toward: each character's arc ends them in a different position on the species-level question, and their disagreement is not resolvable because it should not be resolvable. The reader earns the right to hold the disagreement by having lived inside all four POVs. That is what the four-POV structure is *for*.

The risk: the setup for that payoff is currently thin. Through Chapter 20 (v2), the four Confluence containers have been mentioned once (Chapter 3, "four smooth containers") and not returned to. The reader does not yet know there is anything inside them, does not know they are locked, does not have the ache-of-curiosity that makes the Act III reveal land. That is a fixable problem — the containers can be given a moment or two in Chapter 21 (Aldric's arrival, he brings them up) or Chapter 24 (Aldric tells the group about them) — but it is a problem. Right now the manuscript's Chekov's-gun inventory is dominated by the disc; the containers are listed in the bible as the thematic climax but not seeded as a plot object. Seed them. You don't need much — one scene of Aldric saying *there were four other objects, sealed, I did not open them* is enough. The reader will carry that.

**Can the ending land?** Yes, if you write it with the same discipline you wrote Chapters 9, 10, and 19. The promise of an ending where four POVs disagree over the archive, the closing image is the disagreement held, and the book refuses to resolve — that is an ambitious, literary, adult ending. This manuscript can write that ending. My worry is not craft but scale: Chapters 27-30 are going to have to do a lot of work (arrive at hill, activate disc, receive message, discover archive, process Aldric's institutional reckoning, confront Horst's agent, stage the four-way disagreement), and you have 4 chapters for it. If any of those chapters tries to do two of those beats, you will feel it. I would plan on Chapter 27 being *only* the arrival and the disc, Chapter 28 *only* Davan inside the hull, Chapter 29 *only* Aldric with the records and the follower's arrival, and Chapter 30 the confrontation and the argument. Respect the pacing you've established for the rest of the book. The ending wants to breathe.

- **Chapter 13's middle (Brennan's letter summary)** can compress by 30%. The reader does not need four pages summarizing eight pages. Two pages of letter, one page of Aldric's processing, and we're out.
- **The "not-quite-a-smile / corner of Vane's mouth" tic** — flagged in the bible already; cut all but one instance across Chs. 10 and 14.
- **"Careful" as overused word** — same, flagged.

## What I'd Add

- **One grotesque-but-celebrated Blooming scene in Chapter 1 or 2.** This is the single highest-leverage addition. The feedback flagged it; I agree; I'd put it in Chapter 2 (Davan's chapter) where it can be staged in a waypoint inn or a village ceremony he passes through, because a grotesque Blooming from Davan's POV does triple work (hollow stigma weight, Davan's emotional vocabulary established, the theological machinery made specific). Four pages would do it.
- **One seeding beat for the Confluence containers.** Aldric mentions them to himself or to Brennan somewhere in Ch. 17, with one precise detail that lets the reader carry the object forward. Even *"four containers, sealed, I left them there because I did not understand what opening them would do"* is enough.
- **One scene where Vane's professional tools fail her without the Bloom.** The feedback called for this; I agree. Somewhere between Chapter 4 and Chapter 11, Vane attempts to read a room and gets it wrong in a way that costs her. This makes her Chapter 18 decision cost more. Without it, the stopping reads as will-power rather than sacrifice.
- **One Maren chapter that sits inside her misreading of the disc's meaning longer.** The feedback — Maren should believe she is broken for another 20% of the book — I half-agree with. What I'd do is not delay her understanding that she is unblooded/the key; that's her Chapter 9 reframe and it works. What I'd delay is her understanding of the disc's *function* — let her believe through Chapter 14 or 15 that the disc is a memento, an inheritance, a thing her father loved, and let Chapter 20's *not a keepsake, a test* be an earned reversal rather than a summary of a conclusion she's been drifting toward.

## Where I Disagree With Prior Feedback

I read *moons_feedback.md* after forming the above, as the template instructs. Areas of agreement: reveals arrive too early in aggregate (though I frame it as corroboration-too-clean rather than discovery-too-fast); hollow stigma underweighted; harvesting irony needs to be made explicit in Vane's realization (and the v2 Chapter 18 does explicit this — it now works); filter words need a line pass; Crane should be a physical threat (the v2 Chapter 13 end, with the chair wrong and the pencil on the wrong side, is better than a physical search would be, actually — an earlier draft may have needed the physical escalation, but the *accumulation rather than confrontation* register is more sophisticated and I would keep it).

Areas of disagreement:

- The feedback says Vane's Bloom sacrifice is underearned. I agree the sacrifice is rushed, but the fix is not to show Vane failing without Bloom; the fix is to compress the Bloom-supply discovery scene differently (see Chapter 18 notes above) so the reader feels the cost at the moment of the decision. Showing a Bloom failure in Chapter 4 or 8 would flatten her arc; the professional-tools-breaking is the Chapter 18 scene's whole content, and that is where it should live.
- The feedback says make Renner a true believer in the Chrysalis. I'd go further: make the cooperage group *wrong about the direction of causation* in the same way Brennan is (the Chrysalis came from the fourth ship's people) and make Maren's disc disprove the inversion. That gives Act III Aldric's institutional arc and the cooperage arc a shared correction rather than parallel ones.
- The feedback says the disc should have a "low-battery / malfunction hum." I disagree. The disc is active from Chapter 1 forward, pulsing and warm; adding malfunction would be an out-of-tone register shift. The bible's resolution here — escalate in frequency, warmth, and visibility as the disc approaches the crash site — is the right call.

## The Path Forward

If you make three revisions, the book becomes substantially stronger and commercially viable as a literary-SFF debut:

1. **Seed the Confluence containers early and let them carry the Act III weight they're locked to carry.** This is the single highest-impact structural move. Without the seed, Act III is a hill-and-records climax; with the seed, it's an archive-and-choice climax, and the latter is the book you want.

2. **Refactor the middle (Chs. 15-16 cooperage / meeting arc and Chs. 19-20 v2 drafts) so that corroboration becomes complication.** Give one POV (Renner's group is easiest, Brennan is alternate) an inverted-causation error that the disc's behavior at Ch. 20 visibly disproves. Let Maren's arrival cause the group to be *wrong* in front of her, not just *joined by* her.

3. **Add the three scenes I named above** — grotesque Blooming in Ch. 1-2, Vane-without-Bloom failure somewhere in Ch. 4-11, and a Maren-still-misreading-the-disc beat somewhere in Ch. 12-15 — and you will have the stigma weight, the Bloom cost, and the Maren reversal that the current draft is asking the reader to take on faith.

A fourth, smaller move: tighten Davan's middle (Chapters 12 and 15 can probably compress together) and push Aldric's liturgical register further into the earlier chapters via revision, so the voice-blur concern is fully retired by Ch. 15.

The book is a good book. It is a strange, slow, adult book that has the courage to treat its science-fiction premise as a context rather than a twist and to commit to its characters' interiority with the patience of the literary novel it partly is. What it needs is not a ground-up rethink — the architecture is sound, the voice is there, the ending is correctly conceived. What it needs is the revision pass that trusts its own structure enough to let complication replace repetition in the middle, and that seeds the Act III payoff with the same specificity as the Act I question.

Write the ending. Then come back through for the middle. The middle is easier to fix when you know exactly what it has to set up.

Warmly,
The Editor
