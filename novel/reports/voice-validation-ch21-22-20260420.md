## Chapter 21 (Aldric)

### Liturgical register

**Anchor count: 3 distinct moments. None saturating.**

1. *Providence/procedure* (temple district exit, p. 3): "*Providence,* he
   thought, and the word arrived in his mouth with its old liturgical
   weight — and beneath the weight, the word that belonged here instead:
   *procedure.*" This is exactly the two-languages-in-conflict the bible
   specifies — the ecclesiastical word arriving first, the scholarly
   correction underneath it. One of the strongest executions of Aldric's
   signature in the full manuscript run.

2. *Wreckage/congregation/commercial community* (docks district, p. 4):
   "*Wreckage,* he thought, because the word arrived and he let it. The
   church would call the district a *congregation.* The scholar in him
   would call it a *commercial community.* Between the two, the word he
   was actually using was *wreckage.*" Three-way word-conflict in a
   single passage. Slightly more explicit than the coin-circle moment in
   Ch. 17, but the exposition earns it: Aldric is consciously naming what
   the words are doing, which is itself characterization.

3. *Sanctus* (basement, final section): "the way he had been trained to
   hold his breath in chapel at the moment of the sanctus." Liturgical
   vocabulary as procedural muscle memory, not reflection. The restraint
   of one term here, after the two extended passages above, is correctly
   calibrated — Aldric is not delivering a sermon, he is caught in an
   involuntary reflex.

**Assessment:** The bible's "1-2 anchors per scene" standard is met
and occasionally exceeded. The docks district passage is the longest of
the three; it works because the three-way vocabulary conflict is Aldric's
signature move, not narration stepping in to explain. The chapter does
not reach self-parody. The two-languages-in-conflict is audible throughout
without becoming a tic.

**One miss:** The Keeper's House scene — Yelin, the filing, Crane's
advance agent — has no liturgical register at all. Aldric processes a
genuine threat to his safety in purely operational terms. This is the
scene most at risk of Vane-register blur (see Adversarial Read below).
A single ecclesiastical word in his processing of the Keeper-who-was-
not-him would anchor the scene without changing what is said.

### Rhythm signature

**Long careful clauses → stripped simple sentences: strongly present.**

Multiple correct deployments:

- "He did not say anything." (after descending the stairs)
- "He sat." (Renner's command)
- "He had arrived." (end of the basement inventory)
- "He looked at it. / He did not reach for it." (disc moment, two
  sentences separated by a longer paragraph)
- The lamp-tic paragraph builds across four sentences and lands on
  "and he closed his fingers on the empty space and let them fall" —
  the simplest sentence doing the most work.

The closing sequence (final two pages) is the chapter's best sustained
rhythm demonstration: the extended interior clause about what the hand
was doing is followed by "He let the hand stay. He did not force it
forward. / He did not force it back." Three short declaratives, each
a breath.

**One drift:** The sentence "He had ridden west for four days with this
count as his companion, and the count had not settled" (moons, p. 1)
uses "companion" and the image of the lintel — both extended metaphors
back-to-back in the same paragraph. The paragraph is one of the chapter's
weaker rhythm moments; it describes more than it enacts.

### Self-awareness-about-lying quality

**Present and working.**

Two distinct scenes:

1. Yelin exchange (temple district): "He said it more quickly than he
   meant to and had to do the small repair-work of seeming unconcerned."
   The narrative notes the lie's failure condition (the speed), the
   attempted repair, and then: "The mistake had already done its work
   and could not be undone." Aldric does not fool himself that he has
   recovered; he notes the cost precisely while still in the performance
   of normality. This is correct.

2. Basement account (final section): "He did not say everything. He did
   not speak of Crane — not yet. He did not describe the Keeper who was
   not him... He did not say the word *providence* as he had said it to
   himself on the road, though he thought it once, near the end of his
   account, and pressed it down." The inventory of what he did NOT say
   is narrated alongside what he did say. The self-consciousness is
   complete: he is aware of the omissions while making them.

**Assessment: Green. The self-aware-about-lying quality is the chapter's
most fully delivered voice element.**

### Filter words

**Count: 3 instances. All low-grade or in-voice.**

1. p. 2: "He was aware, with the stripped clarity that had been following
   him since Greenford, that he had just crossed a threshold..." — "He
   was aware" is a classic Aldric filter form flagged repeatedly in the
   prose audit (Chs. 3, 7). This is the most mechanically identical repeat
   of his worst filter pattern. Suggested fix: "He had crossed a threshold
   he would not be able to re-cross. The shop was a room he had entered
   once in the simple way and would never enter the same way again."

2. p. 5: "Aldric catalogued the shift without mentioning it" (Davan's
   skin-light) — "catalogued" is Maren's and Vane's signature verb, not
   Aldric's. The word choice is a minor cross-contamination. Suggested
   fix: "Aldric read the shift and did not name it."

3. p. 6: "a quality made of decades of the same materials in the same
   small space" (shop smell, p. 2) — borderline; sensory in-voice
   description rather than filter. Low priority. Keep.

**Ch. 21 filter count: 1 firm ("he was aware"), 1 verb-contamination
("catalogued"), 1 borderline/keep.**

This is significantly cleaner than the Chs. 7 and 13 Aldric chapters
(6 and 5 instances respectively). The "became aware" tic is present once
but not repeated. Net: **clean enough; one fix warranted**.

### Tics

**Non-smile-mouth:** 1 instance.

> "Marrick's mouth moved, not into a smile — Aldric had thirty years of
> reading faces in chapels, and he knew the difference between expression
> and arrangement — but into the settled arrangement of a man who had
> found a negotiating partner and had stopped expecting to."

The "not a smile" construction is there, but with a meaningful structural
difference from the flagged tic: the clause does NOT use the "might have
been [X]. Might have been [Y]." epistemic-uncertainty syntax that the
prose audit identifies as the tic's specific grammar. Instead it uses
a long qualifying clause ("Aldric had thirty years of reading faces in
chapels...") that is itself an Aldric voice beat, and the landing is a
positive characterization rather than an ambiguous hedge. The phrase-form
is recognizably adjacent to the tic but not the tic's most problematic
variant.

However: the prose audit counts 7 confirmed mouth instances across the
full manuscript. Ch. 21 adds one more, on a character (Marrick) who has
not had one before. The drafter should note this is the eighth use.
Given the overall manuscript instruction to reduce these, this is a
yellow flag worth resolving in revision — find something other than the
mouth to show Marrick's settled satisfaction.

---

**"The particular [noun]":** 3 instances.

1. "the specific attentiveness of a man who had just been at his
   worktable" (p. 5) — uses "specific" not "particular"; same template,
   different word. The variation is marginal; this still counts.

2. "the specific chilly dampness of a cellar" (basement, p. 6) —
   again "specific." Two uses of "specific [noun]" in the same chapter
   is the "particular" template under a different word.

3. "the specific careful cadence of an educated man who was trying to
   sound ordinary" (Marrick reading Aldric, p. 5) — third use.

The prose audit documents 25+ instances of this template across Chs.
1-16 and calls it the most pervasive prose habit in the manuscript. Ch.
21 adds three more ("specific" rather than "particular," but identical
construction). The drafter has avoided "the particular" by name while
reproducing the template. The disguise does not address the underlying
habit. **Yellow flag: revise one of the three, vary the second, keep
the third.**

**"Had the quality of":** 3 instances.

1. "the specific attentiveness of a man who had just been at his
   worktable" (p. 5) — the "of a man who [past participle]" construction
   the prose audit inventories as 15+ across Chs. 1-16.

2. "the quick uninterested kindness of someone who had seen every kind
   of guest pass through" (landlord, p. 4) — same construction, secondary
   character.

3. "the bearing of someone who had run rooms" (Renner, p. 6) — same.
   The prose audit notes this is the author's preferred mode for rapid

   (lower than Ch. 4 Vane's four). Not urgent, but consistent with the
   flagged habit.

### POV discipline

**The four-POV meeting: Clean.**

The basement scene is the main risk zone. Aldric describes Davan's
skin-light, Maren's composure, Renner's framework, Asha, Corvel, Pip,
the young cartographer — all from observation and inference, not from
interior access to any other character's thoughts.

Key checks:

- Davan's skin-light is read as external observation: "Aldric read it
  without yet understanding that he was reading it." The limitation is
  built in. Aldric doesn't know what it means; he only notes it. POV-clean.

- Maren: "Her face did nothing he could read." Correct Aldric POV —
  he names his reading limit.

- "Her face did, however, register him completely — the look of a person
  whose first act in any room was to count the exits..." This is Aldric
  inferring Maren's mode from her behavior, not entering her interiority.
  It reads as a highly competent inference from a 30-year face-reader.
  On the line; defensible.

- The green-gold skin-light passage: "Aldric did not have a name for
  [it]. Not amber. Not blue. Something between them and also beside them,
  a quiet warm green-gold that held steady rather than pulsing... The young
  man was showing his recognition of what Aldric was describing in the only
  way his body had for recognition. A second narrator. A skin that said
  what the body underneath it had already known." — This last sentence
  ("A second narrator") is the narrator stepping in to gloss the reader,
  not Aldric's thought. It is voice-neutral — no character is "thinking"
  "second narrator." But it is the chapter's one moment of near-omniscient
  narrator intrusion at a high-stakes scene. The phrase should be cut;
  the observation without the gloss is stronger.

**POV discipline verdict: Pass with one cut recommended ("A second
narrator. A skin that said what the body underneath it had already known."
→ stop after "held steady rather than pulsing").**

## Chapter 22 (Vane)

### Compression / short-paragraph discipline

**Strongly honored throughout.**

The chapter opens with an inventory in italic list form — "*Six days.
Three narrowing arcs. One-shift coat. Two-handler lodging sweep. One
outer cordon.*" — which is the Ch. 18 cost-benefit list structure applied
to threat-assessment. This is Vane's professional cognitive mode as
chapter architecture: the form is the content.

Paragraph lengths throughout: tight. Most under five lines. Several
single-sentence paragraphs. The action sequence (Lintel Street entry,
Deren confrontation, freeing the woman) uses clipped declaratives with
minimal subordination: "He was not trained. He stiffened and then
loosened, which was what untrained people did." "She crossed the room.
She did not speak yet."

The withdrawal reporting: "Nausea. Tremor. Hearing's off." This is
Ch. 18 compression applied to a new context. The form is consistent.

**One expansion to flag:** The closing page goes longer. The final
four paragraphs shift from the chapter's usual compression into a more
ruminative, less clipped register — particularly "The three moons were
over the harbor outside, at their stations, doing what they had always
done, which — she did not know yet and would not know until the hill..."
This is the chapter's longest sustained sentence. It expands outside
Vane's clipped mode into something closer to omniscient or lyrical
narration. The information is correct (the orbital-station framing), but
it arrives in narrator voice, not Vane's voice. The sentence is doing
expository work the Vane register cannot do — Vane does not yet know
the orbital-station truth, and the narrator is whispering it over her
shoulder. **This is both a compression failure and a POV boundary
question. The sentence should be cut or rewritten in Vane's actual
ignorance: she does not know what they are doing; she can observe that
they are there.**

### Interiority-as-professional-analysis

**The model from Ch. 18 is honored and extended.**

Key passages where feeling arrives through the professional form:

1. The Horst inventory (opening): the emotional content (they are running
   out of time) is entirely contained in the professional threat-read.
   No stated anxiety. Just the pattern-recognition.

2. The bark-skin observation on the walk home: "*Data,* she thought.
   *Just data. Put it in the log and keep moving.* / It was not moving
   her the way observations usually moved her. It was resisting filing.
   She wanted to feel it as absolution, and the wanting was what made her
   suspect it; she wanted to feel it as cost, and the cost was not yet
   legible." — The professional category ("data," "filing") is applied
   to a physical change that carries enormous emotional weight, and the
   form's failure to contain the weight IS the emotional disclosure.
   This is exactly the Ch. 18 model: the tools breaking against what
   they cannot hold.

3. Withdrawal self-diagnosis: "Hour four. Withdrawal begins with a
   forward edge. Nausea. Tremor. Sensory misallocation. Standard
   presentation." Then: "The cataloguing was itself a reduced version
   of the thing she used to do with her cataloguing — not the clean
   analytical filing that had once served her, but the effortful
   pretense of it." This is the form commenting on the form's own
   degradation. Strong execution.

4. Davan's hand on the blanket: "the fact that he had not required
   anything of her for putting it there was one of the things she filed
   without commentary, because he had now done several things she would
   have filed without commentary if she had been keeping a file on him,
   and it was becoming clear that she was." The emotional content
   (growing attachment) is rendered entirely through the professional
   meta-commentary on her own filing behavior. Correct.

5. Maren's "All right": "Something had settled." Two words receiving
   the same treatment as in Ch. 18. Consistent.

**One slight miss:** "She thought about the woman in the square, the
too-large coat, the cousin south along the coast road, the coin a mother
had sewn into a shoe against an unreachable future..." This passage runs
four sentences before returning to analysis. It is the most emotionally
direct passage in the chapter — closer to Maren's register (cataloguing
objects with feeling-weight) than Vane's (professional categories
containing suppressed feeling). Not a failure, but the softest moment
in the chapter's voice discipline.

### Cognitive-verb variation

**"she catalogued" / "she inventoried" / "she sorted":** 4 uses.

The Ch. 18 line report flagged three uses of "catalogued" in one
chapter as the beginning of a tic, and the v2 voice validation confirmed
"she sorted" replaced one instance. Ch. 22 uses the verb family as
follows:

- "Vane filed the trust and did not let it move her" (p. 2) — "filed"
- "She noted the cover and took it" (p. 3) — "noted"
- "She catalogued it. The cataloguing was itself a reduced version of
  the thing she used to do with her cataloguing" (withdrawal, p. 9) —
  "catalogued" × 2 in close succession; but the doubling here is
  intentional — the second use is meta-commentary on the first,
  which is the chapter's thematic argument. This is Ch. 18's
  "she'd always been good at cataloguing" replaced with a version
  that earns the repetition by naming what the repetition costs.
- "she filed without commentary" (Davan's hand, p. 11) — "filed" again.

**Tally by verb:** "filed" × 2, "noted" × 1, "catalogued" × 2
(one meta), "sorted" × 0. No single verb repeats beyond 2 in the
chapter, and the meta-use of "catalogued" earns its echo. Variation
is adequate. The "filed" repetition across pages 2 and 11 is below
threshold.

**Assessment: Cognitive-verb variation is clean. The Ch. 18 three-
instance same-verb problem is not reproduced.**

### Professional cost-benefit scene

**The Ch. 18 precedent is honored.**

The Lintel Street surveillance section (pp. 3-5) is the chapter's
closest analog to Ch. 18's cost-benefit ledger. It is organized as a
pattern-read with professional notation: the runner's schedule, the
timing gaps, the inference about morning staffing. The emotional content
(she has already decided she is going back; the surveillance is
professional confirmation of what was already decided) leaks through
the form without being stated.

The chapter makes one upgrade on Ch. 18's precedent: where Ch. 18 had
Vane's cost-benefit ledger fail at its final entry (the weight of stopping
overwhelms the ledger's symmetry), Ch. 22 has the cataloguing-as-form
degrade under withdrawal — "the effortful pretense of it, the form of
filing she had learned so well it continued to function even as the
function underneath it failed." The escalation from "tools breaking" (Ch.
18) to "tools pretending to work while breaking" (Ch. 22) is a clear
and correct progression.

## Adversarial read: Aldric vs. Vane

**Verdict: Clear margin. Better than the Ch. 17-18 adjacency.**

Stripped of chapter headers, the two chapters are distinguishable by
every available axis:

**Syntax:** Aldric builds long subordinated clauses with nested qualifications.
The sentence "He had read the sealed chamber in the undercroft. He had
looked into the face and counted four. He had ridden a thousand miles of
road with the word *providence* turning in his chest into the word *wreckage.*"
uses anaphora and the long middle clause, ending in a two-stage word-
transformation that requires the setup. Vane's sentences stop. "The tin
was still in her pack." "Her hands shook at the fingertips when she laced
the boots. Fine tremor. She worked around it."

**Diction:** Aldric: "sanctus," "pastoral," "providence," "wreckage,"
"congregation," "lintel," "threshold," "undercroft." Vane: "arcs,"
"overlap," "cordon," "runner," "production pattern," "withdrawal,"
"misallocation." Aldric uses ecclesiastical and architectural vocabulary.
Vane uses operational and supply-chain vocabulary. The word "procedure"
appears in both chapters — in Aldric's it is the word that replaces
"providence"; in Vane's it would simply be "procedure." The semantic
load each carries in their respective registers is different.

**Interiority mode:** Aldric's self-awareness is narrated in complete
sentences, nested within the scene, with internal qualification ("he
understood... with the specific awkward clarity of a man watching a habit
misfire into an unintended grace"). Vane's interiority arrives in italics,
short phrases, and the professional-category vocabulary applied to her
own state: "*Data. Just data. Put it in the log.*"

**Structural pattern:** Aldric's chapter builds toward the span between
his hand and the disc — the hesitation rendered as speech. Vane's chapter
is organized as logistics: inventory, execute, debrief, sleep. The
emotional content of both is enormous; the shape it takes is opposite.

**The Yelin scene:** The one blur risk in Ch. 21 is the Crane/Keeper
scene in the Keeper's House — Aldric processing a professional threat
in tactical terms with no liturgical register (flagged above). If this
scene is placed next to any Vane action sequence and stripped of proper
nouns, the distinction is narrower than elsewhere. The addition of one
ecclesiastical word in that passage would eliminate the risk.

**Verdict: Clear margin maintained. The liturgical additions in Ch. 21
do the same work the v2 Ch. 17 additions did — they anchor Aldric in
a vocabulary Vane cannot produce. The ch. 21 Yelin scene is the one
passage to watch.**

## Verdict

**Chapter 21 (Aldric): Yellow-green.** The voice is substantially on-
target. The two-languages-in-conflict arrives at exactly the right
density (three anchor points, none saturating). The self-aware-about-
lying quality is the chapter's strongest element. The rhythm signature
is present and well-deployed in the final sequence. Yellow flags: one
firm filter ("he was aware"), one verb contamination ("catalogued"), one
non-smile-mouth tic (Marrick — the eighth manuscript instance, warranting
revision), and the "A second narrator" gloss in the basement scene (cut
recommended — POV boundary). The "specific [noun]" template appears three
times disguised as "specific" rather than "particular" — the prose habit
is present under camouflage. None of these are structural problems; all
are line-level. The chapter earns a green light pending one pass of
targeted cuts.

**Chapter 22 (Vane): Green, with one red-line item.** The compression
is honored throughout the chapter body and the interiority-as-professional-
analysis model from Ch. 18 is not only maintained but formally extended
(cataloguing degrading under withdrawal). Filter count is the lowest
action-heavy chapter in the run. Cognitive-verb variation is clean.
The "Junie" naming scene is the chapter's best moment — it arrives
entirely through action and does not explain itself. **The red-line
item is the penultimate paragraph:** "The three moons were over the
harbor outside, at their stations, doing what they had always done,
which — she did not know yet and would not know until the hill — was
not waiting to come home but holding the line they had been told to hold
ten thousand years ago..." This is the narrator speaking over Vane's
shoulder with information Vane does not have. It breaks POV, breaks
compression, and breaks the chapter's voice on its last page. Cut the
appositive clause and return to what Vane actually knows: she does not
see them; she is under a roof; the moons are where they have always been,
which she has no framework yet to interpret. Everything after "which —"
should go.
