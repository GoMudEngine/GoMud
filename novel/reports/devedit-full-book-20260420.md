# Editorial Letter II — What the Moons Keep (Full Draft)

Dear Author,

I've read the whole thing now — prologue, the revised Chs. 1–16, the v2 drafts of 17–20, and the drafts of 21 through 30. I ignored the v1 drafts for 17–20 per instruction and read only the v2s. I did this cold in narrative order, stopping at the end of the book and sitting with the ending for an afternoon before coming back to write this. Two things I want to say at the top, because they should not get lost in what follows: you wrote the ending the book wanted, and you did not cheat. I was worried you would cheat. The genre-facing conclusion was visible in your bible for months — four POVs, four positions, no resolution, the moons over the eastern hills — and there are a hundred ways to blow that landing. You did not blow it. What is in front of me now is a finished literary science-fantasy novel of substantial craft, and my job this pass is a narrower one than it was on the first: to tell you whether the sentence-by-sentence work you now have to do is sentence-by-sentence work, or whether there are still structural issues that need addressing before you query.

## The Book That Now Exists

This is a novel about what you do inside a story that has become the wrong shape for the world — the same book the first letter described, now delivered. The four-POV braid has landed as braid rather than as parallel tracks: Maren carries the disc across its full arc from coal-pouch deception to thumb-on-the-recess; Aldric crosses from Keeper of a legible doctrine to reader of a genetic-archive footnote; Vane empties her own tin over five chapters and dismantles the supply chain that has been making her the body she is; Davan arrives, across his long slow circling, at a new register of color his skin has been reaching for since his change began. The four threads meet in the cooperage basement at Ch. 20, ride the same road east, kneel at the same hill, and disagree in the closing image. The book's thesis — *the map is useful but wrong, the question is whether it's wrong enough to send you off a cliff* — has been converted from thesis to experience: the map has been proved wrong in a specific way the reader has watched each character discover from their own direction, and the cliff-question arrives at the end as an archive-lock the story explicitly refuses to resolve.

What has changed from my first read is that I was, then, cautiously optimistic about the ending on the theory of the ending. I am now persuaded by the execution of it. Ch. 27 delivers the activation with the discipline I asked for (one beat per chapter, no compression). Ch. 28 renders Davan's skin-light at a new register in the exact language the bible locked, and the "honey held up to a lamp, only cooler / edge of dawn a morning nobody had seen" formulation pays off in Ch. 30 as promised. Ch. 29 does the record-reading scene in a way I thought would defeat you and did not: Aldric is reading in scholarly movement, one object at a time, and each realization is staged as a separate internal event rather than a cascade. And Ch. 30 makes its four-POV structure work by keeping each section short, each position distinct, and the closing image's grammar continuative rather than terminal. "She kept walking." The right sentence. The right tense.

## Did the Revisions Land?

Three asks from the first letter. I want to grade each with precision.

**1. Seed the Confluence containers earlier (Chs. 3 / 7 / 13).** Partially. The containers are seeded in Ch. 3 (Aldric's first entry on them — "five objects recovered from sealed chamber"; his attempts to pry one open; his notation that "the containers did not object to his hands and did not recognize them either") and the four-smooth-containers detail is there for the reader to carry. By Ch. 7 and Ch. 13 they are present but handled lightly — mentioned as part of the sealed chamber inventory but not foregrounded as their own Chekhov's gun. The effect is that the reader retains them in peripheral memory without being clubbed over the head with them, which is the correct register for this book's voice. When Ch. 29 arrives at "Hold IV" and the genetic archive, the reader is not surprised by the existence of the containers — they have been carrying the word *container* since Ch. 3 — but they are surprised by what is inside them, which is the correct order of surprise. I judge this landed. If I were pressing further I would add one more beat in Ch. 13 where Aldric, riding east, thinks about the containers specifically — one sentence in which he wonders what is inside them, with the phrase "I left them sealed because I did not understand what opening them would do" or its cousin. That single beat would make the Ch. 29 payoff feel even more earned. It is optional. The structure holds without it.

**2. Corroboration-into-complication in Chs. 13–20 (invert a cooperage thesis at the Ch. 20 pivot).** Fully. The v2 Ch. 20 does the inversion I asked for, and does it cleanly. Renner's thesis, as delivered in Ch. 15, is *the key is the trait — any unchanged hands would answer.* The v2 Ch. 20 disproves this precisely: the disc pulses specifically on Maren's placement, and does not have pulsed in fourteen-years of Corvel's container being handled by Chrysalis-touched hands in the same room. The prose carries the refutation without announcing it: "The disc had not answered the room. It had answered the person. Her face did not fall — it rearranged, slowly, in the small public way of someone who would do her grieving later." That is a sentence that earns its weight. Renner's capitulation-into-grief is now the book's emotional peak of Act II, not the info-dump I worried it would remain. Asha's "Hasn't existed" correction carries through cleanly, and the three Ch. 21 reconstruction — "a voice that had been aggressive-precise until recently, and had been carrying its new humility in a way that made the old precision stand out by contrast" — extends the refutation into the following chapter's texture. This fix was the single highest-impact ask in my first letter, and you executed it. The middle no longer sags.

**3. Three load-bearing scenes.** Evaluating each:

*The grotesque-but-celebrated Blooming (Ch. 1 / 2).* Landed in Ch. 1, not Ch. 2. Hett Orval's opening at the Autumnal Rite — "the capillaries were visible for a moment and then the skin thickened over them again, and then thinned, and then thickened" — does exactly the work I asked for. The community's "held note the older ones knew by heart" and the mother's weeping-while-smiling transform what was a quiet stigma-by-overhearing into a body-horror beat with social machinery visibly at work. Maren's interior — "she had seen it from the outside — a body being opened against its own clear preference, and the ring singing the opening on" — is the sentence that makes the hollow/unblooded stigma load-bearing from chapter one. This was the single most important addition and it is the correct addition. Do not cut or soften.

*Vane failing without Bloom (Ch. 4–11).* Landed in Ch. 8, in the best possible form. The six-minute trail-lapse after the involuntary vision is *precisely* the professional-tools-breaking scene I asked for, and it lands without melodrama: "On Bloom she would have had the three layers at once — road, girl, weather — and could have walked the trail blind. Off it, the layers did not cooperate." This is Vane cataloguing her own diminishment in her professional register, which is the register the bible (rightly) locks as her voice. By the time she empties her tin in Ch. 22 and the withdrawal-symptom catalog begins in Ch. 26, the reader has the Ch. 8 memory of what Bloom *did* for her, and the cost of stopping is therefore on the page. This ask landed cleanly.

*Maren still misreading the disc (Ch. 12–15).* Landed in Ch. 14, in what I think is the book's most underrated paragraph of interior work. The "disc-as-grave-marker" misreading — "she had also been holding it the way you held a marker for a grave you had not been allowed to stand at… *The disc will open it* — open the ground, open the hill's silence, give her back the body no one had returned" — is a fully articulated wrong reading that the reader can feel is wrong (because the reader has seen the disc act on much more than grief) while Maren holds to it earnestly. The Ch. 20 reframe ("She had been carrying grief. She was carrying a test") therefore lands as earned reversal rather than as summary. And then, as a gift, Ch. 27 comes back and refutes the Ch. 20 reframe itself: "The hill taught her what she had not had the words for, which was that she had been carrying both, and both had been true, and the reframe had not been an exchange. It had been an addition." Two reframes is better than one. The grave-marker misreading was the right seed.

All three load-bearing scenes delivered. The middle-sag concern is retired.

## The Ending

Ch. 27 through 30. I want to take them in order.

*Ch. 27, the hill.* The activation lands. You did the thing I was most worried about, which was giving the ending's first beat — disc-into-recess — its own chapter, and you put the father's remains scene inside it without making the chapter about the father. That was the risk: a dead father discovered five paces from the lock he walked ten years to reach is a melodrama trap. You avoided it by giving Maren the interior sentence that does all the thematic work ("The hill taught her what she had not had the words for… It had been an addition") and then having her *not weep.* "The weeping was a country she could not afford today." This is correct for Maren and correct for the book. The activation itself — the disc's hum stopping at seating, the bone-sound beginning, the air changing from morning-cool to interior-cool, the four arcs beginning faintly to glow through the overgrowth — is paced at two lines per sense-beat, which is the right pace. Maren's thumb on the symbol, not removed. Chapter ends on "The hill answered under her. / She held on." That is how you close Act III's first act.

*Ch. 29, Aldric reading.* This is the chapter I thought would break the book. A priest-scholar walks into a room full of records, reads them, and understands the whole history of Gaius — it is the textbook setup for an info-dump, the kind of ending that genre SF books routinely ruin themselves on. You did not ruin it. You did it in movement: Aldric picks up one object, then another, then a third, then a fourth. Each object teaches him one specific thing. The manifest teaches him "three for station, one for descent." The ship diagram teaches him that the containers under his temple were from Hold IV. The *native* study teaches him the technical register: *native-resistant baseline*, against the theology's *chosen*. The archive-document teaches him the bio-lock. The voice from the voiced wall teaches him that the office was staffed by a man, among other men. Each of these is dramatized as a body-event — the knees-crossing, the hand-stopping-short-of-the-lamp-cord tic, the "tenderness" register that arrives instead of anger — and the cumulative effect is not of being lectured but of watching a man at a library desk have the floor of his life re-composed one document at a time. The sentence "The temple had been built over a debris-field. The altar had been laid over the bloodline's immunity. The Rite he had administered for thirty years had celebrated, as a gift, a biological event that had killed most of the colonists and had changed the rest" is the book's intellectual climax, and it lands because you have earned the reader's trust that Aldric would arrive there as a man, not as exposition.

*Ch. 30, the four-POV structure.* This chapter's structural risk is the inverse of Ch. 29's: Ch. 29 risks info-dump, Ch. 30 risks fragmentation. A four-section chapter in which the book structurally echoes the prologue's eight-account First Coming can easily feel gimmicky — an author saying *look, I have closed my form*, rather than an author closing her form. It does not feel gimmicky here. It works for one specific reason: each of the four POV sections is *shorter than it has any right to be*, and each does exactly one piece of load-bearing work without trying to do two. Vane's section is an operational hunting scene ending with the agent bound in the grass and a threat that is, explicitly, not revenge but *contract* ("If Horst's men come over this ridge in the next month, I find you first"). Davan's section is a private *no* to the archive rendered as a room-ritual ("I am not going to ask you to be opened") with no dialogue. Aldric's section is the Ch. 25 arc-beat delivered in its final form — "the next Keeper is not a future me. The next Keeper is me, now" — without underlining. Maren's section is the captain's voice, the moons-look, the memory-of-her-father-in-the-courtyard, the Tidemark-register, and the disagreement, in that order. The order is the correct order. "She kept walking" is the right final sentence because it is *continuative* — you end on an aspect of verb, not a period of event — and because it arcs back through Chs. 5, 9, 16, 23, 27 where Maren walks through her own uncertainty. I asked in the first letter whether the book could earn the four-POV closing structure. It does. Do not reconsider it.

*The activation at Ch. 27, emotionally.* Yes. The disc-hum-stopping is a brilliant reversal — the reader has been tracking the escalation-of-activity for twenty chapters, and you give the climax as *silence.* "For the first time since she had taken it out of her coat three years ago and held it for the first time with a hand that knew what it was, the disc was *quiet* in her hand." That is the book's best single sentence, and it is also the one that best demonstrates your voice's patience — a lesser writer would have made the climax a blaze of light and sound, and you made it a stopping of a hum Maren has carried for three years. This is the whole book in a moment.

*Aldric's Ch. 29 reading.* Handled above. No info-dump. The "tenderness" register instead of anger is the correct register for this character, and it is what makes Aldric the book's most fully realized figure, now even more clearly than at Ch. 20.

*The four-POV disagreement.* Holds. Does not get dominated by one position. Each of the four positions — Vane's leave-as-found, Aldric's build-the-table, Davan's never-open, Maren's open-eventually-for-company — emerges from that character's journey, and none of them gets more screen-time or authorial sympathy than the others. I will say below why I think Maren's position works.

*The "old patient grief" thesis.* Landed at the right moment, in the right words. The Tidemark harbor-master's phrase appears in Ch. 30 not as a quotation but as a register — "Grief that did not belong to her personally. / Grief that was hers anyway. / She let it be hers." — surfacing through a "clerk-compiled manuscript she had read at eleven without remembering." That sourcing trick is a beautiful small move: the phrase's origin in the prologue's fourth fragment has been ambient reader-knowledge for 130,000 words, and now it arrives in Maren's body as something she read at eleven and has been carrying unlocated. This is how you close a thematic loop without ringing a bell.

*"She kept walking" as the final sentence.* Correct. The aspectual choice is doing work the reader does not consciously register. She walked in Ch. 5 (Betta's farm). She walked in Ch. 9 (woodcutter's shelter). She walked in Ch. 16 (cooperage yard). She walked east in Chs. 23–26. The book's entire structural grammar has been Maren-walking-toward-something. To end on "She kept walking" is to refuse the terminal event the form would ordinarily demand. It is also correct for the book's central claim: the archive is not opened, the disagreement is held, the work continues. Don't change it.

*The agent's non-violent release.* Correct register. Killing the agent would have done two wrong things — it would have given Vane a closure her arc is explicitly refusing (her arc is *not the war, the first moment of reckoning*), and it would have granted the book a finality the book is structured to refuse. The falsified-report device ("signature did not register") is in addition a clean narrative engine: it buys the group a specific window of time the sequel or the reader's imagination can work within. The "contract" framing — Vane making a professional threat to a professional peer — is also correct for her register. This choice does not undercut the stakes. It establishes that the stakes are larger and longer than one killing.

## Voice — Final Verdict

The voice has held across 132,000 words. I want to be specific about where, and where not, and what to do about it.

*Maren.* Her voice is the book's single greatest consistency. The compressed watchfulness, the "All right" register, the cataloguing of rooms before people — it is stable from Ashwick to the hill, and it *deepens* rather than calcifies. The Ch. 27 beat at the father's remains ("She did not say anything. / She did not weep. The weeping was a country she could not afford today") is the same voice as Ch. 1's coal-pouch deception, but with four hundred miles and a disc under her thumb in it. This is voice as character-growth-made-audible, which is the hardest thing prose can do.

*Aldric.* The voice I was most worried about after the first read — where the Ch. 7 and Ch. 13 registers read as "thoughtful scholar" rather than "Keeper" — has been sharpened in the v2 Ch. 17 and onward, and, as far as I can tell from what I reread in Chs. 3 and 7, is now audible throughout. The liturgical-vocabulary-stumbling-in-where-scholarly-belongs pattern ("Providence / procedure / wreckage") is working. Ch. 21's walk through the docks with *wreckage* arriving as the correct word, Ch. 29's "oh" after reading the third object — these are the voice I asked for. Aldric is now the book's most richly interior character; he was almost that before; he is that now. You have retired the Aldric/Vane voice-blur concern from the first letter. Do not relax on it; line-editing will need to watch for drift in the early Chs. 3/7/13 where the revisions are most recent.

*Vane.* The clipped-noir register I called her greatest sentence-level asset has held, and the prose-audit note you received mid-project — that her interiority is *presented as professional analysis even when it is feeling* — has been internalized. Ch. 18 v2's cost/benefit ledger ("Cost of continued use: the body on the pallet. Not abstractly… Concretely") is the most articulate version of this register I have seen in the book. The withdrawal chapters (22, 26) sustain it without melodrama — her shaking hands, the "data, just data" cataloguing, the "not going, retrograding" self-correction — all in voice. The one place the voice frays is Ch. 30's section where the agent-capture is staged; the Threshing Yard backstory and the Jax-cohort reference arrive fast and could feel like backstory-in-a-hurry. I would leave them. The pace matches what Vane's body is doing (withdrawal, vibration-at-the-clavicle, adrenaline), and the register earns the fast reference.

*Davan.* His voice is the most improved across the book. In my first read he was observing more than contending, and by Ch. 28 he is contending — the *no-to-the-opening* he says to the room in Ch. 30 is a decision made in his body, articulated at the register of skin-light and held-color-between-body-and-wall, and it is wholly his. The skin-as-second-narrator device has paid off across 130,000 words: the green-gold at Edvar's face in Ch. 19, the green-with-warm-edge at the hill approach in Ch. 27, the new-color-that-is-not-green-not-amber-not-gold at Ch. 28's shelf — this is a single sustained arc of color-as-character-growth that no other book I can think of has attempted at this scale. It works. The one note I have is that Davan's Ch. 30 section is the shortest of the four, and while I think that is correct (his position is a body-position, articulated silently), a reader could experience it as under-weighted relative to Aldric's. I would not add length. I would leave it as the quietest of the four voices, which is also Davan's character throughout.

*The strongest POV as the full picture emerges.* Aldric. He was my pick after the first read — "the most fully realized character in the book" — and the Ch. 29 reading sequence has only deepened the case. He carries the largest intellectual and spiritual arc and he carries it in the smallest, driest, most scholarly register, which is the hardest possible combination to write. *The thinnest POV.* Vane, but only because hers is structurally the briefest and because her withdrawal arc in Chs. 26–30 is rationed to the minimum. This is a feature, not a bug; I would not expand her. But a first-time reader will come away thinking Aldric and Maren are the book's dual spine, with Davan and Vane as the two bookends. That is also the correct reading.

*The "not X, Y" construction tic I flagged in the first letter.* Watch for it at line-edit. I did not count systematically this pass but I noticed it a few times in Chs. 21–26, and with the v2 drafts recent, it may have crept back in. Cut to one per chapter at most.

## Structure — The Full Arc

*Three-act architecture.* Earns itself. Act I (Chs. 1–10) is four ignitions and a convergence at the road meeting. Act II (Chs. 11–22) is the city arc: four threads gather, corroborate, then complicate (Ch. 20), then depart. Act III (Chs. 23–30) is the journey, the hill, the interior, the record, the disagreement. Each act has a clean entry and a clean exit. The Ch. 10 road meeting is the Act I turn and it is the book's best chapter. Ch. 20's cooperage basement is the Act II midpoint and it is, on this read, earning its weight because of the Renner inversion. Ch. 27's disc-in-the-recess is the Act III climax-setup and it sits at exactly the two-thirds point of Act III (four chapters to follow), which gives the ending its required air.

*The middle, revisited.* No longer sags. I was worried in the first letter that Chs. 17–22 would read as aftermath. They do not. The v2 Ch. 17's Reth-confirmation, Ch. 18's harvesting-irony-made-explicit, Ch. 19's Edvar-face-activation-and-Davan-recognition, Ch. 20's Renner-inversion, Ch. 21's Aldric-arrival-and-hand-stopped-short-of-the-disc, Ch. 22's Bloom-giver-freed-and-departure-tomorrow — these are six chapters each doing distinct load-bearing work. The middle is now one of the book's stronger stretches, not its weakest.

*Act III pacing.* Not rushed. Not bloated. Chs. 23–26 are the journey and they do the work a journey needs to do — group-forming, withdrawal-visible, empath-reads-older-register-under-country, face-displays-compared-and-reconciled, follower-smelled — without any wasted scene. Chs. 27–30 are the four climax beats on four chapters, exactly as I recommended in the first letter. One chapter per beat. The pacing respects the voice the book has established. Act III is the right length.

*The four-POV braid at Ch. 30.* Lands. I have said why above. It does not feel inserted; it feels completed — the prologue's eight accounts find their companion in the epilogue's four, and the two brackets make the book itself into the multi-vantage structure its thesis demands. The decision to echo form at close mirrors the thesis ("no single account contains the whole") at the scale of book-shape.

## The Archive Disagreement as Ending

Let me take a side: the disagreement works.

*Earned, not a punt.* A punt would be: four characters gesturing vaguely at what to do and the narrator refusing to say what they chose, leaving the reader to imagine. That is not what you have done. You have given each of the four characters a specific, articulated position, each traceable to their arc's specific journey, each defended in their own voice. Vane's *leave as found* is a professional's last act — the woman who spent ten years arriving at places and removing things from them, refusing, at the end, to take one more thing from a room. Aldric's *build the table* is a scholar-priest's only honest response to a decision larger than any single Keeper. Davan's *never open* is his body's answer — he is the Chrysalis now, and his bodily kinship is with the children the captain named, not with the unchanged ancestors in the holds. Maren's *open eventually, for company* is the surprise position and the one the book gives the most interior weight to, and I want to defend it specifically.

*Would a general-audience reader be satisfied?* Some will be and some will not, and this is the correct failure mode for a literary SFF novel. The reader who wants a resolution — "they opened it and it was X" — will be frustrated, and those readers will leave reviews saying so. The reader who has been paying attention to the form — the prologue's multi-vantage structure, the bible's treatment of the Chrysalis-as-theology-built-on-biology, the book's repeated demonstration that every character who has been sure about anything has been at least partially wrong — will recognize that the refusal-to-resolve is itself a statement, not a default. The second reader is your audience. The first reader is not. You have positioned the book correctly for the audience it wants.

*Maren's position as the surprise.* It works, and it works because of one specific craft move in Ch. 30: the captain's own voice, through the paired-register playback mechanism, says "the children will be a new people. I think they will be a people worth the coming." That sentence is spoken — heard by all four — before Maren articulates her position. The captain's sentence is the warrant for her position. She is not arbitrarily wanting to open the archive; she is responding to the only ancestor who has ever spoken to her directly, who told her generation they might be "a people worth the coming." This is the book's most emotionally earned move, and it is also the move that prevents Maren's position from reading as sentimentality. The sentimentality, if any, is the captain's — ten thousand years dead, tired, getting up from a desk to go sit in the light. Maren receives it. The book does not endorse her position over the others; it simply gives her access to a piece of evidence the others do not have in their bodies the same way. *Small hour in the long room* is the tempered register that keeps her position honest, and it is the right register.

So: the ending does not cheat, does not punt, and does not let any one character win. The archive stays sealed, the disc stays seated, the ship remains open for whoever comes next. This is the correct ending. Hold it.

## Where It Lives on the Shelf — Revised

Comp titles. I want to reconfirm and then update.

*Reconfirmed from the first letter:* Roanhorse's *Black Sun* still holds as the commercial register comp. Tesh's *Some Desperate Glory* still holds as the structural comp for "the mystery is solved early and the novel keeps going." It is closer in shape to **Arkady Martine's *A Memory Called Empire* / *A Desolation Called Peace*** — multi-POV SF that ends on a deliberate un-resolution of a species-level question, with a strong interior voice and a willingness to hold its science-fictional material as political and theological rather than adventure. Martine is more plotted than you are, and her sentences are brisker, but the register of "we have reached the question we cannot resolve and the ending is the resolving of our positions on the question" is the same register. I would put Martine in the pitch.

A second update: given Ch. 30's "She kept walking" and the book's overall commitment to what I will call *aspectual endings* (a verb form rather than an event), there is a literary-fiction comp in the bloodline of **Marilynne Robinson's *Housekeeping*** and Han Kang's shorter work that I would gesture at in the query letter if you want one non-genre comp to show the literary-side of your audience ambition. You are more plotted and more world-built than Robinson; you are less plotted and less cruel than Kang. *What I'd still avoid in comps.* Tchaikovsky, Powell, any Ship-falls-to-Earth SF that resolves its mysteries through action. Also: N.K. Jemisin. The book is now longer than a typical debut at ~133k words; this is not a problem for a literary SFF submission (Tesh is in that range, Suri is longer) but some agents will ask about trimming. *Commercial viability.* Mid-list debut, literary-SFF crossover imprint (Tordotcom, Saga, Orbit US's literary line, FSG Originals, Erewhon). The book is now a stronger version of what the first letter called a real but not a lead-title book. I would now say: it is a strong debut in a lane that has been commercially proven by *A Memory Called Empire*'s success, and an agent with the right list could position it for a respectable deal. Not auction. A two-book deal is plausible.

## What I'd Change, and What I'd Leave Alone

*What I'd change.* Honestly, not much. The three items above (trim 6, trim 13, light pass on Aldric voice in early chapters) are the substantive craft moves. The "not X, Y" tic and "careful" overuse are line-edit chores. These together are three weeks of work for an author who knows their own sentences. You are, at this point, editing rather than revising.

*What I'd leave alone.* Almost everything else. Specifically: do not cut Ch. 3 (Sella's death + first face encounter); do not cut Lera's Ch. 6 speech (it is, as I said in the first letter, the book's thesis statement and it is delivered without strain); do not cut the Ch. 9 woodcutter's shelter reframe; do not shorten Ch. 10 (the bird-carving); do not cut Edvar's Ch. 19 face-activation; do not compress Ch. 20's disc-pulses-specifically beat; do not cut the Ch. 27 father's-remains scene; do not shorten Ch. 29; do not touch Ch. 30. Absolutely do not change "She kept walking."

If I were to be unkind and find something to cut that I was more tempted to cut on this read than on the first — the Davan Ch. 12 dock-watching-Maren scene is the one place I could imagine compressing, because Ch. 15 covers most of the same ground and Ch. 16 delivers the meeting. But Ch. 12 is Davan's city-arrival chapter and I think the tissue it does — Pip introduction, dock-labor establishment, the first sighting of Maren — justifies its length. I would leave it.

## Verdict

Publishable with light revision. This is a fundamentally finished literary SFF novel that has earned its place in the lane the first letter identified for it, and the revisions I asked for have landed in forms that make the book substantially stronger than the Chs. 1–20 draft I first read. The pre-query punch list above is a four-to-six-week editing pass, not a rewrite. You have a book. Send it out.
