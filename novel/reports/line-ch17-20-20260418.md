## Filter Words

**Highest-priority finding per FB-7. Every instance catalogued below.**

### Chapter 17 (Aldric)

**Ch. 17, para. 2:** "He had slept at waypoint inns and eaten what was
offered and spoken to no one about anything that mattered, and the
discipline of this had been less difficult than he'd anticipated, which
troubled him, because it meant the lying was becoming easier."
— The construction "he'd anticipated" is a soft filter. The sentence
already shows the thought; trim the scaffolding.
→ "...the discipline of this cost him less than he'd feared — which
troubled him. The lying was becoming easier."

**Ch. 17, para. 6 (Aldric enters Brennan's house):** "Aldric stood in
the doorway and looked at the room and *felt*, for the first time since
leaving the Confluence, something other than the low persistent unease
that had been his companion on the road. What he felt was recognition."
— Double filter ("felt"/"What he felt was"). Muffles a strong moment.
→ "Aldric stood in the doorway and the low persistent unease lifted for
the first time since the Confluence road. Recognition. This was the room
of a man who had been working on a question for a long time..."

**Ch. 17, para. 7 (Brennan at the worktable):** "...a posture Aldric
*recognized* from his own habits — grounding yourself in the physical..."
— "Recognized" as filter. The habit can be shown, not noticed.
→ "...hands flat on the surface the way Aldric himself stood before the
difficult — grounding yourself in the physical first."

**Ch. 17, Brennan-and-maps section:** "In each map's margin it was
rendered slightly differently — different hands, different implements,
different degrees of haste — but the form was consistent."
— Clean; no filter. Flagging as CLEAN.

**Ch. 17, Reth-at-dawn section:** "He *looked at* Aldric for a long
time without speaking. He was *reading* him, Aldric *realized* — not
the way Brennan read..."
— "Aldric realized" is a filter, and unnecessary: the reader can realize
it too.
→ "He looked at Aldric for a long time without speaking. Not the way
Brennan read — for information and implication — but the way a surveyor
read terrain, for what the surface told you about what was underneath."

**Ch. 17, lamp tic, road west:** "The lamp tic found him twice on the
road — once at a waypoint where he pinched a candle stub beside the
register..."
— "The lamp tic *found* him" is a vivid construction; not a filter. CLEAN.

**Ch. 17, closing para:** "He *looked at* them the way Reth must have
*looked at* the formation..."
— The doubled "looked at" is a repetition issue (covered below), not
strictly a filter. Noted there.

**Ch. 17 filter count: 3 clear instances** (felt/What he felt,
recognized/from his own habits, Aldric realized).

### Chapter 19 (Davan)

**Ch. 19, para. 1:** "Davan had learned to *notice* it the moment
Edvar opened the door"
— "had learned to notice" is a mild filter. The smell can simply arrive.
→ "The shop smelled of linseed and old paper — not the sharp chemical
bite of fresh oil but the settled, sweet undertone of decades of it
soaked into wood and plaster."
(Cut the framing clause entirely; the description already works without it.)

**Ch. 19, face activation:** "Davan did not pull his hand away. His
skin-light had gone to blue at his throat and was climbing toward
something else, something he could *feel* building but had not yet
arrived."
— "he could feel building" = filter. The skin-light is already showing
it.
→ "...something building at his collarbones, not yet arrived." (The
skin-light is the externalized "feel"; no need to state it twice.)

**Ch. 19, para after "It came down":** "He let the thought arrive at
its own pace." — Not a filter; this is Davan's characteristic processing.
CLEAN.

**Ch. 19, Edvar's face reaction:** "Davan *watched* something cross the
mapmaker's face that he *recognized* because he had seen it in the
mirror."
— "Davan watched" + "he recognized" = stacked filters around the
moment. Let the thing cross the face; Davan doesn't need to be caught
watching.
→ "Something crossed the mapmaker's face: the expression of a man whose
half-thought had just been finished by someone else's voice. Davan knew
that look. He'd worn it."

**Ch. 19, late section:** "He could *feel* something else — not cold,
not warm, but present. The same quality he had *felt* near Corvel's
container..."
— Doubled "feel/felt" within two sentences, both as filters. This is a
significant moment; it deserves direct rendering.
→ "Something registered — not cold, not warm, but present. The same
quality as Corvel's container at the cooperage meeting, the proximity-
sense that was not temperature but registered in the same place
temperature did."

**Ch. 19 filter count: 4 instances** (noticed, feel building,
Davan watched/he recognized, feel/felt).

### Chapter 20 (Maren)

**Ch. 20, entry para 2:** "She *catalogued* it automatically: one exit
behind her..."
— Maren's POV signature move. Same as Vane's "cataloguing" — CLEAN as
intentional voice.

**Ch. 20, post-Hollow exchange:** "She could *see* her doing it — the
slight narrowing of focus that happened when someone was fitting a new
piece into an existing framework..."
— "She could see her doing it" = filter. The narrowing of focus can
be observed directly.
→ "The slight narrowing of focus as Renner fitted the new piece..."

**Ch. 20, disc-reveal interior:** "She *thought about* her father. Not
sentimentally — Maren did not *think about* her father sentimentally..."
— "Thought about" doubled in two sentences. The first is acceptable as
POV transition; the echo in the second ("Maren did not think about")
reads as the author stepping in to annotate Maren's own thought. Cut the
second.
→ "She thought about her father. Not sentimentally, because
sentimentality was a luxury she'd sold three years ago along with her
real name and her hometown..."

**Ch. 20, disc-reveal interior continued:** "She *thought about* what
Vane had said... She *thought about* Ulla's letter... She *thought about*
the recess on the disc's underside..."
— Three consecutive "she thought about" constructions over five
paragraphs. This is the most significant filter-word cluster in all four
chapters. The anaphora reads as intentional (building toward Maren's
decision), but its effectiveness is undercut by leading with the filter
each time rather than the thing itself.
→ Each "she thought about X" → just: the X. Let the objects appear in
sequence without the announcement.
Example: "Her father: he had carried this disc. He had gone east.
He had not come back." / "Vane at the gate in the dark:..." /
"Ulla's letter, her father's words..." — the rhythm holds; the filter
disappears.

**Ch. 20, post-disc-set:** "Maren *looked at* the disc on the table and
the container beside it and the faces around the room, all of them turned
toward her, and she *understood* something she had not understood
before."
— "she understood something she had not understood before" = quintessential
filter announcement. The understanding is already landing in the paragraphs
that follow; the announcement is redundant.
→ Cut the clause. End the sentence at "all of them turned toward her."
Begin the next paragraph directly with the insight: "The disc was not a
keepsake."

**Ch. 20 filter count: 5 instances** (could see her doing it, doubled
thought about, three consecutive thought about, looked at/understood).

begins to feel like the author's stage direction every time a character
suppresses feeling.

## Repetition

### Word and Phrase Repetition

**"catalogued" / "cataloguing" (Ch. 18)**
Used 3 times within the chapter: "She catalogued. She'd always been good
at cataloguing." / "the operational part of her brain didn't stop
working just because the rest of her had" (paraphrase) / and once more
in the final basement section. The first and third uses are separated by
enough text that the echo is acceptable; the doubled use in the opening
block ("catalogued. She'd always been good at cataloguing") is
tautological within two sentences.
→ Cut the meta-commentary: "She catalogued" is sufficient. The reminder
that she's "always been good at" it explains what the prose has already
shown.

**"twenty years" (Ch. 19)**
Used 7 times across the chapter, including twice in close succession:
"Twenty years," Edvar said again. But this time the words carried a
different weight." / "For twenty years I have known..." / "I have spent
twenty years building a case." The repetition is clearly intentional —
"twenty years" is Edvar's refrain, his personal count — and most
instances are appropriately spaced. However, the cluster in the middle
section (three uses within 10 lines) tips from rhetorical into
mechanical. Suggest removing one instance from the dense cluster
and preserving the framing ones.

**"the particular" (Ch. 17 and Ch. 20)**
"the *particular* warmth / unease / patience / stillness / silence..."
appears as a construction across both chapters. In Ch. 17: "the
particular cruelty of self-knowledge" (earned). In Ch. 20: "the
particular dampness of stone" / "the particular stillness of a large
man" — two uses within 40 lines.
→ One is fine. Two in close proximity becomes a template. Vary the
second.

**"the way" as connector (Ch. 17)**
"the way Reth must have looked at the formation / the way a wall becomes
invisible when you've walked past it / the way Brennan read / the way you
handled a name..." — 8+ uses in Ch. 17. Several are strong simile; the
pattern runs hot across the chapter. Not every instance is wrong, but
the chapter reads as "the way [X] the way [Y]" in its middle stretch.
A trim of 2-3 would sharpen the remaining ones.

**"the particular" + "the way" compound**
Both tics appear in the same chapters. The combination signals a prose
style that is building analogies by template: "the *particular* [noun]
of a [person type] who [past participle], *the way* a [thing] [verbs]."
Aldric's voice uses this structure frequently enough that it may be
intentional to his formal register. If so, slight variation — occasionally
breaking the template by leading with the image rather than the framing
— would make the intended uses hit harder.

**"stone floor" (Ch. 19)**
"The stone floor was cold through his trousers." /
"They sat with that. [...] Davan did not say [...] He sat beside the
mapmaker on the cold stone floor..."
— Used twice in a single scene. One of the two should be varied;
"cold stone" is doing the same work both times.

**"still" and "stillness" (Ch. 20)**
"Corvel had gone very still" / "the particular stillness of a large man"
/ "Maren stood in it...with her weight even" (synonymous posture) /
"Pip [...] did not move at all."
— Four descriptions of physical stillness in the cooperage basement
within 50 lines. The stillness of the room when the disc appears is the
chapter's emotional peak; diluting it with earlier stillnesses softens
the impact. Consider reserving "very still" and "particular stillness"
for that moment only, and varying the earlier descriptions.

## Rhythm / Sentence-Level

**Ch. 17, Brennan's house description (para 4)**
A long paragraph of inventory (books, maps, worktable, documents) with
uniformly medium-length sentences. The paragraph would benefit from
variation: one short sentence — a beat — after the listing, before Aldric
registers what he's looking at.
→ After "each weighted at the corners with small smooth stones": break.
New sentence: "He stood in the doorway." Then continue.

**Ch. 17, Reth's testimony (extended section)**
The three-paragraph block where Reth describes the formation maintains
a stately, controlled pace — appropriate. However, the pause that breaks
Reth mid-sentence ("He stopped again. This time the stop was different.")
is the single best rhythmic moment in the chapter. The two paragraphs
before it are slightly too uniform; one shorter sentence inserted two
or three beats before the first stop would make the stop itself feel
more sudden.

**Ch. 18, the cost-benefit section**
The short-paragraph, short-sentence structure throughout Ch. 18 is
Vane's voice rendered at the prose level — and it works. The one area
where rhythm could be sharpened is the cost-benefit list itself. Right
now it reads as three symmetrical blocks (cost of use / benefit of use /
cost of stopping). The asymmetry of what stopping costs — it's listed
with the same even weight as the other items — slightly undercuts the
weight of the decision. A longer, less organized final "cost of
stopping" block would enact the difficulty of facing it.

**Ch. 19, "they stayed a while longer" closing section**
This section is intentionally quiet and ruminative — correct for the
chapter's emotional landing. One passage runs long:
"He thought about the carving he'd sent to Maren. The pine figure of a
woman with one arm held close, guarded. He'd carved it before he met
her, working from something he couldn't have named — an impression that
arrived in the grain and guided his knife. He thought about how the
things that mattered arrived in their own time. How you couldn't rush a
shape out of wood any more than you could rush an understanding out of
evidence."
The last two sentences ("he thought about how..." / "How you couldn't
rush...") are explaining the metaphor after it has already landed. Cut
both; the carving detail is sufficient.

**Ch. 20, Renner's explanation (para after "Sit")**
Two sentences in the Renner paragraph begin identically: "The bloodline
held that..." / "The theological framework said..." — parallel structure
that is serving the chapter but could be sharper. The first sentence
does the work; the second restates it. Suggest folding them.

**Ch. 20, the disc-reveal section**
The five-paragraph interior monologue before Maren sets the disc down
is the most ambitious rhythmic passage in these four chapters. "She
thought about her father. [...] She thought about what Vane had said.
[...] She thought about Ulla's letter." — the anaphora is intentional.
The problem, as noted under Filter Words, is that each anaphoric phrase
leads with the filter. If the filter is cut from each ("she thought
about" → direct object), the anaphora becomes stronger, not weaker:
"Her father: he had carried this disc." / "Vane at the gate..."
The rhythm survives; the filter doesn't.

## Other Prose Issues

### Clichés

**"sit with this" / "sat with that" (Ch. 17 and Ch. 19)**
"He sat with this as the road straightened..." (Ch. 17)
"They sat with that." (Ch. 19)
Both use "sitting with" as a metaphor for processing difficult
information. It is a workshop-era cliché that appears twice across two
chapters and once more in the bible's description of Edvar ("wrap it up
and put it back in the trunk"). The image is fine once; three variations
reads as a tic.
→ Ch. 17: vary ("The thought accompanied him west..." or cut the framing
entirely and go directly to what he thinks on the road).
→ Ch. 19: "They sat with that" is the more defensible use — it describes
literal, physical sitting in a shared silence. Keep this one; revise Ch.
17's version.

**"the way you [familiar activity] when [state]" (Ch. 18)**
"She knew without opening it. Had known for three days, the way you
know a room's dimensions before the lamp catches up — by the quality
of the echo when nothing comes back."
— This specific instance is excellent; the metaphor earns its space.
CLEAN. Note it here only to distinguish it from the template uses in
Ch. 17 (see "the way" repetition above).

**"something that might have been" (Ch. 17 and Ch. 20)**
"The faintest movement at the corner of Reth's mouth" —
"Renner's mouth did something that might have been the ghost of
approval. Might have been impatience."
Covered above under the non-smile mouth tic. The syntax "might have been
[X]. Might have been [Y]." is itself a Vane-chapter tic that is now
spreading. One use is a voice signature; two in four chapters suggest

than a character-specific choice.

**"with the practiced [noun] of a [person type]" (Ch. 17)**

separated and not egregious, but both could be sharpened by finding
more specific images.

## Sensory Density Notes

**Ch. 17 — primarily visual and auditory**
The Greenford scenes are rich in visual texture (maps, room arrangement,
stone color) but lean on sound and touch only. One smell line would
anchor Aldric in the space: Brennan's house should have a scent — old
paper, wood smoke, chemical preservative from the map materials. A single
line in the house-entry paragraph ("a smell of hot wax and old vellum")
would make the room as immediate as Edvar's linseed-soaked shop in Ch. 19.

Reth's house, by contrast, has a strong sensory detail already: "the
door opened before he reached it" (sound as control/warning), the
"oiled hinges" (tactile precision in maintenance). One smell detail here
too — stone-cold, clean, nothing domestic — would distinguish it from
Brennan's cluttered warmth.

**Ch. 17, road west**
The road closing section is almost entirely intellectual — Aldric
processing, reviewing, thinking. The physical world of the road
appears only at the beginning ("autumn clarity that sharpened every
edge") and at the closing image of the moons. A mid-section beat grounding
him in the body — horse movement under him, cold air, road dust — would
prevent the entire closing section from feeling like reportage.

**Ch. 18 — strong sensory work**
The copper-flower smell is the chapter's backbone and it is used
expertly: introduced at volume on the stairs, present at ghost-level
on the return walk, unresolvable at the chapter's end ("she would
probably taste it for a while"). The contrast with the canal smell
("salt and cold and clean") that cuts through it momentarily is the
best single sensory beat in these four chapters. No gap here.

One missed opportunity: the bark-skin texture. Vane describes it
visually ("tight whorls, the edges angrier than last week") but never
tactilely, even though she's examining it on her own arm. A single
texture note (what does bark-skin feel like from the inside?) would
deepen the body-horror without adding more than a clause.

**Ch. 19 — linseed smell strong, then dropped**
The chapter opens with linseed smell and old paper — excellent sensory
grounding. The back room loses scent entirely after Edvar opens the
trunk. The face activation is all visual and tactile (cold light,
fingertip contact), appropriately. But the tea scene at the close is
a missed sensory beat: "the tea was bitter, under-steeped, the tea of
a man who made it out of habit rather than pleasure." This is good.
Suggest keeping it and ensuring it's not the only grounding detail
in the section — sound (night tide turning in the harbor) is mentioned;
it could be developed one beat further.

**Ch. 20 — basement atmosphere good, then abandoned**
Opening description: "mineral cold, the particular dampness of stone
that never saw sun." Strong. But once the argument begins, the basement
disappears — the chapter becomes purely dialogue and Maren's interiority.
The lamp-guttering beat (Pip trimming wicks) is the only mid-chapter
sensory reset, and it works precisely because it's rare. Consider adding
one more low-level physical anchor in the long argument section: tallow
smell intensifying as the lamps burn down, the cold creeping in from
the floor during a long sit. These don't interrupt the argument; they
remind the reader where we are.

The disc-reveal moment is correctly rendered through its absence of
normal sensory response — it doesn't glow, doesn't ring, doesn't smell.
The contrast is the point. The room's silence and stillness are the
chapter's climax, and they land well because the author withholds
additional description at the moment of maximum reader attention. This
is the right call. Do not add sensory detail at the disc-reveal
moment; the restraint is doing critical work.
