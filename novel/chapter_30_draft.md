## Chapter Thirty — *What the Moons Keep*

---

### *Vane*

The boot came down on scree a second time and she had his weight.

Not his face. His pace. Pace gave her a body's long habit the way a
voice gave her its owner's country. Toe placed before heel, knee
absorbing what the scree wanted to do to him. Horst's instructor
had been a woman named Jax in the Threshing Yard. Vane had taken
the same course in her third year. The carriage on the slope was
Jax's carriage.

*One of my cohort's, or one of the next.*

She catalogued without moving. Flat in the north-shoulder lee, a
knuckle of stone giving her a shadow the man would not break
against until he was three paces past. Wind off her right shoulder.
Breathing through her mouth — the bark grain at the nose-bridge had
dried to a register where nasal air whistled, and whistles carried.

Seventy paces to Edvar and the cart. Edvar flat in the southern lee,
the off-horse at the cheek-strap. The horse knew him and had not
blown.

*Sixty.*

Waxed grey coat short-cut at the hip. A sword — Horst's men always
wore a sword, and a sword set a civilian off by a hundred yards,
which was half its point. No bow. No Wound-maker.

*Fifty.*

Right hand on the knife, hilt turned so the blade came edge-up if
the drawing was what this became. Left hand with a loop of fine
cord bunched in the palm. She had decided on the cord in the five
minutes before he made the crest. She did not want blood on the
hill. The hill had been bled on enough.

Her left hand was steady. Withdrawal had redistributed the tremor
overnight — hands to shoulders, which the coat hid. The shoulders
were vibrating at the clavicle. The hands would do cord-work when
cord-work came.

*Forty.*

She heard Aldric step at the passage mouth. The knee that did not
come up clean made a small asymmetric sound a trained ear would
catch at ten paces. The contract man was at forty, carriage at
eighty percent of attention; Jax's course did not teach a man to
listen for a knee in a passage he did not know existed. The sound
did not reach him.

Maren, up at the recess, had not moved. Vane had checked her three
times from the lee angle — thumb on the disc, back to the slope,
the hillside under her knee in the low warmth Davan had said was
*waking up*. Vane did not know what was waking. Her job was a man
on a slope; other people had other jobs today, and the disciplines
held when the disciplines held.

The man paused.

Not at a wind-sign. At something he had heard through the surface.
The bone-sound. It had registered at the level his training told
him was not weather and not heart. He put his hand inside his coat.

He drew — not the sword. A small pale thing, palm-sized. Not the
inner orbit symbol; a different glyph, the one the bloodline's
domestic office used for field-report. He held it flat. He was
going to report.

*Before he engages, he confirms.* The right move for a contract
man who had walked three days and found a hillside humming under
its own grass. The thing he had found was outside his brief.

She came up off her knee.

Not fast. Fast came at the end. Up at the rate grass came up in
wind, foot placed at the marked toe-spot, three paces in the slide-
step she had been taught the year of her scarring, in at his back at
an angle past the carry of his sword-side, cord over his wrist and
twisted before he could take the hand-disc up to his mouth to speak.

He was good. He turned in the cord and did not lose the disc, and
his left was coming for the sword and had it half drawn when her
knee went into the back of his right knee and her free hand came up
under his jaw and her knife was at the soft hollow under his ear
before his weight transferred.

"Drop it," she said. Threshing Yard register. "Sword. Hand-disc.
Keep the voice out of it."

He let the sword re-sheathe. The move of a man taught that a
sheathed sword was still a sword, and a lost sword in contract
accounting was a cost. She approved. She did not remove the knife.

"Hand-disc."

He lowered it slowly between his boots and set it on the grass with
his fingertips.

"Name."

"Mara."

Contract name. Fine.

"Horst."

"Yes."

"Brief."

"Authenticate signature. Secure the disc. Prefer the holder alive.
Report before close."

"You were reporting."

"I was."

"Outcome."

"Signature confirmed. Holder at the recess. Company four. Request
instruction."

"Request instruction. Smart. What were you going to do if the
instruction was *close*."

"What I was trained to do."

"Alive."

"Preferred."

"If preferred failed."

He did not answer. The silence answered.

She had known this was the answer. She had known it since the ford.
The professional part of her had filed it. The other part — the one
that had been doing work in her for five days without her looking
at it — had filed it somewhere heavier.

"Not today," she said.

His jaw was flat against the knife.

"You are going to kneel. You are going to tell me where the other
four of Horst's team are. Then you are going to walk back to New
Plymouth and tell Horst *signature did not register*. You can say
you lost contact. You can say the disc was a decoy. Tell him
anything except the truth."

"The truth."

"What is on the other side of this hill. Horst's office does not
have a brief for it. If you tell him the truth, the domestic office
comes with five teams, and the five teams make the hill a place
where the question of what to do with what's inside it is decided
by people whose job is to decide questions that way. You are one
man. I am one woman. We decide this like one and one."

Her shoulders vibrated at the clavicle. She kept the knife where
it was.

"If that sounds like mercy, it is not. It is a contract. If Horst's
men come over this ridge in the next month, I find you first. Alone.
In a house at an hour of my choosing. You know I can."

"I know who you were."

"Then kneel."

He knelt.

---

She bound him at the wrist and ankle and pushed him gently onto
his side. She did not gag him — a gag on an unwilling man in open
country was a thing that killed men who had not been meant to be
killed.

"Where are the other four."

"One on the valley road with the horses. Two in New Plymouth with
Horst. One in Stillwater on a different matter."

"The one with the horses holds station until sundown. You will
return by sundown without a signal. You will ride for New Plymouth
with him. You will report *signature did not register* and take
your fee and not come east again."

"I will not come east again."

"Good."

She stood. The vibration had moved to a band across her ribs and
the band was the price. She walked up-slope keeping to the lee,
touched Edvar's shoulder in passing — a palm on the coat, the way
you touched an old dog that had held its stand. Edvar did not turn.
The horse did not blow.

"Clear," she said. For his ear.

"I'm here."

"The cart stays. I'll come for you when."

"I'll be here when."

She climbed the last twenty paces to the passage mouth. Maren still
at the recess, hair at her cheek, thumb on the disc. Had heard none
of it, or had heard all of it and done the work of not turning.

Vane put her palm on the wall at the angle — the cool came into her
palm and did not travel, the way Aldric had said — and she called
in, low.

"Aldric. Davan. The slope is held. Mine. The one who was coming is
off his feet."

A pause inside. Then Aldric, low: "Thank you."

She did not answer. *Thank you* was not a thing she took yet. She
stood at the angle with her hand on the wall and the vibration in
her ribs and the sword and the hand-disc at her hip, and the day's
work — in the sense it was ever done — was done.

Under her boots, through the hill, something was being said.
Something was being answered. Not her language. Not anyone's
language she had been hired to read.

She stood the angle anyway. Which was, she thought, the new thing.
You stood angles for languages that were not yours.

### *Davan*

He had not left the shelf.

His pad was still against the object. He had said *ready* at the
passage mouth and his body had turned with Aldric's toward the
passage, and then his body had turned back.

The one kept thing his finger had chosen had not asked him to stay.
The room had not asked him. The staying had been his.

Outside, on the slope, a man was being spoken to by Vane. He could
not hear the words. The passage at that angle did not carry voice.
But he had — in the underside of his forearms where the new color
lived — the quality of it. A man's fear, reined in. A professional
discipline holding the fear flat. The cost of the holding. Vane's
own pressure on the slope, which he had come to know at the cart
in the last three days — not anger. *Economy*. A woman doing the
least motion that produced the correct result, because the economy
was what had been left her that was still hers.

His wrists were lit low. He did not look at them. He had stopped
looking at them a long way back. He had come, by the slow route a
body took to its own instruments, to trust what his wrists said the
way an older carpenter trusted what his plane said.

He did not want to pick the object up.

He wanted — and the wanting was a new register — to ask the object
a question. Not in words. In the register his skin had made with
the wall's register, when the new color had settled in him and the
wall had held the color and the holding had been the thing the
material did because *keeping* was what the material did.

He put his palm against the flat side of the object. Not the pad of
a finger this time. The palm.

The object was at the same register as the walls. He had expected
this. He had not expected the difference — the object was *denser*.
The wall held what came near it; the object held what was already
inside it. The object was a wall in miniature, closed on a thing
that had been put in it to be there, and what had been put in it
was pressing gently outward against the inside of its own holding
the way a seed pressed gently outward against the inside of a seed.

He read the seed.

He did not try to read *what* was in the object. He had not earned
the what. The what was a different work — an opening work, a
beginning-of-a-line-of-people work, a work none of the four of them
was the instrument for. He read, instead, the quality of the
holding. The long patience of it. The absence of any hurry.

What was being held in this room, and in the four containers under
Aldric's temple, was — Aldric had said it last night through the
small lamp at camp — *preserved, viable, human*. The thing under
his palm was kin to those four.

*A whole line of people, kept.*

*Not kept like a coat kept. Not kept like a memory kept. Kept the
way a seed is kept — alive at a register where the life is paused,
and the life is still the life, and the life is waiting without
knowing it is waiting.*

*And if we were to wake them, we would have to become a thing we
are not, first.*

He took his palm off.

His wrist-light did not fade. It lived in the tendon. It had been
living there since yesterday morning, and it would live there for
the rest of his life. The color was not his mutation any more. The
color was what his mutation had been reaching for all twenty years,
and had reached.

He turned from the shelf.

Aldric was at the passage-mouth and past it, at the angle where
Vane had called him. Davan did not go to the angle. He had not been
called. He crossed to the center of the floor and stood there with
his hands at his sides and let his wrists hold their light, and he
said — not aloud; to the room — the thing he needed to say before
he left.

*I am not going to ask you to be opened.*

He stood a moment with the sentence in his chest.

*I am not going to ask anyone to be opened.*

The walls took his color and held it a hand's breadth and gave it
back. The color between his body and the material was a color
neither his nor the material's, and he had come, in the last half-
hour, to think of that color as the color *deciding* made when a
body decided against the grain of the thing it might, under other
pressure, have said yes to.

His mutation was a gift the world had given him. It was also a debt
the world had laid on him. He did not know, today, how to separate
them. He did not know whether separation was the right work. The
originals he was kin to — the right-hand cell in Aldric's study,
the holds at this ship's stern, the four containers under the
Confluence — were his ancestors. Kin across a distance his body did
not quite have the words for.

He owed them the sense not to open them to relieve himself of the
debt. He did not know what he would have come to think in a year,
or ten. Today, this hour, the answer his body gave was: *let them
rest. Let the Chrysalis be what the Chrysalis is. I am the
Chrysalis now. What I owe the originals is the attention, not the
un-making.*

He was not going to say this to Aldric yet. Aldric had another
instrument. Maren had another. Vane had another. He said it to the
room.

The room held it without answering, the way the room held everything
it was asked to hold. The lack of answer was not silence. The lack
of answer was what answering meant when you were a room whose work
was holding. The room's yes and no were the same thing: *held.*

He walked, slowly, toward the passage.

### *Aldric*

The passage carried his step and handed it back.

He stopped at the angle Vane had named.

Beyond the recess, beyond Maren's shoulder, down-slope — a flat
register of grass and rust and a man's boot in it at the rest-angle
a boot took when the boot was not planning to move again for a
while. Vane was coming up from the lee with the carriage of a worker
whose work had landed. The vibration in the set of her ribs he
caught at the second look. Not there at the cart in the grey
morning. There now. She had spent something to do what she had
done.

He had asked her to spend it, without asking.

He would keep the account.

The *mine* in her call went down like a stone in still water. He
did not step past it. The slope was hers. The storage behind him
was — he did not yet have a verb for it — *attended*. The hill was
Maren's knee. The cart was Edvar's hand. The breach was the
morning's. He was the one at the angle between the passage and the
slope, weight on the balls of his feet the way a man stood when his
body did not yet know whether the next move was forward or back.

His body did not have to know.

"Thank you," he said. Down the lee. She did not answer, which was
the correct not-answering.

He put his palm against the wall of the passage and let the cool
be, and he thought, for the second time in an hour, the sentence he
had been composing for three months.

*The next Keeper is going to be the one who took the floor up.*

He had been carrying it as something about a future Aldric — three
months older, five years older — who had gone back to the Confluence
and had, with the slow patience of institutional change, persuaded
the Temple to admit what the chamber under the altar held. The
composed thing was the wrong composition.

*The next Keeper is not a future me. The next Keeper is me, now.
The floor has been taken up. I am standing on the taken-up floor.*

It did not land as fear. He tried the word the way a man tried a
garment he had not worn for a season; the garment did not fit. What
he carried was closer to *consequence*. Thirty years of weight at a
certain balance, now being re-balanced, and the re-balancing was
the body learning the new load-point. The hand no longer went to
the lamp-cord because the lamp-cord was not the instrument.

Three things, then, in the register a man had three things when he
had stopped composing and had started moving:

*The records are not a find. The records are a floor. The floor is
under my life's work. The life's work does not continue the way it
was going.*

*The floor does not belong to me. It belongs to anyone who will have
to live with what the floor is — which is every living person on
Gaius. I am not the one who tells them. I am the one who does not
stop them from being told.*

*The channel is not the Temple's. The channel has to be built. It
will be built by the people who built the cooperage group, and by
Brennan, and by Reth, and by the young cartographer whose name I did
not catch, and by others I have not yet met. I will carry documents.
I will carry what I saw. I will, for a while, be a man who has lost
his frock and has not yet learned what a man without a frock does,
and I will learn.*

He thought of Crane — the danger of an officer of a company whose
shareholder was a theology, and whose shareholder had just taken a
loss of ten thousand years of dividend. He thought of the long
letter he was going to have to write. It would not be to Brennan.
Would not be to Cael. *The letter is to the next reader,* he
thought. *The next reader is not a Keeper. The next reader is the
country.*

He walked out of the passage into the morning.

Pale blue sky, wind-thinned, the overnight weather cleared by the
dry wind off the country. He did not look up. He was not ready. He
looked across the slope to Maren, stopped one pace short, put his
hands flat on the tops of his thighs the way a man put his hands
when his hands were honoring a work that was not his.

"The storage held," he said. Low. "Davan is coming out."

"Yes."

"The man."

"Vane."

"Yes."

A pause.

"The records say. I have read what I could read. I will read again.
It was as we suspected and more. There is something we will need to
decide about, and it is not a decision I am going to decide for the
four of us."

"All right."

Her thumb had not moved. Her shoulder was the shoulder of a girl who
had knelt at a recess for two hours and would kneel at it for
another two if the hill asked her. He could not tell, from where he
stood, whether she was breathing. Then he could. She was.

"When you are ready," he said.

"When I am ready."

His hand at his side did not move toward any cord. The hand was at
his thigh. The thigh was his thigh. The hand was his hand.

He stepped back two paces and turned toward where the passage met
the morning, and he waited for Davan to come out.

### *Maren*

Something was beginning under her thumb.

Not new. Continuing. The hum had stopped and the bone-sound had
begun and the saying-and-answer — Davan's phrase, from the outcrop
at dusk — had been running under the hill since the seating. Her
knee had gone to the kind of numbness where the numbness was its
own sensation. Her thumb was on the symbol.

The disc was warm and lit and not hers any more and still hers.
*Hers* was not a category of having. *Hers* was a category of
carrying. She had carried the disc for seventeen years and the disc
had carried her for seventeen years, and now it was carrying her
and the hill both, and the carrying was the relation, and the
relation did not belong to anyone.

She had not wept at her father's coat. She was not going to weep at
the recess either. The weeping country was a country for later.
Her body was doing a different work today.

Behind her, on the slope, she had heard Vane. Not the words. The
pace. The slide-step Vane had practiced on the hotel floor in New
Plymouth. The pitched-low professional register Vane had used once
on the road from the woodcutter's shelter when she had been talking
a bolted trail-mare down. Maren had not turned. Turning would have
been a question, and she had not had a question to ask.

Aldric had come out and stood beside her and not touched her, and
had said *when you are ready*, and had stepped back two paces to
give her the recess. The giving of the recess was the same thing
Edvar had done at the shoulder of bare stone when he had put his
hand on her hair and taken it away. Two older men had said *go*
this morning in the only word each had for *go*, and both had let
her go.

She was still at the recess.

Under her thumb a second resonance had arrived.

The bone-sound was the continuous condition, the tide's pull of
being at the recess. The second came up through the first at about
the height of her hip — Davan's *far*, the saying-and-answer at a
distance the word *distance* was not built for. It was in the
underside of her jaw where jaw met neck. A place her body had used,
when she had been a child, to tell her when a frog had moved in the
rushes on the other side of the pond at Ashwick before her eye had
caught it.

Something far was answering.

The answer had been coming for two hours. It was close now, to
whatever *close* meant at this distance.

She kept her thumb on the symbol. She waited.

---

The answer arrived as voice.

Not at her ear. At the hill. The storage behind her took a breath
the way rooms took breaths, and into the breath the voice came —
not a new voice; the one Aldric had heard at the voiced wall and
had told them about by the camp lamp last night. A middle-register
man's voice, tired, at the cadence of a man who had sat down at the
end of a day he had not expected to have. Aldric had said: *the
words did not resolve*.

The words resolved. Not because any of them had learned the
language. Because the saying-and-answer — the *far* Davan had been
reading — had arrived at *close*, and in the arriving it had
brought with it the matching half of the record that had been
locked against the voice in the wall for ten thousand seasons. The
answer laid itself over the saying the way a transparent page laid
over a page it had been drawn to fit.

Not translated. *Paired.* Maren heard, with one ear, the long
vowels and the consonants her country's tongue did not use; with
another ear — if a body had an ear for this — she heard the sense.

The man was saying: *This is the captain of the fourth ship.*

A pause. A breath. The breath of a man who had just set something
down on a desk he was not going to pick up again.

*We failed our descent. The lander is down in the eastern foothills.
Most of us did not reach the ground. Those of us who did are not, I
think, going to remain who we are for very long. The world is in us.
There is no way out of it.*

Another pause.

*We have moved what could be moved from the holds to the secondary
cache at the river-meeting site. The archive goes with it. The
archive does not open with the hands we have now. It is not meant
to. It is meant for whoever comes after, if there is a whoever, if
the after is kind.*

*To the three still on station: we did what we could. You are not
coming for us. I am sorry. Hold your post as long as the post will
hold you. When it does not hold you any longer, go home. We are
home already, in a way. The children will be a new people. I think
they will be a people worth the coming.*

A longer pause.

*This is the last of the log I am going to record. There is a
morning outside and I am going to go sit in it. I am told there is
a hillside to the east that moves in the light. It is our own hull.
Imagine. I am going to go look at it.*

The man said the mission name — the four-letter unit, the inner
orbit figure doing the work of a letter. Aldric had told them about
this. The man said it the way a man said a name. Then a single word
with the shape of a sign-off. A man getting up from a desk.

The voice was finished.

Maren did not move.

She had heard the whole of it on her knee at the recess, and the
hearing had gone through her like a bell rung at a distance of ten
thousand seasons, and the bell had rung as a bell rings when the
hand that strikes it is, just before striking, alive.

Aldric, behind her, had made no sound.

Davan, at the passage mouth, had made no sound.

Vane, at the angle, had made no sound.

She heard them, through the saying-and-answer, being four people who
had heard the same voice at the same moment and had been, each of
them, the only person in the world who had heard it, because each
of them had heard it in the register their own body had made to
catch it.

The captain had looked at a hillside in the morning and had gone out
to sit in the light. Ten thousand years ago. Before the word
*Ashwick*. Before the word *Keeper*. Before the word *bloodline*.
The captain had gone out to sit.

She was a girl at a recess with her thumb on a disc, and the captain
had been a man her body was kin to, and the man had gone out to sit
in the morning, and she was — had always been, without the word for
it — one of the children he had said might be a people worth the
coming.

---

The answer arrived as light.

Above her. A thinning of the morning across the whole east sky, the
way a cloud thinned when a cloud was about to give up. There was no
cloud. The morning had been clear for an hour. For three seconds
the color of daylight acquired an under-register — a second color
under the pale blue — that did not belong to morning.

She lifted her eye from the disc.

Her knee was past caring about knees. She only lifted her eye — from
the recess to the slope-line, from the slope-line to the crest of
the eastern ridge, from the crest of the ridge to the sky above the
ridge.

The sky above the ridge held three moons.

---

She had been looking away from the moons for three years.

She had known they were in the sky. She had looked at her boots.
She had looked at the fire. She had looked at Davan's face or
Vane's back or Aldric's hands. She had not looked up. Looking up
had been a thing some version of herself had done when she had been
a child and had not yet known the moons were a country she had not
been invited into, and that version had been, for three years, one
she had kept at a distance while the version with the disc in her
coat had walked.

Now she looked up.

Three moons, full-bright together, over the eastern hills. The
ridge of the country ran under them in a long dark line. The sky
between them was the dry pale blue of the eastern weather, and the
moons were each what they were and were also, as she looked, three
of a thing.

Her eye gave her what her eye had not given her in three years. Not
that they were ships. Her eye did not know ships. Her eye gave her
that they were a set. That the sky was holding them at the positions
a sky held things at when the things had been placed at the
positions and had stayed. They were not drifting. They were *at*.
At the places they were at. At the work they were at. Doing what
they had been doing, which was — because her body had learned the
word in the passage, and at the recess, and in the captain's quiet
voice a minute ago — *holding*.

They were holding their stations.

They had been holding them the whole time she had been walking.
They had been holding them when her father had walked east and had
lain down four paces short of the recess. They had been holding
them when her mother had died in the winter and she had held her
mother's comb the first time with the full understanding it was her
mother's.

A memory she had not carried came up under the moons-look.

She had been five. Her father in the courtyard at dusk. He had
lifted her onto his shoulder and pointed at the sky and said *look*,
and she had looked, and the moons had been three of a set even
then, and he had not said what they were, but he had made her look.

He had been making her look.

She had forgotten.

She looked in the way he had taught her before she had had the
words to know what looking was, and she let what was in the sky be
in the sky, and what was in her be in her, and for a moment — the
moment was short and the moment was also a moment she would carry
for the rest of her life and would not, afterward, describe to
anyone, not even to Davan — the two of them were at a register that
did not have a name.

The register was the register of the Tidemark harbor-master, whose
words she had read at eleven without remembering afterward that she
had read them. The words had sat in her. She knew them as the
condition at her sternum when she looked up at three full-bright
moons over an eastern ridge with her thumb on a disc that had been
her father's, knowing herself to be carrying a thing worn into the
shape of a thing you were born knowing.

Grief that did not belong to her personally.

Grief that was hers anyway.

She let it be hers.

She did not cry.

---

The four of them were going to disagree.

She knew this with the part of her the captain's voice had opened.

She knew Vane's answer, though Vane had not said it. *The question
is not mine. It is not yours. It is the next generation's, if there
is one. We leave the room as we found it.*

She knew Aldric's. *The answer is a process, not an act. The
deciding is the instrument. We do not decide today. We build the
table at which the decision will be made, and we may not be at the
table when the decision is made. That is correct.*

She knew Davan's. *The Chrysalis is me now. The originals are my
kin. I owe them the attention, not the un-making. Let them rest.*

She knew her own. Her own was the one she had not expected. Her own
was the one the captain's quiet voice had just handed her.

The captain had said *the children will be a new people*. He had
said *I think they will be a people worth the coming*. He had said
it ten thousand years ago and had gone out to sit in the morning
and had not come back. The archive was his last care for a new
people he had not lived to see. Her own people.

She wanted — for her small hour in the long room of the choice —
to open it. Not soon. Not in a panic. Not for the bloodline or the
Temple. For *company*. The originals her body was kin to were the
only kin she had left except the four she had found on the road.
She wanted them to exist. She wanted to live in a world in which
her father's people were not only in her body as an absence. She
wanted them to be in the world as *people*.

She also wanted what Davan wanted, and what Vane wanted, and what
Aldric wanted. The four wants were in her at once, and one was the
loudest this hour, and the loudness of the one did not un-make the
others.

She was not going to get her way. The others were not going to get
theirs. The disagreement was going to be the thing they made,
together, instead of the decision.

She breathed in the eastern air. The air was dry and held and
smelled of the grass and the rust and the faint indoors that still
came down the passage from the storage. She lifted her thumb.

The disc stayed seated. The symbol stayed lit. The bone-sound did
not stop. The saying-and-answer held. Whatever had been begun did
not need her thumb to continue. Her thumb had been the beginning.
The continuing was the hill's work.

She put her hand on her thigh. She did not stand yet.

She looked once more at the three moons over the eastern hills. The
moons did not look back, because the moons were not in the business
of looking. They were at their stations. They were keeping.

She let them keep.

She stood.

She turned from the recess and walked, with the stiff knee, up the
slope toward the passage where Davan and Aldric were waiting and
where Vane was coming up from the lee.

The records were inside the hill, and the four containers were
under the floor of a temple six days' ride to the west, and none of
what was in either place was going to be resolved today. What would
be resolved today was that they were going to walk down to Edvar at
the cart, and eat something, and sit in the grass, and begin to
disagree. The disagreement was going to be the beginning.

She looked up one more time.

She had not looked in three years. She had looked twice in three
minutes. She was going to look tomorrow and the day after and every
night for the rest of her life, because the knowing was a small
hard warm thing at her sternum she was not going to set down.

She walked up the slope.

Behind her the hill kept keeping. Above her the moons kept their
stations. Ahead were three people who did not agree, and one old
man at a cart, and a contract man tied in the grass, and a long day
of which most had not yet happened.

She kept walking.
