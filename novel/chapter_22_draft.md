# Chapter 22 — What the Contract Was For

## Chapter Twenty-Two — *Vane*
### What the Contract Was For

---

The patterns told her in the morning.

She had been reading Horst's search for six days, and on the sixth day the shape of it closed by half. Not all at once. In increments. A lodging visited twice on Sellcross Road, which was north of the cooperage by four streets. A boy sent down Marlin's Walk asking after strangers, which was south of it by three. A tailing man she had clocked on Tuesday along the river quays, retracing the same ground on Wednesday in a coat he had not worn on Tuesday.

She sat on the edge of the bed with her boots unlaced and the inventory in her head.

*Six days. Three narrowing arcs. One-shift coat. Two-handler lodging sweep. One outer cordon.*

Horst was not a sloppy man. He was a thorough one. Thoroughness had a signature — it showed as symmetry when the net was wide, and it showed as overlap when the net was closing. The overlap had started Monday.

She gave them two days. Possibly three. Not four.

---

The tin was still in her pack.

She had not taken it out in four days. The last wafer was in it. She had not eaten the last wafer. She had been deciding, every morning, not to eat it — a small sustained decision she made by not opening the tin and not reaching for it, which was the only form of deciding she now trusted. Promises to herself were cheap. Motions not-made were not.

Her hands shook at the fingertips when she laced the boots. Fine tremor. She worked around it. She had laced boots with worse tremors.

---

Maren was asleep in the other bed. Dark hair across the pillow. Face slack in the way a face went slack only after three years of vigilance — the vigilance dropped, briefly, because she had judged the room safe enough to drop it in. Vane registered the trust and did not let it move her.

She crossed to the door and picked up her coat from the hook. She did not wake Maren. Waking Maren would have required explanation, and the explanation was not speakable yet.

*One hour,* she had written on the scrap of paper by the basin. *Back by second bell.*

She put the note where Maren would see it and went out.

---

Lintel Street was different in the morning.

At dusk four days ago the street had been a transaction corridor — grey, underlit, end-of-day traffic thinning toward the bridge, the rooming-house smells dying out of the air and the canal smell coming up. Now it was a working street in daylight. Carts passed both ways. A scullery girl threw a bucket of water into the gutter from a doorway. A barge captain stood outside the pawnbroker's at the corner, arguing price on a coil of rope. Three children ran past her chasing a dog.

She noted the cover and took it.

Third door from the bridge. Iron-banded. No marking. Still there — the city had not swallowed it in four days, which had been a thin private hope she had not let herself formulate.

She did not knock the pattern this time.

She crossed the street and sat at a chandler's stall four doors west and bought a wick-trimmer she did not need. She paid in coin. She waited.

The pattern she was reading now was not a search pattern. It was a production pattern. She wanted to know the schedule.

---

The door opened twice in the first hour. Once: a man in a waxed coat came out carrying a parcel. Went left, toward the bridge. Nine minutes. Returned without the parcel. Went back in. Twice: the same man again, carrying nothing, who went east along the canal path and did not return while she watched.

Not Deren. A runner. The runner was the delivery system — sealed wax pots out, some to a confectioner's or a pharmacist's or a back-room broker's; nothing on the face of them to tell a street-watcher what they were. The confectioner-pharmacist fiction was the kind of cover a Bloom chain used. She had used similar covers herself, on the other side of the transaction, for years.

After the second run, the door stayed closed.

She waited another forty minutes.

It stayed closed.

*Runner out. Deren either upstairs at the desk or out himself. No third body up there I've seen.*

The door-body from four days ago was not on the street. He worked afternoons, probably. The morning was thinly staffed. She had been betting on this and the bet had come in.

She crossed the street.

---

She did not knock the pattern. She knocked like a woman who had come for household service and had been sent to the wrong door.

It opened an inch.

"Who?" A man's voice. Not Deren. Younger. An unfamiliar runner, or a house-boy.

"I was sent from the harbor clerk's office."

"Wrong house."

"Two-one-five Lintel. The clerk's register says Deren at two-one-five."

"Wrong house."

She leaned in, the way a lost stranger leaned in — irritated but harmless, the body-shape of a woman whose morning had already gone badly and who wanted a straight answer before she had to walk back in her good shoes to be told by the clerk she had misread the register.

He opened another inch.

She went in on the inch.

---

He was young — twenty-two, twenty-three. Half a head taller than her. She had the inside of the door before he had his balance back, and she had his arm behind him and her knee in the back of his knee before he had the breath for the sound he would otherwise have made. She put her mouth near his ear.

"Quiet," she said. "This is not about you."

He was not trained. He stiffened and then loosened, which was what untrained people did when a professional told them to be quiet. She guided him to the wall and pressed him flat and held him there with forearm across his shoulders.

"Where's Deren."

"Upstairs."

"How many."

"Just him."

"You're lying."

"Just him." She felt the truth of it in the tension. A liar lied with the neck and the gut; a truth-teller lied, if he lied, with the voice. This one was neck and gut. "He's got the ledger. He's — the morning's slow."

"Door at the end of the hall — the basement. That's the one I want."

"Yes."

"How many down there."

"Just her." He hesitated. She pressed. "Just her. I swear. I don't — I don't go down."

"You've been told you don't go down."

"Yes."

"Key."

"On him. Deren. Upstairs."

*Of course it is,* she thought, and did not say. Deren's kind of man kept the key on his body because keys were trust, and trust was paid out, not given.

She took a length of soft rope from her coat pocket — she had packed it this morning without allowing herself to review what the packing implied — and she bound the boy's wrists behind him and lowered him to sit against the wall, and she stuffed a wadded kerchief into his mouth and tied the ends behind his head. She did it efficiently. She did not hurt him. He watched her the whole time with the flat dumb fear of a dog that had been beaten without being told why.

She put her face in front of his.

"Nothing happens to you today," she said. "Not by me. Sit here."

He nodded.

She went up the stairs.

---

The stairs to the upper floor creaked on the fourth and seventh steps. She had been counting them in her head from the street, and the count matched her estimate.

The door at the top was ajar.

She listened for the space of three breaths.

A pen on paper. A man's cough. The small dry cough of a man who had drunk nothing yet this morning and was clearing a night's stale throat. A cup being set down on wood.

She pushed the door open.

Deren looked up from his ledger.

He was older than she had pictured from the one short meeting four days ago. Early fifties, possibly. His eyes read her quickly and were still.

She saw him decide not to reach for whatever was at his right hand under the desk. She saw the decision happen and approved of it. He was a merchant, not a fighter. He had made his professional peace with the fact long ago.

"I remember you," he said.

"I remember the house."

She did not move into the room. She stood in the doorway with her weight on her back foot and her right hand at her side, and she let him finish what his face was doing. His face finished. It arrived at the small slack settling of a man who understood that the next hour was not going to be his hour.

"The key," Vane said.

"Which."

"The one to the basement door."

He looked at her. A long look. Older than fear. Not pleading. Not bargaining. The look of a man taking the measure of a turn in his day.

"If I give it to you," he said, "what then."

"You sit at that desk. You do not stand up for a full count of two hundred. I leave."

"And if I do not give it to you."

"Then I take it. And you sit at that desk anyway."

She said it without heat. The flatness of it was the argument.

He reached into his collar — slowly, his hand open to show her he was not reaching for anything else — and he drew up a cord from which hung a single iron key with a square bow. He lifted the cord over his head. He laid it on the ledger.

"The girl is worth something to you," he said.

"No," Vane said. "She isn't."

She took the key. She did not put the cord over her head. She put the cord in her pocket with her thumb on the key so she would know by touch it was still there. She backed out of the room the way she had come in, watching him, and she went down the stairs without her weight registering on the fourth step or the seventh.

---

The boy was where she had left him. His eyes tracked her as she crossed the hall.

The door at the end of the hall was stone-framed, iron-banded, locked. The lock was old — an old wardlock whose key was the one Deren had handed her. She tried it. It turned.

She opened the door and did not go down immediately. She stood at the top of the stairs and listened.

Copper-flower.

It came up the stairwell at volume, the same smell from four days ago, biological, thick, the scent Bloom made when it was not yet the wafer. Her body — her professional instruments, her fieldwork body, the body she had spent ten years and four years of Bloom refining into the tool she had used for everything — reacted to the smell with the small pulse of *yes.* The tremor in her fingertips stilled for the length of a breath. Her shoulders went loose.

She marked the reaction. She did not act on it.

She went down.

---

The corridor was the corridor she had been seeing for months. She did not have to orient to it. Her body knew where the lamps were.

The room at the end. The door, ajar. The same pallet. The same low ceiling. The same condensation trays on the same table.

The woman was awake.

That was new. Four days ago she had been in the induced sleep, her breathing thick and slow, her fused fingers twitching in her dreams. Today her eyes were open. They tracked Vane as she entered the room with the same slow deliberate movement that a small animal used when it was deciding whether you were a danger it could outrun.

The mutations were the same. The frond-fingers, the translucency at temples and wrists. The small white scar at the corner of her mouth was the same. She was thinner than she had been four days ago. Four days was not long enough for hunger to have caused that; it was the slow sustained thinness of someone whose body was being tapped at a rate that exceeded her ability to replace what was taken.

Vane crossed the room. She did not speak yet. She knelt beside the pallet.

The woman's breathing altered at her approach — not into fear, not into hope, but into a held stillness, the held stillness of a person who had learned that hope and fear were both expensive and had stopped spending.

*Junie.* The private word from four days ago, still there in Vane's head, still doing no work but the one work of making it impossible to pretend the person on the pallet was not a person.

"Can you walk," Vane said.

The woman's throat worked. "A little."

Her voice was dry and cracked and small. She had not used it often.

"I'm getting you out of this room. We are going to move slowly. If you have to stop, tell me. If anyone is in the hall, I will handle it. Do you understand."

"Why."

The word was not accusation. It was exhaustion putting forward the last honest question it had.

Vane took the restraint at the woman's right wrist between her fingers and began to unbuckle it. Her fingers were steady enough for the work. The buckle was padded in clean cloth. Whoever changed the cloth kept things clean.

"Because the supply chain I was in ends with you on that pallet," Vane said. She did not look up from the buckle. "I'm not asking you to come with me. I'm asking you to come up the stairs and into the street. Once you're in the street, what happens next is yours. If you want to go, go. If you want to come with me to a place where you can sit down and be given water and not be chained to a pallet for some hours, I can give you that too. I have one friend in this city. She will not touch you without your leaving. Her room is two streets from here."

The woman watched her. Her eyes were grey-green and very tired.

"Who are you."

"I've been using you," Vane said. "For four years. Not you. Someone like you. Now I'm stopping."

"And this is stopping."

"This is stopping."

The woman closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, her pupils had contracted, and some small coiled thing in her had uncoiled, and she lifted her free wrist for Vane to unbuckle the second restraint herself.

---

They went up the stairs slowly. The woman leaned on Vane's right arm. Vane held her up with the bark-skin side of her body, the side that felt least like skin, which was the side she used for loads she did not want to feel through. The woman weighed almost nothing.

The boy in the hall watched them pass. His eyes had the wet shine of a person who had not known, exactly, what lived behind the door at the end of the hall, and who was now putting it together and would not sleep tonight.

At the street door Vane paused. She listened. Cart traffic. A boy shouting about eels. A woman laughing. No rhythm that did not belong.

She opened the door.

The light was hard after the cellar. The woman flinched and then did not flinch, the flinch overridden by a deeper stillness, the way a body held a pain it did not yet have energy to acknowledge.

"This way," Vane said.

They crossed the street at the pace of a sister supporting a sister who had had too much at breakfast. Vane had practiced the pace. She did not know whether the woman had practiced anything in a long time, but the woman matched her.

They turned off Lintel at the first alley and kept going.

---

Three blocks in, at a small public square with a dry fountain, the woman stopped.

"I'm not coming with you," she said. "To your friend."

"All right."

"I have a cousin in the fishing villages south. Two days on the coast road. I have a coin in my shoe that my mother sewed in before they took me. I have been saving it for this."

Vane looked at her. The woman looked back, and her face had in it the thin hard clarity of a person who had been composing this moment for longer than she had any right to.

"I do not know you," the woman said. "I do not want to know you. I thank you and I am going."

"Can I give you anything."

The woman considered.

"A coat."

Vane took her own coat off her shoulders and put it around the woman's. It was too large. The bark-skin on her right arm met the air and the air met the bark-skin and the shiver she had not let herself have caught her once and passed.

"A name," the woman said. "For the road. In case someone asks. Not yours. Mine. I need one."

"Junie," Vane said. It came out before she thought about it.

The woman looked at her.

"That's not mine."

"I know."

"But I'll use it."

She adjusted the coat across her shoulders. She turned. She walked across the square without hurry and disappeared into the mouth of the alley on the far side.

Vane watched her go.

She stayed until she could not hear the woman's steps.

---

She walked.

Not the long way this time. A direct line through the Crafting Quarter, past the Inkwalk, east along a warehouse row, three turns to clear the residual tailing she had not seen but was not going to trust the absence of, and up to the rooming house.

Halfway there the shiver came back. The air was cool; the morning had warmed but not enough. Without the coat her right arm was exposed from shoulder to wrist — the bark-skin a continuous grain from nearly the shoulder-joint down almost to the bend of her wrist, the newest edge thickest just below the ridge of her shoulder where this season's advance had run the grain toward the collarbone. Visible for anyone to see. She pulled her sleeve down as far as it would go over the bark and held it there with her thumb.

The patch at the back of her right wrist felt different under her thumb.

Not the raised dark grain she had been closing on with a thumb for months. A drier patch. Slightly flaking at one edge. The whorls less tight. She pressed it, and the texture gave a little under the press — a sand-grain tactility, minor, minor — and then she released it and kept walking, because she was not ready to ask her body what it was telling her.

*Data,* she thought. *Just data. Put it in the log and keep moving.*

It was not moving her the way observations usually moved her. It was resisting filing. She wanted to feel it as absolution, and the wanting was what made her suspect it; she wanted to feel it as cost, and the cost was not yet legible.

She kept walking.

---

By the time she reached the rooming house her stomach had turned twice. A mild nausea. The light was too bright at the angle the street lay at. Her hearing was not right — not loud, not muffled, but misallocated, some frequencies standing out and others missing, as if someone had lifted a few of the middle strings off a harp and the chords she knew were coming back to her with the holes where those strings had been.

The shakes were in both hands.

*Hour four,* she thought. *Withdrawal begins with a forward edge. Nausea. Tremor. Sensory misallocation. Standard presentation.*

She catalogued it. The cataloguing was itself a reduced version of the thing she used to do with her cataloguing — not the clean analytical filing that had once served her, but the effortful pretense of it, the form of filing she had learned so well it continued to function even as the function underneath it failed.

That was withdrawal too. It came for the tools first.

She climbed the stairs.

---

Maren was at the table. The note was in her hand. She did not stand when Vane came in. She looked at her.

"I went back," Vane said. Her voice came out steady. That part still worked. "She's out. She's gone south. She doesn't want to come with us and I didn't try to change her mind."

Maren set the note down.

"How bad."

"Starting."

"Tell me."

Vane went to her bed and sat. The weight of sitting was a relief that registered in her knees before it registered anywhere else. She laid her hands flat on her thighs the way she had seen Aldric do last night in the cooperage basement — a borrowed gesture, she noted, because her body had not been making gestures of its own today and had been borrowing from what was in the room.

"Nausea. Tremor. Hearing's off. I'll be less functional for —" she stopped, because she did not know for how long, and saying an estimate she did not have would have cost her something she could not afford to lose. "I'll be less functional."

"How long before you're flat."

"Three days. Four."

"Before you're back."

"Weeks. Maybe a month. The literature is —" She almost said *thin.* She said: "inconclusive."

Maren nodded. Her face did the small thing it did when she was composing herself around information she did not like. It did not hide the composing. That was one of the things about her that kept Vane off-balance: Maren did not hide her adjustments, and the not-hiding was somehow the toughest part of her.

"Horst," Maren said.

"Closing. Two days. Three at the outside."

"Then we leave tomorrow."

It was not a question. Maren had read the shape of the situation before Vane arrived and had already done the arithmetic, and the arithmetic had landed where Vane's had landed, and the fact of their both landing in the same place was the most useful thing that had happened in this room in a week.

"Tomorrow," Vane said. "The hill is five days east."

Maren's eyes held hers.

"The Keeper?" Maren said.

"He'll come."

"You're certain."

"His hand stopped a span from the disc last night. He isn't going home."

Maren absorbed this. She did not ask how Vane had read it through a basement window from the gate. It was sufficient that Vane had read it.

"Davan?"

"Davan will come."

"All right."

The two words. The two words Maren had used with Vane in this room four nights ago when Vane had said *I'm stopping.* They arrived with the same weight now. Something had settled.

Vane let her head down against the headboard.

"Water," she said. "If there's water."

Maren brought water. Vane drank it. The shake of her hand made the cup ring against her teeth, and she set the cup down with the care of a woman setting down an object she no longer trusted herself to hold.

---

The afternoon went. She did not track it well.

At some point Maren went out and came back with a packet of dried ginger root and a small sack of something from the herbalist at the end of the street. At some point a message went — Vane saw Maren write it and saw a boy come for it at the door — to the cooperage, for Davan, or for Marrick, or for both. At some point the message came back: one word on a folded paper, which Maren read and put into her pocket without showing Vane.

Vane dozed and did not dream, which was a small mercy. When she woke the light had changed. Evening. Maren was at the table repacking a traveling bag with the economy of someone who had packed it several times in her life already.

Vane watched her from the bed. Her right arm, uncovered by sleep, lay across the blanket. She looked at the bark-skin in the dusk light.

The patch at the back of her wrist was the same as it had been on the walk home. Drier. A little looser at one edge.

She did not look closer. She looked for the length of a breath and looked away.

It was not for her to examine yet. Looking at it too hard would be an asking, and what she was asking for she was not entitled to ask for, and the bark-skin — she had learned — responded to expectation the way some things did. She would not offer it an expectation. She would let it do what it was doing and not name it until the thing had a shape she could name without flattering herself.

She turned the arm under the blanket.

---

Davan came at dusk.

He came up the stairs quietly and knocked quietly and Maren let him in. He looked at Vane and he did not say anything about how she looked. The wash of color at his collar was blue, calm, and held without fluctuation. She appreciated that. Calm from him was worth more than reassurance from most people.

"Edvar's bringing his trunk," he said. "The face goes with us. He won't leave it behind."

"Horse?" Maren said.

"He has a pair. And a cart. He says the cart doesn't go past the ford but we'll figure what to do then."

"Marrick is coming."

"He's coming. Tela's riding decoy through the east gate. One of the Inkwalk boys with her."

Vane turned her face toward the wall. Her eyes had started to water for no reason she could name. She closed them.

"Aldric?" Maren said.

"Aldric's at the cooperage tonight. He's going to lodge there until we move. He — he asked Marrick if the iron-banded trunk was a burden he should take on his horse because he has a stronger horse than Marrick's."

"Did Marrick agree."

"He agreed."

Vane heard them talking. Their voices were a distance away that was not spatial. She kept her eyes closed and let the talk go on over her, and she catalogued the textures of the voices — Maren's flat-edge steadiness, Davan's open-grained circling that returned to points without forcing them, the silence between sentences where each of them was thinking — and the cataloguing was a comfort and a loss in the same gesture.

She had used to be able to pick up this much from a smell across a room. Tonight she was working from what was being said. The tool she had been for ten years, the instrument she had sharpened and overclocked with the Bloom, was going.

*Not going,* she corrected. *Retrograding. Back to what it was before. My nose. Which still works. Just not for what I've been asking it to do.*

The correction was a kindness she allowed herself. She did not fully believe it. It would become truer over weeks. She would have to wait.

---

They went on talking. At some point Davan laid a hand briefly on the blanket near her knee — not on her knee, on the blanket, the gesture of a man placing a warmth where a warmth could be taken or not taken — and she did not reach for it, and he did not move it. He left it there for the length of a breath and then took it away, and the fact that he had not required anything of her for putting it there was one of the things she filed without commentary, because he had now done several things she would have filed without commentary if she had been keeping a file on him, and it was becoming clear that she was.

He went at second bell. The door closed behind him. Maren turned the lamp down.

---

She lay in the dark a long time.

The plan was in her head, exactly, the way plans had always been in her head: five days east along the highland road; the ford crossing, not the east gate; morning departure; four riders, one cart, a trunk with a face in it, a disc in a small dark-haired woman's coat pocket, a Keeper whose hand had stopped at a span from the answer and was now traveling with them toward the longer answer; and somewhere behind them, at a distance she had estimated and would re-estimate every morning, a professional she had trained with for three seasons in a previous life, closing on a city that would empty of their scent by noon tomorrow and would start to empty of her scent the moment they crossed the ford.

She would not smell him coming. That was the cost. She had been the one who smelled, and now she would not.

She thought about that for a while, and then she thought about the woman in the square, the too-large coat, the cousin south along the coast road, the coin a mother had sewn into a shoe against an unreachable future and the daughter who had carried that shoe and that coin through years of captivity and had walked out today with them and with a coat and with a name that was not hers.

Then she thought about her arm. Just once. Under the blanket.

The drier patch was still drier. The edge was still loose. The body underneath the grain was doing something she had not asked it to do, and the something was not nothing, and it was not for her tonight to decide what kind of something it was.

She put her good hand over the bark-skin through the blanket, not pressing, only covering, and she lay there with her hand on her arm and listened to Maren's breath in the other bed even out and deepen into sleep. She listened to the street sounds through the shutter. A late carter. A dog. A drunk somewhere singing a song she had last heard on a road eleven years ago.

*Tomorrow,* she thought. *Four of us. Five days east. A cart, a trunk, a disc, a plan.*

The patch on the back of her right wrist, unexamined under her palm, flaked a little more overnight. She knew this without looking. She would know it when she washed in the morning. She would not mention it.

She slept after a while, shallow, the body insisting on its minimum.

The three moons were over the harbor outside, at their stations, doing what they had always done.

She did not see them. She was under a roof. She slept, and the roof held, and the plan was a quiet shape in her chest, and the flake at the edge of the bark-patch was a smaller quieter shape under her palm, and between the two the night passed and the city breathed and tomorrow came on.
