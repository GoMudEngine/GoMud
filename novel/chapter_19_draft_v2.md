## Chapter Nineteen — *[Davan]*
### What Edvar Keeps

---

The shop smelled of linseed and old paper — not the sharp chemical bite of fresh oil but the settled, sweet undertone of decades of it soaked into wood and plaster. The walls themselves had absorbed the work done here. Davan thought of tree rings, how a trunk held its years in layers you could only see once it was cut.

Edvar let him in without speaking. That was new. The first three visits had each begun with some small exchange at the threshold — a comment about the weather, or the state of the harbor road, or once a dry observation about the quality of Davan's boots. Tonight the mapmaker simply stepped aside and gestured him through.

The front room was as Davan remembered: the broad drafting table under the north-facing window, the racks of pigment jars along the far wall sorted by hue, the rolled charts in their vertical cubbies like a library built sideways. Tools hung from pegs in an order that was not alphabetical or chronological but made sense to the hand that reached for them — compass, ruling pen, divider, stylus, each within the arc of a seated man's arm. A workspace shaped by forty years of the same motions.

But Edvar walked past the drafting table. Past the pigment racks. He went to the back wall, where a curtain separated the shop from his living quarters, and held it aside.

Davan had not been past the curtain before.

"Boots are fine," Edvar said, reading the hesitation. "Floor's just stone."

The back room was smaller than Davan expected. A cot against one wall, made up with the tight corners of a man who lived alone and saw no reason to be loose about it. A washstand. A shelf of books — not many, but handled often, their spines softened. A single chair near a cold hearth. The room of someone who spent most of his life in the front half of the building and came here only to sleep.

There was a trunk at the foot of the cot. Iron-banded, the wood dark with age, a lock that Edvar opened with a key he wore on a cord inside his shirt. Davan watched him kneel — slowly, one knee at a time, the way a man in his sixties negotiated with a floor — and lift the lid.

Inside, nested in a wrapping of oiled canvas, was something flat and dark.

Edvar lifted it out with both hands. He set it on the cot and folded the canvas back, and the object was roughly the size of a serving tray — rectangular, perhaps two handspans by three, and thin. The surface was dark. Not black, exactly. Not the black of ink or iron or char. A dark that seemed to go down into the material rather than sit on top of it. Light from the single lamp touched it and did not come back.

Davan's skin pulsed once, low amber, at the wrists. He was aware of it and did not try to suppress it. He had learned weeks ago that suppressing made it worse.

"Twenty years," Edvar said. He was still kneeling beside the cot, looking at the face with an expression Davan could not immediately read. Then he could: it was the look of a man opening a door he had kept locked, unsure whether the air on the other side would still be breathable. "I found it in a building collapse on the eastern ridge. The old quarter — before the fire took most of it. There was a cellar that had been sealed. Stones had come down and broken the floor open, and it was in the rubble."

He did not say *I knew what it was.* He did not say *I didn't know what it was.* The omission sat between those two statements like a third option: *I knew it was something.*

"You can touch it," Edvar said.

Davan knelt beside him. The stone floor was cold through his trousers. Something registered — not cold, not warm, but present. The same quality as Corvel's container at the cooperage meeting, the proximity-sense that was not temperature but registered in the same place temperature did. Something in the face was aware of the space around it, or held a residue of awareness, the way Edvar's front room held the smell of linseed.

He reached out and put his fingertips on the surface.

---

The dark lit.

Not all at once. It began at the point of contact — a pale light spreading from where his fingers pressed, cold and precise, nothing like lamplight. Lamplight was warm and uneven and alive. This was something else. This was light that had been made, designed, given exact properties and held to them. It spread across the surface in a wash that took perhaps three seconds, and then the entire face of the object was illuminated from within.

Davan did not pull his hand away. His skin-light had gone to blue at his throat and was climbing toward something else — building at his collarbones, not yet arrived.

The image assembled itself.

First: a sphere. Blue-green, not large — the size of his closed fist, rendered in the cold light at the center of the dark surface. It turned slowly, and he could see the suggestion of landmasses, oceans, the marbled texture of atmosphere. A world. He knew it the way he knew his own palm lines — not because he had studied it, but because he had always been standing on it.

Then the shapes appeared.

They came in from the edges, or rather the image widened to include them — the perspective pulling back as if the viewer were rising, lifting away from the surface of the blue-green sphere to reveal what moved around it. Orbiting. Shapes in fixed paths, each tracing a line that the cold light rendered as a faint curve.

One. Large, steady, its path a clean circle.

Two. Smaller, closer to the world, moving faster.

Three. Between the other two, its orbit slightly tilted.

Four.

The fourth shape. Smaller, as if further away. As if it had always been the most distant of them. Its orbit was wider than the others, a long slow track around the blue-green world, and its shape — where the first three were rendered as distinct forms, each with enough detail to suggest a body with surface and mass — the fourth was reduced. A point that was almost a shape. Something that had been there when this record was made, caught at the limits of whatever instrument or eye had captured the arrangement.

Davan's skin went green-gold.

Not a pulse. Not the quick flash that came with a sudden feeling and faded. This was sustained — a glow that started at his wrists and moved up his forearms and across his collarbones and settled. It was the color of recognition, the register his body kept for the moments when something he had already known made itself visible. Like the carvings. Like the symbol he had been cutting into pine and cedar for months without knowing what it was, his hands working out a shape that his mind had not yet assembled.

Four.

He counted them again. One, two, three, four. The three he had always known — the three that anyone who looked up at night could count, the moons that moved through the sky in their ancient arrangement. And the fourth. Smaller, further, captured in a record that was older than anything he had ever touched.

"Four," he said. His voice was steady. The green-gold held.

Edvar was watching him, not the face. Davan could feel the quality of that attention — the mapmaker studying him the way he studied coastlines, looking for the features that confirmed or denied what his measurements had predicted.

"The margin notations," Davan said. "The old maps. The ones that show four."

"Yes."

"They weren't wrong."

"No."

Davan looked at the face again. The four shapes continued their orbits, the cold light tracing paths that had been fixed into whatever this material was — fixed and held and waiting for a touch to release them. He thought about how long they had been waiting. Twenty years in Edvar's trunk. Before that, sealed in a cellar beneath a building that had collapsed. Before that —

The fourth shape. Smaller, as if further away. But not further away, not really. The other three were still in the sky. He saw them every night. Whatever this record held was the arrangement as it had been, and the arrangement now held only three.

Something that had been in orbit was no longer in orbit.

"It came down," Davan said.

---

The silence that followed was not empty. It was the silence of a room in which something had been said that could not be unsaid, and both men in it understood this, and neither moved to fill the space with words that would only dilute what the words had done.

Edvar lowered himself from kneeling to sitting. His back found the side of the cot. He looked at the face — still lit, still turning, the four shapes in their patient loops — and something crossed the mapmaker's face: the expression of a man whose half-thought had just been finished by someone else's voice. Davan knew that look. He'd worn it.

"Twenty years," Edvar said again. But this time the words carried a different weight. Not *I have kept this for twenty years.* Rather: *For twenty years I have known what this meant and have not let myself say it.*

"You saw the four," Davan said. "Every time you opened the trunk. You counted them."

"Yes."

"And you looked up at the sky and counted three."

Edvar's hands were resting on his knees. They were the hands of a man who worked with fine instruments — steady, deliberate, the fingers long and spotted with age. They did not tremble. But there was something coming off the mapmaker that was not visible in his body — a vibration in the emotional register, the frequency of something long held finally being set down.

"I told myself it could be an error in the recording," Edvar said. "A flaw in the material. An artifact of age. I had seventeen plausible explanations for why the record showed four when the sky showed three, and I tested each of them against every old chart and margin notation I could find, and none of them held." He paused. "The fourth shape is consistent across every source I've found that predates the Quiet Century. After that period, every source shows three. The transition is clean. Whatever happened, happened within a single generation."

"Within living memory," Davan said. "Someone saw it happen."

"Someone saw it happen. And then, apparently, everyone agreed not to record it."

They sat with that. The cold light of the face painted them both in blue-white, and Davan's green-gold glow mixed with it, casting the small room in colors that did not belong to any lamp or hearth. He thought about the shape he had been carving. The orbital symbol. His hands had known, working it out of wood the way water worked its way through stone — not by force but by patience, by returning and returning to the same surface until the shape emerged.

The fourth shape on the face was not damaged. Not from this angle, this recording. It was simply distant and small, a body on a wide orbit, far enough out that the instrument or eye that had made this record could barely resolve it. But it was there. It was whole. It was in the sky.

And then it was not.

"Something that size," Davan said slowly, "coming down."

"Yes."

"The impact."

"I've spent years on the calculations." Edvar's voice had shifted — not louder or faster, but denser, the way a man spoke when he was finally using words he had rehearsed in solitude for decades. "The mass required to sustain an orbit of that period. The energy of a descent. Even a partial descent — even a fragment. The numbers are beyond what I have language for. Beyond what anyone in the cooperage group has language for, and several of them are better mathematicians than I am."

"But you've never told them."

"No."

"Why?"

Edvar looked at him. The expression was complex — layered, like the linseed smell, built up over years. Davan read it as best he could. There was protectiveness in it, and something like shame, and beneath both of those a loneliness so deep and so long-held that it had become structural. Part of the foundation. The man had built his life around a room that held a secret he could not share because sharing it meant completing a thought he could not face alone.

"Because I am a mapmaker," Edvar said. "I draw what is there. I record coastlines and elevations and the positions of landmarks relative to fixed points. I do not speculate. I do not theorize. I observe and I render and I verify." He looked down at his hands. "And for twenty years I have been in possession of an observation I cannot render on any map. A thing that was in the sky and is no longer in the sky. And the only conclusion that the evidence supports is that it came down, and that when it came down, it —"

He stopped.

Davan waited. The green-gold at his wrists pulsed once, gently, and then held steady again.

"— it changed everything," Edvar finished. "The Quiet Century. The gaps in the records. The things we've lost and can't account for losing. If something of that mass struck the surface or broke apart in descent, the consequences would have been —" He shook his head. Not a negation. A man reaching the limit of what solitary thought could carry. "I couldn't complete the sentence. I could never complete the sentence. I would get to that point and stop and wrap it up and put it back in the trunk."

"For twenty years."

"For twenty years."

Davan did not say *I understand.* He did not say *that must have been difficult.* He sat beside the mapmaker on the cold stone floor and let the silence hold what words would have only reduced. The face still turned between them, the four shapes in their ancient, patient orbits, and outside the shop the real sky held three.

---

The lamp had burned down by half before either of them spoke again. Davan had spent the time in the way he spent most of his thinking time — letting his attention move across the details of the room, the textures and proportions, the way the cold light from the face interacted with the warm light from the wick. His thoughts circled. The orbital symbol he'd been carving. The margin notations — *four* written in faded ink on charts so old the paper crumbled at the edges. The cooperage group's arguments about bloodline capacity and unchanged hands. Maren's disc, warm with something that was not temperature. The gray container Corvel had shown them, the one nobody could open, made of a material nobody could identify.

The container.

The thought arrived before he had words for it. A connection forming the way a shape formed under his knife — not all at once, but by the slow removal of everything that was not the shape, until what remained was clear.

"The container," he said.

Edvar turned his head. He had been looking at the face, or through it, in the way of a man whose eyes were focused on something that was not in the room.

"Corvel's container," Davan said. "The gray material. The way it absorbs light instead of reflecting it."

He put his hand on the surface of the face again. Not to activate it — it was still lit — but to feel the material itself. The substance of it. The way it took the light from the lamp and did not give it back. The way it sat in his hand with a density that was not stone, not metal, not wood, not anything he had worked with.

"It's the same," he said.

He looked at Edvar.

The mapmaker's expression was one Davan would remember for a long time afterward. He had seen many faces in the weeks since arriving in New Plymouth — the guarded scrutiny of Corvel, the sharp intelligence of the cooperage group members, Maren's complex stillness at the yard. But he had not seen this. This was the face of a man who had been sitting alone with a question for two decades, turning it over in his solitude, knowing what it meant and unable to say it — and who was now looking at someone who had walked into his shop and, in the space of an evening, asked the question he had been waiting for.

Not surprise. Not relief, exactly. Something older than either. The recognition of company after a very long time alone.

"Yes," Edvar said.

"The same material."

"I believe so. I have believed so since Corvel first brought it to the group, eight years ago. I held it in my hands and I felt —" He gestured at the face. "This. The same quality. The same way it sits against the skin. He found it in the marshland south of the ridge, half-buried in clay that the geological surveys suggest was laid down during the Quiet Century."

"During the impact window."

The words landed in the room like stones dropped into still water. Davan heard himself say them and understood that he had completed another of Edvar's sentences — not because he was presumptuous, but because the sentence had been waiting a long time to be finished and the man who had started it could not do it alone.

"During the impact window," Edvar repeated. Quietly. Testing the words in his own mouth. "Yes."

"So the container isn't from here."

"No. I don't think it is."

"And this —" Davan's fingers rested on the face, the cold light washing over his knuckles, his green-gold glow mixing with the blue-white. "This isn't from here either."

"I found it in a sealed cellar. Old construction — older than the colony records for that district. The building above it was already a ruin when it collapsed. Whatever was down there had been down there for a very long time."

They looked at each other across the lit surface of the face, and the full architecture of what they were sitting with settled into place. Not a revelation. Not the dramatic unveiling of a hidden truth. Something quieter and heavier: the slow, thorough understanding that the world they stood on had been struck by a piece of its own sky, and that the strike had left debris, and that the debris was not rock.

The face was a made thing. The container was a made thing. Whatever had occupied that fourth orbital position had not been a natural body — not a moon of stone and ice, tumbling through space on a path set by gravity alone. It had been something else. Something that could produce a flat dark surface that responded to touch and rendered light with a precision that no lampmaker or glassblower on this world could match.

The green-gold at Davan's collarbones flared and held. The recognition-register, his body's way of acknowledging what his mind was still assembling. He did not fight it. He let it burn.

"The cooperage group," he said after a while. "The arguments about capacity. Unchanged hands. All of it."

"All of it," Edvar said. "Every question they've been circling for years. Where the bloodline abilities come from. Why some people carry them and others don't. Why the oldest records show things that the newer records have forgotten." He folded his hands together in his lap. "If something fell — something made, something that was not a moon — then the question isn't just what it did to the surface when it landed. The question is what it was doing in orbit before it fell. And what it left behind."

"What it left in us," Davan said.

Edvar closed his eyes. Just for a moment. When he opened them, the tiredness in his face was the tiredness of twenty years of solitude laid down all at once, and beneath it was something that might have been gratitude, if gratitude could be that heavy.

"I have never said any of this to another person," he said. "I need you to understand that."

"I understand."

"I have maps and margin notations and geological surveys and this —" he gestured at the face, still patiently displaying its ancient record, "— and I have spent two decades building a case that I could never present. Because presenting it would mean saying what you just said. That something came down. That it was made. That it changed everything. And I am a mapmaker, Davan. I draw coastlines. I am not the person who says these things."

"No," Davan agreed. "But you're the person who found the evidence. And you kept it. And you waited."

"I waited."

"For someone to ask the right question."

Edvar looked at him with that expression again — the one Davan would carry with him when he left tonight, out into the dark streets where three moons hung in a sky that had once held four. The expression of a man whose long patience had been, finally, answered.

"Yes," the mapmaker said. "For someone to ask the right question."
