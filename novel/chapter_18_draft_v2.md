## Chapter Eighteen — *Vane*
### The Bloom-Giver

---

The tin was light. She knew without opening it. Had known for
three days, the way you know a room's dimensions before the
lamp catches up — by the quality of the echo when nothing
comes back.

Vane sat on the edge of the narrow bed and held the tin in her
lap. Maren was out. Market run, she'd said, which meant
walking a pattern through the stalls near the fishmonger's
quarter where foot traffic was thick enough to dissolve a
silhouette. Good fieldcraft for someone who'd learned it from
watching rather than training. Maren picked things up. That
was the problem with her, or one of them.

The room was small and smelled of tallow and the particular
warmth Vane had stopped pretending she didn't track. Maren's
warmth. It threaded through everything now — the blanket on
the second bed, the chair back, the doorframe where she leaned
when she was thinking. Vane used it the way she used any
reliable signal. Orientation. Proximity. Threat absence.

She turned the tin over once and set it on the mattress.

The bark-skin on her right arm itched. Above the elbow now,
upper arm nearly to the shoulder, the grain creeping toward
the joint where the change would stop feeling like a sleeve
and start feeling like a shirt. She rolled back her sleeve and
looked at it in the grey light from the window — dark grain,
tight whorls, the edges angrier than last week. Redder. The
border where skin became something else was precise as a tide
line and about as negotiable. Under the grain she could feel
the arm, still: the muscle, the tendon, the pulse. Not gone.
Just wearing something she had not chosen. A tightness when
she flexed, as if a thin glove of bark had been drawn up and
stitched to her from the inside.

She rolled the sleeve down.

The contact's name was Deren. Older than Cade. Deeper current.
A name she'd picked up in Port Aelith from a woman who sold
information about information, which was the only kind of
broker Vane respected. He operated out of the docks district,
southside, where the warehouses backed up against the canal
retaining walls and the air tasted of brine and rotting hemp.

She'd sent word yesterday. The reply came back on a folded
slip tucked into the frame of the rooming house door: an
address, a time, and the figure. The figure was high. She
could meet it.

Vane checked the tin one more time with a brief precise motion,
thumb across the clasp. Confirmed what she already knew. Two
wafers left, maybe. Or one. Or the dust of one.

She pocketed it, pulled on her coat, and went out.

---

The docks district in late afternoon was a study in practiced
disinterest. Longshoremen finishing second shift. Cart traffic
thinning as the chandlers closed up. Women with baskets moving
toward home with the focused stride of people who'd stopped
finding the city interesting years ago. Vane matched their
pace. Coat collar up, hands in pockets, right hand curled
around the tin because the tremor was worse in open air and
she didn't need to advertise it.

The address was on Lintel Street, which ran parallel to the
canal before ducking under a stone footbridge and becoming
something narrower and less named. The buildings here were
old — pre-colonial stonework, the kind the city had paved over
and built on top of and then grudgingly preserved when the
foundations turned out to be the only thing keeping the canal
bank from sliding. Vane counted doorways. Third from the
bridge. Iron-banded. No marking.

She knocked the pattern from the slip.

The door opened inward. The man behind it was short, heavy
through the shoulders, with the flat affect of someone whose
job was to be a surface between the street and whatever lay
behind him. He looked at her hands. She showed them, palms
up, and he stepped aside.

Stone stairs going down. Of course. The canal district was
built on top of the old quarter, and the old quarter was built
on top of what came before that, and at some point you stopped
calling it a basement and started calling it what it was. Vane
went down.

The smell hit her on the seventh step.

Copper-flower. Bloom. But not the refined note she knew from
the wafers — not the compressed, preserved version that sat
on the tongue like a petal pressed in a book. This was the
smell at volume. At source. The way a tannery smells nothing
like a leather glove. It filled the stairwell like a
temperature, warm and thick and unmistakably biological.

Her hands went flat against the walls. Both of them. The
tremor had stopped, which it did sometimes when her body
decided to be afraid before she gave it permission.

She kept going down.

The corridor at the bottom was stone, low-ceilinged, lit by
two oil lamps mounted in iron brackets. She knew this
corridor. She'd been seeing it for months — in the visions
that came with the Bloom episodes, the involuntary ones, the
flashes she'd catalogued and cross-referenced and filed
without ever asking the obvious question about where the
images were coming from. This was the corridor. The lamps were
in the right places. The ceiling was exactly as low as she'd
seen it, close enough to brush her hair if she stood straight.

A door at the end, ajar. Behind it, a room.

Vane stopped walking.

She could hear someone breathing. Not Deren — Deren would be
upstairs or elsewhere, the way suppliers were always elsewhere
when the product was being shown. The breathing was slow and
thick and had a quality to it she'd heard once before. In
Cade's back room in Harrowgate. Through a wall. *If she
survives the first month.*

She pushed the door open.

---

The room was eight paces by six. She measured it because
measuring was what she did when she couldn't do anything else.
Stone walls, stone floor, stone ceiling at the height she
already knew. A pallet against the far wall. A drain in the
floor. A table with collection apparatus — glass, copper
tubing, clay vessels sealed with wax. The lamps in here were
turned low.

The person on the pallet was hollow.

Vane knew this the way she'd learned to know it over the past
months — by the quality of the skin, by the particular
translucency at the temples and inner wrists, by the faint
luminescence that hollows carried like a watermark. This
person's luminescence was dim. Banked. The mutations were
extensive — limbs elongated, joints thickened, fingers fused
past the second knuckle into something that looked more like
frond than hand. A strand of dark hair had come loose from
where someone had tied it back and lay across her cheek. The
features under it were still readable as human: young, with
a narrow nose and a small white scar at the corner of the
mouth, the kind you got as a child from falling wrong onto a
step. Vane looked. She made herself look.

*Junie,* the thought arrived, unbidden — not a name she knew
this woman by, not a name at all, just the kind of name a
farmhand girl might have had before someone took her
somewhere.

Female. Young, or had been. Breathing the slow thick breath of
induced sleep. Restraints at the wrists, padded with cloth
that had been changed recently — Deren or whoever ran this
kept things clean, which was worse, somehow, than filth would
have been. The copper-flower smell was overwhelming here. It
came from the skin. From the pores. The secretions that
hollows produced during sleep, the ones Vane had spent years
consuming in pressed wafer form with a cup of tea in the
morning like it was a habit and not a transaction.

She sorted. The operational part of her brain did what it
always did — inputs, outputs, cost structure, product
architecture — because that was the only way to stand in this
room and keep standing. The collection apparatus was designed
for continuous harvest. Condensation trays positioned along
the body. Copper tubing running to a settling vessel. The
wax-sealed clay pots on the table were the intermediate
product — raw secretion before pressing and refinement. The
wafers in her tin had started in a room like this. On a body
like this.

She had known this.

Not the address. Not the face. But the fact. She'd known since
Harrowgate, since Cade's back room, since the phrase that had
lodged in her like a splinter — *if she survives the first
month* — and she'd kept buying. She'd known since the Bloom
episodes had started showing her this exact ceiling, this
exact quality of lamplight, this exact breathing, and she'd
catalogued the visions and cross-referenced them and never
once followed the thread to its source because following the
thread would have required standing where she was standing now.

The woman's chest rose and fell. The condensation trays
caught the light. Her fused fingers twitched once in sleep
and were still.

Vane turned around and walked back up the stairs.

---

The man at the door looked at her as she came up. His face did
the thing faces do when a transaction hasn't been completed —
the slight recalibration, the question that isn't asked because
asking would be unprofessional.

"Changed my mind," Vane said.

He opened the door. She walked through it.

Lintel Street was exactly as she'd left it. The canal smelled
like a canal. Cart wheels on cobblestone. A woman with a
basket of dried cod walked past without looking at her. The
footbridge threw a shadow across the road that moved with the
late sun.

Vane crossed the street and stood against the wall of the
opposite building. The stonework was cold through her coat.
She put her hands in her pockets and found the tin and held it
and stayed.

The tremor came back. Fine, at the fingertips, the way it
always started. The bark-skin on her right arm pulsed once,
a slow deep itch that she'd stopped scratching weeks ago
because scratching didn't help and because the texture under
her nails made her think of things she didn't need to think
about.

The street changed around her the way streets do. Afternoon
foot traffic thinned. A lamplighter came through, then
doubled back, then came through again on the other side. The
shadow of the footbridge migrated across the cobblestones and
climbed the wall to her left. Vane tracked all of it — exits,
sightlines, density patterns — because the operational part
of her brain didn't stop working just because the rest of her
had. The rest of her was in the room. The rest of her was
standing in the doorway looking at the condensation trays.

A cost-benefit analysis. She was good at those.

Cost of continued use: the body on the pallet. Not
abstractly — she'd had the abstraction for months and it
hadn't stopped her. Concretely. That specific ceiling. That
specific breathing. The cloth on the restraints changed by
someone who kept things clean. The small white scar at the
corner of the mouth.

Benefit of continued use: the tracking sense. The ability to
find Maren, to read hollow proximity, to do the job she'd
assigned herself.

Cost of stopping: the episodes. The shaking. The loss of the
tracking sense, probably, though she didn't know the timeline.
Operational degradation at the worst possible moment, with
Horst in the city and Maren depending on her ability to stay
functional. The bark-skin she didn't know about. Nobody knew
what long-term Bloom use did, because long-term Bloom users
either stopped or stopped being something you could ask.

She held the tin in her pocket and felt its weight, which was
almost nothing.

*If she survives the first month.*

Vane pushed off the wall and walked.

---

She took the long way back. Not for countersurveillance,
though she ran the pattern anyway — three turns, a doubled
block, a pause at a vendor's stall she didn't see. The long
way back was for the same reason she'd stood against the wall:
she needed to finish the calculation before she arrived, because
she wouldn't do it in front of Maren, and she wouldn't do it
after, and if she didn't do it now it wouldn't get done.

The calculation was simple. She'd made it complicated for
months with operational justifications and tactical necessity
and the professional categories she used to keep things sorted,
but it was simple. She was consuming a substance made from a
captive person's body to track another person she was supposed
to be protecting. The tracking target and the production source
were the same kind. Hollow. Maren's kind. And Vane had been
using one to find the other and calling it fieldwork.

She crossed through the market square. The stalls were closing.
A fishmonger was packing ice around the unsold catch and the
smell of it cut through everything for a moment — salt and
cold and clean — and then the copper-flower was back, ghost
of it, memory of it, she couldn't tell. It didn't matter. She
could still taste the last wafer she'd taken, three days ago,
on the back of her tongue. She would probably taste it for a
while.

The rooming house was on a quiet street near the old seminary.
Vane let herself in, climbed the stairs, and stood outside the
door for a count of ten. Not composing herself. There was
nothing to compose. She was standing in the place where a
decision had been made and checking whether it was still there.

It was.

She went in.

---

Maren was at the small table by the window, mending a tear in
her sleeve with a needle and thread she'd bought at the
market. She looked up when Vane came in. Read something. Went
back to the needle for a moment, then set it down.

"You went to the docks."

Not a question. Maren had a way of stating things that made
them available for correction without requiring it. Vane had
noted this too, the way she noted other reliable signals.

"I went to a Bloom contact." Vane closed the door. Hung her
coat. Sat on the edge of her bed, which put her across from
Maren with the width of the room between them. "Different from
Cade's network. Older supplier. The address was a harvest
site."

She said it flat because flat was how it came out. Maren's
hands went still on the table.

"The substance is made from hollows." Vane's voice was level,
the way it got when she was delivering a field report that
contained information no one was going to enjoy. "Captive
hollows. The secretions produced during induced sleep.
Collected, refined, pressed into wafers. I've known the
general mechanism for months. Today I saw the specific
production."

Maren didn't speak. Her eyes were steady on Vane's face with
that quality of attention that was itself a kind of silence —
active, receiving, not filling the space.

"One person," Vane said. "In a stone room under the canal
district. Female. Heavily mutated. Restrained." She paused.
Breathed once. "The room matched the visions I've been having
during episodes. I've been seeing the source for months and
cataloguing it as sensory noise."

Maren's expression didn't change, which meant it had changed
and she was keeping it still. Vane could read that now. She
could read a lot of things about Maren now, and some of that
was the Bloom, and she didn't want to think about what it meant
that the tool she'd used to read Maren was made from someone
like Maren.

"I didn't buy any," Vane said. "I didn't free the person. I
stood in the street for —" She stopped. Checked. "A while."

"What did you decide?"

Maren's voice was even. Not gentle — Maren had the sense not
to be gentle right now, which was one of the things about her
that Vane had stopped trying to file under a professional
heading.

"I'm stopping."

Two words. They sat in the room like stones dropped on a table.

"The tracking sense may degrade. I don't know the timeline.
The tremor will get worse before it gets better. The episodes
will intensify. I'll be less operational for a period I can't
estimate. This is a poor time for it, with Horst in the
Merchant Quarter, and I'm doing it anyway."

She laid it out the way she would have laid out a logistics
problem — sequenced, with known variables and acknowledged
unknowns. Maren watched her lay it out and didn't interrupt.

"What's in your pack," Maren said. Still not a question.

Vane looked at her. "The tin. Two wafers left, maybe less."

"And you're keeping it."

"For now."

This was the part that could have become something else.
Maren could have reached for her hand. Could have said
something about courage or the right choice or any of the
phrases that people used to make decisions feel like they'd
already been completed just because they'd been spoken aloud.
She didn't. She picked up the needle, looked at it, set it
down again.

"All right," Maren said.

"There's a gap," Vane said, and then stopped because she
didn't usually explain herself and didn't know why she was
starting now. But Maren was watching with that quality of
attention, and the words came out anyway. "Between deciding
and executing. I've decided. The tin is still in my pack. I
haven't destroyed it. I'm going to sit with that for —" She
turned her right hand over on her knee and looked at the
bark-skin where it vanished under her sleeve. "Until I don't
need to anymore."

Maren nodded once.

The room was quiet. Tallow candle, thread on the table, the
two narrow beds, the window with its view of the seminary wall.
Vane's pack sat against the wall by the door with the tin
inside it, weighing almost nothing, containing almost nothing,
representing a supply chain that ended in a stone room with a
low ceiling and a woman who breathed the slow thick breath of
someone kept asleep so that what her body produced could be
scraped off and sold to people like Vane.

The tremor was back in her fingertips. She pressed her hands
flat on her knees and held them there.

Maren went back to the mending. The needle moved through the
cloth in small precise stitches, and the sound it made was the
sound of thread pulled through fabric, which was a quiet sound,
and it was enough.
