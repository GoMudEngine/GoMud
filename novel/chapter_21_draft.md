## Chapter Twenty-One — *[Aldric]*
### Convergence

---

New Plymouth announced itself a half-day out by the thickening of traffic on the highland road — carters first, then droves of penned sheep, then the long slow wagons of the inland merchants with their tarpaulins drawn tight against the coastal damp. By the time the road rose to the last low ridge and gave him the city, Aldric was riding in company without having chosen it: a Keeper on a tired horse between a wool-factor from Stillwater and a family with three children asleep in a handcart. Four days of solitary travel had thinned something in him. The human noise was a warmth he let himself take.

The city lay in the wide bowl of its river mouth, harbor opening west to the sea under a sky gone the color of pewter. Slate roofs, smoke haze, a gridwork of streets older at the center and newer toward the walls. The river cut through from the northeast and emptied into the harbor with the slow argumentative authority of a big slow water. Ships at anchor along the eastern quays, their masts crossed like a small leafless forest.

Above the ridge, in the pale sky, the moons.

He counted them without meaning to. The Steady, low and westerly, pale as watered milk. The rust-tinted one, higher and smaller. Third, near the zenith, the fastest. Three.

Three where the oldest maps counted four. Three where the face in the undercroft had shown him four. He had ridden west for four days with this count as his companion, and the count had not settled. It sat in his chest the way a lintel sits — load-bearing, unobtrusive, the thing that held the rest up — and every time he looked at the sky the lintel was there.

He pressed his heels and his horse began the descent.

---

The temple district ran along the southern ridge above the river, its buildings older and less crowded than the merchant streets below, with the deliberate spaciousness of institutions that had claimed their ground early and never been asked to yield it. The Keeper's House was a narrow stone hall attached to the secondary chapel of Saint Imret the Patient — a minor saint whose chief virtue had been the willingness to remain at a post that no one visited for seventy-two years. Aldric had always found the dedication faintly pointed.

The warden at the gate was a lay brother named Yelin whom Aldric had last seen nineteen years ago at a conference in the Old Capital. Yelin remembered him with the sharp accurate pleasure of a man who had cultivated his memory the way other men cultivated a garden. They stood a quarter-hour in the yard trading the small currencies of seminary conversation — who had died, who had taken orders in the western provinces, who had disappeared into a scholarly obsession in some fishing village.

Aldric waited until the talk had thinned before he asked what he had come to ask.

"Yelin. Has the office had a Keeper through lately? Scholarly consultation?"

Yelin's attention oriented. "Yours?"

"I'm asking generally. The last month or two."

"Sister Ilona came through. Brother Tomas overnight on his way to the island. There was a Keeper from Confluence — I don't remember the name. He didn't stay at the House. Kept at a private lodging. Came to the office only to file his entry."

Aldric had filed no entry. He had not come to the office.

"When was this?"

"A week past. Perhaps ten."

"What was his business?"

"Scholarly consultation. Eastern provinces. Father Thane handled him. I could ask."

"No," Aldric said. He said it more quickly than he meant to and had to do the small repair-work of seeming unconcerned. "No, don't ask. I thought I might have missed a colleague."

Yelin accepted this with the surface courtesy of a man who had made a professional practice of not probing what was offered. But something had registered — the small filing-motion in Yelin's eyes, the entry of the question into an interior ledger for later review. The mistake had already done its work and could not be undone.

Scholarly consultation. Eastern provinces. A Keeper from Confluence a week past, traveling under a description that matched his own almost exactly. Crane had not bothered to invent a different cover.

"Yelin," he said. "I find I may not need lodging at the House after all. I have an old friend in the docks district. I'll come up another day for the office."

"As you wish, Keeper."

He walked his horse out of the gate and down the ridge road with his face composed. When he reached the first switchback on the descent he let his breath out fully, and it came out shaky, and he steadied it.

*Providence,* he thought, and the word arrived in his mouth with its old liturgical weight — and beneath the weight, the word that belonged here instead: *procedure.* Crane had not extended her reach by providence. She had extended it by procedure. A Keeper had been sent ahead. A description had been filed. A desk clerk named Father Thane had accepted the filing without looking at it closely, because filings did not require looking closely, because the procedure assumed good faith, because the procedure had been written two hundred years ago by people who could not have imagined a use for it like this one.

He had been counting the wrong kind of reach. Crane's had not needed the length his fear had granted it. It had needed only the infrastructure that was already there, and the infrastructure was everywhere, and the infrastructure was patient.

The lamp tic moved in his hand. He had reached — at nothing, at air — for a flame that did not exist, and he closed his fingers on the empty space and let them fall.

---

The docks district met him at the river's edge the way a different country met you at a border — not gradually, but all at once. The temple district had been bodies at rest or in ceremony. The docks district was bodies at work, and the work was loud, and the work did not pause for an uncertain stranger with a tired horse and a traveling coat.

A stevedore shouldered past him at the head of a line of men moving sacks from a cart to a warehouse door; the sacks smelled of grain and coarse linen, the cart horses of sweat and piss and the corn they had been eating. A gull argued with another gull above a fish-cart three stalls down. A woman at a flower-stall told a boy to stop running into her pots, and the boy ran into a pot, and the pot went over, and the noise continued past the event without registering it.

The smell of fish was underneath everything — not the clean cold brine he had taken the harbor to be when he first saw it from the ridge, but the worked smell, cut and gutted and salted and smoked and newly-caught all braided together in the warm damp of the waterfront.

A Keeper in a docks district was already a small heresy of bearing. The order's vestments were designed for chapels, not quays. He adjusted. He slowed. He let the crowd flow around him rather than through him, and he let his expression soften into the amiable vagueness of a traveler who had not quite found what he was looking for — an expression so common on the waterfront that it was effectively invisible.

*Wreckage,* he thought, because the word arrived and he let it. The church would call the district a *congregation.* The scholar in him would call it a *commercial community.* Between the two, the word he was actually using was *wreckage.* Small working salvages of a population making its living from the edge of a sea. The boy with his pot. The stevedore with his sack. The flower-woman with her vendor's voice. Each a bit of what the crash had scattered, still functioning, still making the small exchanges by which a life was assembled from the wreck of the larger thing.

He found a public house two blocks up from the waterfront with a yard that would take his horse, paid for a bed and a stall, and came back down into the common room with his cassock replaced by a plain dark overtunic. The landlord — heavy-built, scarred at the lip, the quick uninterested kindness of someone who had seen every kind of guest pass through — asked no questions. He paid her in advance for three nights. She brought him a cup of warm cider without his having ordered one.

He drank it slowly and let his body understand that he was no longer on a road.

---

He needed a way in.

He knew what he was looking for. Reth's directions in memory. Brennan's maps in his saddlebag. Brennan's letter with the phrase *for unchanged hands* — a sentence that had ridden with him for weeks, nearly legible now, not yet.

He did not have, and needed, the people. A city this size, a question this old — there would be people working it. Not institutionally. The institution had decided eight hundred years ago that the question did not exist. But informally. In workshops and the back rooms of scholarly clubs. Brennan had worked it alone in Greenford for twelve years. Someone in New Plymouth was working it too. The question had a gravity, and gravity collected.

He spent the rest of the afternoon walking the Crafting Quarter.

He did not ask for anything. He walked. Smithies together, glassblowers together, mapmakers and scribes along one particular curving lane that the locals called, for reasons no longer clear, the Inkwalk. He walked it twice in the late afternoon, past a chandler's and a bookseller's, and he stopped at a cartographer's window to look at a set of coastal charts drawn by a hand that had spent a lifetime getting the fall of a shoreline exact.

A card in the window: *Consultations by appointment.* A name: *E. Marrick, Cartographer and Chartmaker.* A second address on the door.

He walked the two blocks east and stood across the street from the shop in the dusk and watched it. One man came out carrying a wrapped parcel and did not return while Aldric stayed. He noted the posted hours — *dawn to second bell* — walked back to the public house, ate a plain dinner at the window table, and slept poorly. He dreamed of the face.

---

The shop opened shortly after first bell, which was earlier than the posted hours — the kind of small lie a tradesman told to buy himself a quiet hour at the start of the day. Aldric had been on the street for twenty minutes when the shutter came up. The man who lifted it was in his sixties, spare-built, with ink-stained fingers and the specific attentiveness of a man who had just been at his worktable and had come forward reluctantly to let the day in.

Edvar Marrick looked at him over the half-raised shutter.

"We open at second bell," he said. His voice was level. Not unfriendly. Not inviting.

"I know," Aldric said. "I was hoping for a consultation."

"Consultations are by appointment."

"I understand. May I make one?"

Marrick considered him through the gap in the shutter. Aldric stood still under the consideration and let it happen — he understood that he was being read, and he understood that what Marrick was reading was the fact of a plainclothes traveler at a cartographer's shop before opening, asking in the specific careful cadence of an educated man who was trying to sound ordinary. The cassock was off but the cadence had never come off. Brennan had read him the same way.

"What is the consultation regarding?" Marrick said.

"The eastern highlands," Aldric said. "Specifically a formation east of the highland plateau, approximately eleven miles northeast of the old commission station, roughly four hundred meters in lateral extent. I'm told it's been described in a territorial commission report as a mineral deposit of uncertain composition. I'd like to know if you have mapped it."

Marrick's face did nothing.

He looked at Aldric for a long slow interval that was not silence but a very particular kind of attention — the attention of a man turning a question over in his hand and examining each of its faces in turn, the way he would have turned one of his small instruments. Aldric did not fill the interval. He had delivered the question. The question was doing its work.

Marrick raised the shutter the rest of the way.

"Come in," he said.

---

The shop smelled of linseed and old paper. Aldric stepped through the door and the smell met him the way the smell of a familiar church met him when he had been traveling — a specific density of atmosphere, a quality made of decades of the same materials in the same small space. He was aware, with the stripped clarity that had been following him since Greenford, that he had just crossed a threshold he would not be able to re-cross. The shop was a room he had entered once in the simple way and would never enter the same way again.

Marrick closed the door behind him. He did not turn the sign on the door. The sign still read *Closed.*

"You haven't told me your name," Marrick said.

"Aldric Voss. Keeper at the Temple of Confluence."

"A Keeper."

"Yes."

"And you are asking about a formation in the eastern highlands that does not appear on any public map."

"Yes."

Marrick nodded, small, once. He walked to his worktable and sat down at it — an old wooden chair, scarred at the arms by forty years of the same forearms resting on it — and he did not invite Aldric to sit. He looked at his hands for a moment, arranging them on the table in the way Aldric had come to recognize as the way a person of long patient inquiry arranged the body when the inquiry arrived in the room. Brennan did this. Reth had done it. Aldric did it himself, though he had not been aware he did until he watched other men do it.

"Why are you asking me?" Marrick said.

"Because you make maps, and a formation of that size and nature should appear on your maps if it appears on anyone's."

"And it does not appear on any map you have seen."

"It does not."

"But you have the directions to find it."

"I do."

Marrick looked up. The look was long and measuring and not unkind.

"From whom?" he said.

Aldric considered the question. He had come prepared to offer Brennan and Reth as references, had rehearsed the sequence on the ride from the temple district. But standing in the shop with the linseed smell around him and the old cartographer looking at him with the attention of a man who had clearly been working some version of this question for a long time, he understood that names were not what Marrick was asking for. Marrick was asking for the shape of the approach.

"From a friend in Greenford who has been studying margin notations on pre-crash maps," Aldric said. "Who introduced me to a surveyor who walked the formation forty-odd years ago and could not place what he had found."

The silence that followed was different from the silence at the door. It was the silence of a man who had just heard a sentence he had given up on hearing.

Marrick closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them his hands had not moved on the table.

"The surveyor's name," he said.

"Reth."

"Reth." Marrick said the name as though he had been holding a candidate for it and the candidate had resolved. "He still lives?"

"He does."

"He told you what he saw?"

"He told me. And more than that. He described a recess at the southeastern end of the formation, where the surface curves down into the hillside. A recess shaped like a coin. With a symbol in it."

Marrick's right hand lifted off the table and pressed flat against his chest, over the heart, a gesture so involuntary and so complete that Aldric knew the mapmaker had not known he was going to make it. When he lowered the hand, his eyes were wet, though his face had not otherwise moved.

"The symbol," Marrick said.

Aldric did not describe it. He had come to know, over the last months, the precise moment at which describing a thing replaced the thing with the description, and this was that moment, and he would not replace the thing. He simply looked at Marrick, and Marrick looked back, and whatever communication was required happened between them without the word.

"There are people," Marrick said slowly, "who have been asking some version of your question for years. I have been asking it for twenty. There are others. We meet."

"I would like to meet with them."

"You have not told me everything you know."

"No. I have not."

"Will you?"

Aldric considered. The face in the undercroft. The four shapes around the blue-green world. The one irregular, damaged. The four smooth containers in the sealed chamber, lined against the wall like patient urns. The phrase from Brennan's letter riding in his chest. The Keeper who was not him, filing an entry in the office a week past. Yelin's registering face. The lamp tic on his thumb.

"I will tell them what I have," he said. "Once I see the shape of what they have. Not before."

Something in Marrick settled — not a smile, Aldric had thirty years of reading faces in chapels and knew the difference between expression and arrangement — but the settled quiet of a man who had found a negotiating partner and had stopped expecting to. He stood up from his worktable. The stand was slow, the knees reluctant, the old cartographer's practiced negotiation with his own body.

"There is a cooperage two streets from the river," he said. "I will write you an address. You will present yourself this evening after the second bell. You will buy a set of iron hoops from the apprentice at the counter. You will wait for a nod. And then you will go where you are directed."

"A cooperage."

"The people I meet with meet in its basement. It is as private as any room in this city. The workshop above makes noise enough to cover the talk below."

"And they will receive me?"

"They will receive someone I send."

Marrick took a small square of paper from a drawer and wrote an address on it in a hand that was neither hurried nor decorative — the hand, Aldric recognized with the small shock of a pattern recognized across contexts, of a man who used the same hand to write in the margins of twelve maps over fifteen years. He folded the paper twice and held it out.

"Keeper Voss," Marrick said.

"Yes."

"There is a young woman who will be in the room tonight. She carries an object that you will recognize when you see it. I am telling you this now so that when you see it you do not say the wrong thing. The room has spent the last three days adjusting to it. I would prefer not to adjust again."

"I understand."

"You do not understand yet. But you will."

Aldric took the paper. Marrick turned the sign on the door to *Open.*

---

He went back to the public house and slept three hours. Then he wrote to Brennan — a short institutional letter that said, among other things: *I have arrived. The shape of the question here is larger than I had understood. I am meeting the people who work it tonight. I will write again.* He sealed it and left it on the table to be posted.

He washed his face, dressed plainly, did not wear the cassock. When the second bell rang out over the river from the merchant campanile, he left the public house and walked east along the waterfront and then inland two streets, and he found the cooperage by the smell of oak shavings and the pale lamplight under the shutter of the workshop door.

The apprentice at the counter was sixteen or seventeen with red steam-scalded hands, and did not look up from his ledger when he took Aldric's coins. The nod came after a long enough pause that Aldric had time to doubt it would come at all. A door behind the stave rack. Stone stairs leading down. The air changed three steps down, the way Reth had described the change in the light at dusk on the formation — not subtle, not gradual, but a decisive crossing from one kind of atmosphere to another, and the atmosphere below was stone-cold and tallow-smelling and held the faint chemical bite of a tannery.

At the bottom of the stairs, the basement opened.

Two lamps on a central table. Corners left in shadow. The room held the specific chilly dampness of a cellar that had been dug into living rock and never fully warmed by a summer in six hundred years. Eight or nine people turned to look at him as he came down. He registered them as arrangement first — the old method, the chapel method — and then as individuals.

Marrick at the end of the table, seated. He had come ahead.

A woman Aldric took for Renner by the description Marrick had not given him — the straight-backed, gray-threaded, unsoftened woman who had the bearing of someone who had run rooms. Beside her a younger woman with forearms that told him glassblower without his needing to be told. Across the table a square-built man whose hands had been tanner's hands long enough for the leather smell to live in them. A young woman with ink on the heel of her writing hand who was almost certainly the one Marrick had mentioned in some other context he did not yet have. A lean angular boy in his twenties, all forward-lean, who looked at Aldric with an assessing brightness that was somehow both friendly and cold.

A young man near the far wall, quiet, watchful, with a stillness to him that registered before the rest of him did — twenty, perhaps; long-limbed; carrying himself in the specific way that men carried themselves when they were managing a body that did things without their permission. His skin at the collar of his shirt, where it was bare, had the faintest wash of a color that was not a color — a suggestion of blue under the skin that was not a vein or a shadow or a trick of the lamplight.

And a young woman, small and dark-haired, seated to the left of the glassblower, whose hands rested in her lap and whose eyes met his directly when he reached the bottom step. She was, by Marrick's warning, the one who carried the object. He had been told this so he would not say the wrong thing. Looking at her, he understood the warning better. She had the composure of a person who had made a difficult decision very recently and was still carrying the freshness of the making. Her face did nothing he could read. It did, however, register him completely — the look of a person whose first act in any room was to count the exits and catalogue the inhabitants, and who had done so before his second step off the stair.

The girl who carried the object. He was a Keeper of thirty years. He had read the sealed chamber in the undercroft. He had looked into the face and counted four. He had ridden a thousand miles of road with the word *providence* turning in his chest into the word *wreckage.* He had arrived.

He did not say anything.

Marrick spoke first.

"Friends," he said. "This is Aldric Voss. He is a Keeper from the Temple of Confluence. He has come to us along a thread I will not yet describe in full. He has directions to a formation in the eastern highlands that I believe you will recognize. He has, in addition, something else that I will let him say in his own time."

Renner looked at him for a long interval. Her look was not unkind. It was the look of a woman whose framework had been broken recently, and who was no longer assuming that the next stranger down her stairs belonged in any of her existing categories.

"Keeper," she said. "Sit."

He sat.

The young man near the wall — the one with the wash of blue under his skin — had been watching him since he descended. Now the skin at his wrists had gone, very faintly, from the blue wash to something warmer, a low amber that was not alarm but was not calm either. The young man caught Aldric's eyes on the shift and something in his face registered the catch. He lowered his gaze. The amber at his wrists receded by a degree.

Above the table, the two lamps. Between the lamps, on the wood, an object. Smooth gray. Forearm-length. The surface so uniformly dark it did not quite register as a surface — the lamplight reaching it and stopping there as though it had crossed a boundary into a different kind of light. Beside it, smaller, palm-sized, the same material: a disc.

Aldric's breath caught — one long-practiced careful breath, inhaled and held, the way he had been trained to hold his breath in chapel at the moment of the sanctus. He did not let it out audibly. He let it out slowly through the nose, and his hands did not reach, and his hands did not move, and his hands stayed on his thighs where he had put them when he sat down.

*For unchanged hands.*

The phrase from Brennan's letter arrived in the room the way a piece of cathedral masonry settles into its place and becomes, from that moment, structural — no longer an object carried but a load-bearing part of the standing thing. He had carried the phrase for weeks without knowing what it would hold up. He saw it now. He saw it in the girl's face, and in the disc on the table, and in the young man's amber and blue, and in Renner's broken-and-mended framework, and in the container's inert silence beside its smaller companion. The sentence was coming into focus. He was still outside it — they had a week he did not have, they had arguments he had not heard, they had a specific person he did not yet know by name or history — but the sentence was in the room, and he was in the room, and he was closer to it than he had been at any moment since the sealed chamber.

---

They went around. Names, brief accounts, what each of them held.

Renner. Former temple archivist. Four years on the astronomical question. The argument about the bloodline's capacity. She delivered her account in a voice that had once been aggressive-precise, Marrick had warned him; what Aldric heard was a voice that had been aggressive-precise until recently, and had been carrying its new humility in a way that made the old precision stand out by contrast — the way a hand that used to grip something and has let go still keeps the shape of the grip.

Asha. Glassblower. Circular evidence. The test case. The correction — *hasn't existed* — delivered to him as a phrase already polished by previous use, a stone smoothed by handling.

Corvel. Tanner. A container he had found in the marshland south of the ridge, half-buried in clay, eight years ago, that he had carried to Marrick and through him to this group. The container sat on the table beside the smaller disc. Aldric looked at it for the first time with attention, and under the attention the container's surface did what Reth had said the formation's surface did at dusk: took his looking and did not give it back, the light seeming to go into the material rather than off it.

The young cartographer — Tela, her name was; Aldric did not catch the family name on first hearing and did not press for it — had ink stains fresh on her writing hand and the kind of silence that was not deferential but intent. She was listening rather than speaking. She looked at Aldric the way Marrick had looked at him in the doorway: with the open directness of a person who had decided that courtesies could wait.

Pip — the lean boy — introduced himself with a half-grin that was not practiced. He found things, he said. It was how he had come into the group. He did not elaborate. Marrick, beside him, did not elaborate either, and Aldric took the non-elaboration as data.

The young man near the wall was Davan. Davan, from Amber Valley. New to the city. A carver, among other things. He had come to Marrick through Pip, three months or so ago. He had seen a thing in Marrick's keeping that he did not name in this introduction. He kept his hands on his knees and the amber-blue wash at his collar shifted slowly as he spoke, and Aldric registered the shift without mentioning it — because the young man was clearly aware of it and clearly managing it, and it was not Aldric's place to name a body's tell in a room where the body in question was already performing the management of it.

And Maren. She said her name. She said that she was from Ashwick, and that she had been in New Plymouth for some weeks, and that she was unchanged — she used the word herself, unchanged, evenly, without either defiance or shame — and that she carried an object her father had left her, which was on the table, which had, in the last several days, begun to pulse with increasing frequency, and which had — she said this without drama, setting it down like a stone into the wall of her account — pulsed three days ago, in this room, in a way that the container beside it had not and had never.

She did not look at him when she said it. She said it to the table. She had delivered an account this way before. He understood that.

When she finished, she did look at him. Her eyes were dark and not guarded, not in the usual sense, but had the quality of a room he had once walked into in the old monastery at Vellicar — a chapel whose door had been left open to the weather for an afternoon, the air inside it changed by the outside, and every object in it more itself for the exposure.

"Keeper," she said. "Your turn."

---

He spoke for perhaps a quarter-hour.

He did not say everything. He did not speak of Crane — not yet. He did not describe the Keeper who was not him filing a fabricated entry in the office a week past. He did not say the word *providence* as he had said it to himself on the road, though he thought it once, near the end of his account, and pressed it down.

He said: *A sealed chamber in the undercroft of the Temple of Confluence. Found two months ago. Five objects. One of them is a flat dark surface that responds to touch. It shows an image. The image is an arrangement of orbital bodies. Four of them. One of them is not like the others. It is irregular. It reads as damaged.*

He said: *I have a friend in Greenford — a scholar named Brennan. He has been working the margin notation question for twelve years. Twelve maps in his possession. Four in the oldest, three in the youngest. A clean transition.*

He said: *He introduced me to a surveyor named Reth who walked the eastern highlands forty years ago and found a formation four hundred meters long, metallic, no grain, no seam, manufactured, partially buried under centuries of sediment. At the southeastern end, where it curves into the hillside, there is a recess. The size of a coin. With a symbol in it.*

He said the things he could say, and he did not describe the symbol, because to describe it he would have had to take Maren's disc in his hand, and he was not yet certain it was his to take.

The room received it. Not in silence, exactly — Renner moved a pen fractionally on the table; Corvel's breath altered; Asha's hands came out of her lap and lay flat; Tela stopped pretending to take notes — but in the quality of silence that was not an absence of speech but a presence of attention, a silence that had shape to it and texture, and pressed against him from all sides.

Davan's skin at the collarbones had gone, during his account, to a color Aldric did not have a name for. Not amber. Not blue. Something between them and also beside them, a quiet warm green-gold that held steady rather than pulsing. Aldric looked at it and did not understand it and understood it entirely. The young man was showing his recognition of what Aldric was describing in the only way his body had for recognition. A second narrator. A skin that said what the body underneath it had already known.

"The symbol," Renner said, finally.

"I would like to see the disc first."

Renner did not look at Maren. She waited for Maren to answer for herself.

Maren lifted the disc off the table with the same quiet economy with which she had set it down three days ago and slid it across the wood. It came to rest two hand-widths from Aldric's knee, close enough that he could see the inner orbit symbol on its edge clearly in the lamplight — the four nested arcs, the raised points along their paths, the fourth arc drifting tangentially off the pattern. Exactly as on the door above the Temple of Confluence. Exactly as on the margin of a map twelve of which sat in Brennan's workroom in Greenford. Exactly as, by Reth's account, in a recess cleared of sediment in a hillside eleven miles northeast of an old commission station.

He looked at it.

He did not reach for it.

His hand lifted from his thigh and crossed the short space between his knee and the disc and stopped — stopped of its own accord, without his deciding that it would stop, his trained fingers arrested a span from the surface of a thing his body had been trying to name for months and his mind had been counting toward for longer than that. The hand did not tremble. It simply stood at that distance, a hand's breadth from an object it had crossed a thousand miles of road to come into proximity with, and it did not close.

He had reached for a flame that was not there a dozen times in the last week. He had trained the reach to stop. And now the hand he had trained to stop was stopping at the one thing he had come to touch, and the training had held too well, and he understood, with the specific awkward clarity of a man watching a habit misfire into an unintended grace, that this was the hand's refusal and not his own.

He let the hand stay. He did not force it forward.

He did not force it back.

Maren watched him. Her face was very still. She did not offer the disc; she did not withdraw it; she did not, in the manner some people had, attempt to reassure him. She let him stand in his own hesitation at the width of a hand's breadth from the answer, and her letting him stand there was the largest courtesy anyone had extended to him since the sealed chamber.

"Keeper," Marrick said, quietly.

"Yes."

"Take your time."

He nodded. He lowered the hand. He laid it flat on his own thigh, palm down, the way Reth had laid his hands on the table forty years after filing his report. The disc sat where Maren had slid it. Beside it, the container, inert. Above both, the two lamps on the table, one of them down to the last quarter-inch of its wick.

The room held. Nobody moved to fill the space.

Outside and above, beyond the cooperage roof, the three moons were where they had been for ten thousand years, at their posts, at their stations, keeping a count he had finally, in the cold basement of a New Plymouth workshop, come into the room with people who were keeping it with him.

He would touch the disc. He would touch it this week. He would touch it tomorrow, or the day after, when his hand decided it belonged to him again. Tonight he had come this far, and his hand had stopped at the span of a closed flower from the answer, and the stopping was what his body had given him instead of a speech, and he let it be the speech.

Maren's eyes crossed the table and met his, and the look was not expectant and was not patient — it was, with the economy she seemed to bring to every gesture, only the look of one person acknowledging another across a room that had finally assembled everyone it had been trying to assemble.

The disc pulsed, faintly, once — no warmer than the lamp, no brighter than the dark, just the small deepening of the surface that he had not seen before but that he recognized with his whole body as the thing she had described, as the thing Brennan had inferred, as the thing Reth had half-seen and refused. Maren did not look at it. She looked at him.

His hand stayed on his thigh.

Somewhere above them, in the workshop, a stave knocked against a stave, and a barrel hoop settled into place with the faint iron ring of work being done, and the ring carried down through the floorboards and died in the cold air of the cellar, and the room waited, and he waited with it, and the span between his hand and the disc was the exact width of the sentence he had come a thousand miles to begin.
