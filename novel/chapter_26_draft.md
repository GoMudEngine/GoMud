## Chapter Twenty-Six — *Vane*
### Withdrawal

---

The dead hour did not begin at an hour. It began at a change in
the cold.

She had come onto second watch at the ash-lee with her hands
already numb past useful, and she had sat against the north-bent
ash and taken her post because taking her post was what she had
said she would do. Aldric had gone to his blanket without
pressing. She appreciated the not-pressing. She did not want the
Keeper's face looking at her face at the moment her face was
doing what her face had started to do in the evenings.

*Tremor both hands. Gut off. Hearing misallocated — low
frequencies lifted, high ones thin. Temperature off-register,
the kind of wrong-warmth that fools you into taking the coat
off. Do not take the coat off.*

She kept the coat on.

The cold at the ash-lee was the cold of an upland camp at the
third hour — dry cold, a cold whose signature she knew. She had
sat out a hundred of these. Her body knew how to read what was
the weather and what was the body.

Tonight the ratio was not clean.

---

At some point in the dark she checked the arm.

Left hand under right sleeve, thumb to the back of the right
wrist where the patch had been flaking since the night before
they left the city. The grain was drier than it had been at
dusk. She ran her thumb along it once, slowly. The flake lifted
at the edge. It did not come off under her thumb. She took her
thumb off.

*Not looking at it in lamplight. Not tonight.*

She put the sleeve back.

A rook moved in some field a mile off. Nothing nearer. Her nose
told her nothing nearer, which was the information.

Her nose. Still hers.

She let the small fact sit. She did not decorate it.

---

First light came without her deciding to let it.

Aldric rose. She registered him by the long motion of a man his
age sitting up, two pauses in it. He took the watch without
asking and she yielded it without speaking, and she went to her
blanket with her coat still on, and her hands inside the cuffs
kept up their tremor. She had stopped rating it by severity.
Rating by severity required a baseline and the baseline was now
yesterday.

She closed her eyes for the length of two breaths. The body took
what it could and gave the rest back.

---

Maren was at the cart when Vane stood up.

She was repacking the water-skins. She looked up once, briefly,
and her face did a small thing that registered and did not
pretend. The face said: *I have already decided not to ask.*

Vane crossed to her pack and drew out the chapped leather cuffs
she used in cold weather and pulled them over the backs of her
hands. They took the tremor out of the cuff-motion. They did not
take the tremor out of anything else. They were cover.

Maren watched her do this without looking at her.

"There's tea," Maren said. "Short kettle. Davan set it."

"All right."

The cup clinked once against her teeth. She set it down before
it clinked a second time.

Maren's free hand went inside her coat as she reached for the
kettle-handle. Came out. Went back to the kettle. She did not
comment and neither did Vane. Vane catalogued it. *Second of the
day. First was before the bell.*

---

They broke camp in the pale.

She walked her circuit of the cart-stand. She had walked a
hundred of these. The circuit was thirty-four paces and had five
breaks in the ground to be registered and two stumps that
resolved on the return pass differently than on the out pass,
because early light and late light were different sentences.

On the fifth pace she saw it second.

A dew-track across a shelf of stone. Four hours old, probably. A
small animal. Not a person. She should have seen it on the out
pass. The missing was the information.

*Re-reading the same stretch. Day three symptom per Cade's old
chart. Attention fragments. Compensate: extend the circuit, slow
the pace.*

She extended the circuit. She slowed the pace.

She came back in with nothing new to report except the wider
circuit, and she set that against the report she had not made
and went to the cart.

---

The road out of the ash-lee climbed.

She was ahead by ten paces — the position she had been taking
since they left the city. It let her set the pace and put her
face into the wind, which this morning was cold out of the
north-northeast and kept the wrong-sweat from turning on her
under the coat.

Aldric rode behind her with the bay. Then the cart, Edvar on the
bench, Maren at the off-wheel, Davan at the near. She knew their
positions without turning her head.

She would have known their positions yesterday by smell — bay
sweat, Edvar's old oil, Aldric's travel coat, Maren's coat-collar
with the rooming-house soap, Davan's warmth. This morning she
knew them by pace and by the direction of the wind. Her nose gave
her the bay and gave her Davan at distance and the cart-wood
faintly and did not give her Maren and did not give her Aldric.

The baseline had contracted.

*Three feet. Two feet on a wind. Down by an order from what it
used to be. Not gone. Contracted.*

She walked.

---

They were into the beginning of Irongate country by mid-morning.

The shoulders of rock began closing in. In the last country they
had crossed, a follower at the edge of a sightline could be lost
in the weave of heath and rock without having to be skilled. In
this country, with the sightlines lengthened by the gap, he had
to be actively managing distance.

She knew the tactics. She had used them for ten years.

She was working the problem backward before she knew she was
working anything.

*If I were following four people and a cart through this country,
I would drop behind by three quarters of a mile at the last rise,
wait thirty minutes, then close to half a mile and hold. The gap
shape forces me closer than I'd prefer. The rust scree on the
north shoulder gives me cover at eye level. I'd take the lower
line at the stream if I had to.*

She walked another twelve paces before she registered that she
had been, for the length of the thought, inside a man's head
that was not hers, at the distance that thought described.

Information.

She did not act on it. One inferential step was not confirmation.
She needed the wind.

---

The wind shifted at the mid-morning bend.

She had been waiting for it. The gap turned a quarter-compass
off north, and the wind that had been in her face came across at
her right shoulder, and the wind that had been going nowhere
instructive began to take the back of the line and carry it
forward to her.

She turned so her left nostril had the wind's work. The bark
grain at the right shoulder carried scent poorly — the wrong
instrument.

She breathed in and did the old reading.

Dry grass. Sheep-country mud days old. The rust in the north
scree. The horses. Edvar. Maren at the off-wheel, Aldric further
back, Davan at the near. Nothing new in the party.

Back of the party — the long back, the ground they had crossed
this morning.

Nothing.

She waited.

The wind did not hand you a signature on the first breath. It
handed you what was there densest. What was thin and professional
and managed arrived, if it arrived, on the second or third.

Second breath.

Cold-metal.

Not a flood of it. A thread. The thin carried note of a person
who was not panicking — a person holding himself at the flat
register of a body doing its work. Cold-metal without the sour
edge of fear. The cleaner cold. The cold of a contract man
reading his mark.

She had smelled it before, in her own mouth, when she had been
the one doing the reading.

*Professional. Male. Singular. Behind us by a distance the wind
is carrying him at this shift.*

She did not turn her head. She did not change pace.

*How long.*

She ran the wind backward in her memory. The chart she had been
keeping had holes in it now where the entries for the previous
nights should have been. But the ford. At the ford the wind had
been up from the harbor mouth across her right shoulder the way
it was now, and she had stopped on the plank walkway and put her
hand in her coat pocket and taken her hand out with nothing in
it, and she had registered at the time that she had been about
to smell something and had not.

Now the smell was back. The wind had handed her what it had
tried to hand her at the ford, when she had been less broken for
the handing.

He had been with them since the ford.

She knew this with the precision you knew a thing when the wind
delivered it the second time.

---

She did not tell them at the bend.

She walked to the next reasonable stop — a spring coming up
through moss where the road took a half-circle around a knuckle
of rock. When Maren came to the spring with her skin, Vane stood
at her shoulder and took a cup-full and drank first, back to the
road, and said at a pitch meant for the spring and no further:

"One behind us."

Maren's skin-filling hand did not pause.

"How far," Maren said.

"Three-quarter mile. Professional. Singular."

"How long."

"Since the ford."

Maren nodded once.

"Aldric," Maren said, not looking up. "At the cart."

Aldric had come down from the bay. He did not come to the
spring; he understood by the angle of Maren's head that the
conference was not for him to step into. He went to the cart
instead and stood at the off-wheel and said something to Edvar,
and Edvar went quiet.

Davan, on the near wheel, stopped walking entirely. His eyes
came up to Vane's at the spring. His skin at the open collar
took on a thin cool amber for the length of a breath and dropped.
He had read the spring from ten paces by reading her face.

Her face had done a thing without her. She was not entirely in
the driver's seat of her own face today.

---

They regrouped at the cart a minute later.

Vane pitched her voice low. Not private; just short of carrying.

"A man behind us. Professional. One. Cold. Not in a hurry. The
wind just gave him to me. He's been with us since the ford."

Aldric absorbed this without his face doing anything. His hand
at his coat did not reach for any wick or any fold.

"Not Horst," Aldric said. Not a question.

"Not Horst. Horst is in New Plymouth. This is one of his. Horst
stays with a city contract."

"How certain."

"I trained with him three seasons. He works a city."

"Tactics," Maren said.

Vane took a breath. It caught. She let it catch. She took
another.

"Three moves. One: we slow. Quarter-pace. A man dragging a tail
wants to find out whether we've noticed him, and a quarter-pace
is inside the normal error a cart makes over a day. If he holds
his distance at quarter-pace, he's following a contract. If he
closes, he's looking."

"Two," Davan said.

"We change camp. We were going to camp at the east edge of
Irongate. Edvar had a fold picked. We don't camp there. Edvar?"

"There's an older one on the eastern heath. Used to be a
shepherd's. Three walls. I've camped it twice."

"We camp there. If he's working from a route, the empty fold
costs him an hour. If he's working from sightline, he's on us
already and the extra mile is our profit, not his."

"Three," Maren said.

The tremor in Vane's left hand did its small visible work at the
fingertip. She did not hide it.

"I do not walk point today. Davan walks point — not to track;
just to hold the pace. I walk back of the cart. My nose is worse
than yesterday and worse than it will be in a week, but it's
better than any of yours by a margin still worth something. The
back of the cart is where the wind brings what the wind brings."

"Can you walk back of the cart," Maren said. Flat. Not the
softened voice.

"Yes."

"If you can't."

"You'll see it. You'll put me in the cart."

Maren nodded once.

"All right," she said.

---

They moved on.

Vane took her new position at the back of the cart. She walked
with one hand on the tailboard and her weight rolling foot to
foot the way she had rolled it on night watches where the body's
protest had to be managed without reaching the face. The wind
kept up across her right shoulder. The cold-metal thread came and
went at the wind's intervals, not at rhythm but at wind. Once
every fifteen minutes was enough.

He was closing by a few paces an hour. He was doing the sum she
had done an hour ago from inside his head, and he was doing it
correctly, and the fact of his doing it correctly was what she
had needed to know about him.

*Not green. Contract man. Horst's. Not the raw lads. Works
highland routes. Reads rock cover. Knows how carters break the
day.*

The analysis began to stretch and snag. *Male. Right-handed,
probably. Not young.* The next sentence was going to be a
category that did not fit the data. She did not reach for it.

He was a man behind her at three-quarters of a mile. She could
smell him. That was enough.

Twice in the hour after, at the off-wheel side, Maren's hand
went inside her coat and came out again without comment. Vane
did not ask. Davan on the bench had stopped speaking to Edvar at
the second of them, and his collar had stayed the low held green
Vane had seen yesterday at the outcrop. The two of them had
their instrument for this and Vane was not it. That was also
enough.

---

Around the middle of the afternoon Davan dropped back from point.

He did not come all the way to her. He came to the near wheel
for a few minutes, and then drifted toward her without angling
his body — a motion she recognized as one of her own, the kind
of approach that did not ask a room for permission.

He stopped at the tailboard two paces from her hand.

His skin at the wrist had a pale amber wash to it that was not
fear. It was the pale amber of a body carrying a weight whose
name it had not decided on.

"Water?" he said.

"No."

"Shoulder?"

"No. The shoulder's all right."

He let this pass. He was not going to ask the question he had
come to ask. He was going to stand at the tailboard for the time
it took him to stand at the tailboard.

He was reading her.

Not the way Maren was reading her — Maren watched the surface
shapes. Davan was reading the thing under the chart. The register
he worked in was her body doing something without her permission,
and his body took the thing on the far end of the register and
had to manage what it did on the way in.

"Don't," she said. Quietly. Not a command.

"Don't what."

"Don't keep your skin where other people can see what it's doing
on my account. Turn your back to the wind. Turn your wrist in."

A beat. His collar-color dropped a shade. The amber at the wrist
stayed.

"I don't know how to turn it in, exactly," he said. "Yet."

"Then stop standing here."

"All right."

He waited two breaths. He took a step back. He turned, slowly,
so the wrist was on the lee side of his body, and he walked back
up the near wheel to Edvar's bench, and his skin at the wrist —
she watched it for a count of ten — dropped the amber back into
blue over thirty paces.

It had cost him to stand at the tailboard and have his skin do
that at her. It had cost him more than he had been going to say.
He had stood there anyway.

She filed it. Not the way she had filed his warmth a week ago.
Differently. The entry sat in its slot crooked.

She did not try to re-cut the stone.

---

By late afternoon the gap had opened.

The shoulders of Irongate fell away and the country ran out east
in a long heath whose russet the wind had been combing all day.
The fold Edvar had picked two nights ago was at the road-verge
with three walls and most of a fourth. Vane walked off the cart,
stood at the wall's corner for a count of forty, and came back.

"Pass it," she said.

Edvar clucked to the horses. The cart went on.

"He'll find it empty," Aldric said from the saddle.

"He'll find it empty."

They rode on for the better part of another hour. The wind had
dropped. The cold-metal thread had dropped with it. Absence of a
reading from the wind was not a reading; it was the wind not
working. The contract man was still back there. He would not
have turned off a cart he had been following since the ford on
the afternoon of day three because a fold came up empty. He
would take the empty fold as intelligence and press his pace up
a quarter until he had relocated them on sightline.

*Which means he closes by more than a few paces an hour after
dusk. Unless he has a lamp and is willing to risk it.*

He would not have a lamp. Lamps in open country at night were
amateur work. Horst did not hire amateurs.

She kept walking.

---

They camped at the shepherd's fold an hour past dusk.

Edvar put the cart into the lee of the longest wall. Aldric
tethered at the windward. Davan took the water-skins. Maren was
building the fire — small, in a pocket of wall that held the
light in — before Vane had decided whether to sit down.

She sat down eventually, at the leeward wall, with her back
against the stone. Her legs went loose in a way that had not been
a decision. Her hands shook badly now without the cuff cover.

Maren came across the fold with a tin cup.

She did not offer it. She set it on the flat stone beside Vane's
knee.

"Not tea," Maren said. Quietly. "My mother's mother used to boil
this down for women who shook in the winter — sweet-flag root,
and a piece of ginger, and a pinch of the dry mint I packed.
It's not a cure. It's a thing that slows the hands for a half-
hour. Drink it or don't."

Vane looked at the cup.

"Hot?"

"Hot enough. Not scalding. You won't chip the cup."

Vane reached for the cup. She needed two hands. She did not
hide the needing. She drank it in three small swallows. The
sweet-flag hit the back of the throat first, bitter and resinous
the way sweet-flag was, and the ginger came after, and the mint
was the thing that made it drinkable. The heat went down and sat
where the heat sat when it was doing a job.

Her hands stopped shaking about a minute later. Not all at
once. They went from the fine fast tremor to a slower coarser
one to a series of long pauses between the tremors to nothing.
She breathed out a breath she had not been planning to breathe
out.

Maren was already on the other side of the fire turning Davan's
knife in the coals to heat a rasher for Edvar.

She had made no production of any of it. She had brought the
cup, put it on the stone, said what was in it, and gone back to
the fire. The going-back-to-the-fire was what made the bringing
possible. If she had stood over Vane to watch her drink, the
drinking would have been a different thing. She had not stood
over. She had put the cup down and walked away, and the walking
away had given Vane a door.

Vane sat with the empty cup in her lap and the half-hour of
quiet hands ahead of her and the old professional ledger in her
head trying to book the transaction — *gift, received, cost — *
and the ledger refused the entry. The ledger had not been built
for this kind of transaction. This was not a gift. Maren had
named it. *A thing that slows the hands for a half-hour.* It was
a tool in a herbal kit. It was Maren doing for her what Maren
had done for her own ankle at the woodcutter's shelter, Vane now
understood, because Vane had asked Maren about the ankle on the
road north two weeks ago and Maren had said, *I wrapped it. I
went on.* The same register. The body was a body; the body did
what bodies did; you made it a little more bearable and you went
on.

Not care. Craft.

She had been braced for care. Care would have been harder to
receive. The craft was easier because the craft did not require
a gratitude she did not have the coin for tonight.

---

She slept at first watch against her will.

Aldric took the fire. Davan took the wind side. Maren took the
space between her and the open side of the fold, which was, she
registered as she was going under, a position Maren had taken
without its being discussed. She had gone to sleep on the inside
of Maren's shoulder-line by the fold wall, and Maren had placed
herself between the open and the inside, and Maren's position was
the position a professional would have taken.

Maren was not a professional. Maren was a seventeen-year-old
from a hamlet who had learned to read rooms because reading
rooms had been the only way to survive her own village. She had
brought the same instrument to the fold.

*Not a professional,* Vane corrected. *Somebody who has been
forced into the adjacent country without the forced part showing.*

She slept.

---

She dreamed of nothing. The nothing was a mercy.

When she came up out of it the fire had gone down and the wind
had dropped and the stars were very cold overhead. Aldric was
at the wall on watch. Davan was asleep, the hawthorn piece from
the night before out of his coat pocket into his hand. Edvar
snored. Maren, three paces to her left, slept with one hand
inside her coat and her face turned toward Vane a degree more
than the fold would have required.

Vane lay on her back for a while.

Her right arm was out from under the blanket. She had not meant
to let it be out. The bark grain across her shoulder met the cold
air, and the cold went into the grain and did not travel, the way
old stone took cold and kept it. The patch at the back of her
wrist — unexamined — flaked a little more against the cuff of
her coat. She would know it in the morning. She did not look at
it.

The edges were receding.

She had not said this to herself in those words. She thought the
words now. The grain at the shoulder-edge was a degree narrower
than it had been at the city. The grain at the inside of the
elbow was starting to give up the inside of the elbow back to
skin. The body was not healing. The body was un-feeding a thing
it had been feeding. There was a difference. The difference was
the difference between a tree that stopped being watered and a
tree that was being tended back. The tree that was not being
watered did not get its old shape back. It got a smaller shape.
It lived at the smaller shape if the other conditions held.

She was going to get a smaller shape.

The shape would have her nose in it. Her nose would be the nose
she had been born with, sharpened by ten years of use, un-
sharpened by four years of drug. Less than what she had been
last week. More than what she would have been without the ten
years. A working instrument. Not the overclocked one.

She had smelled one professional man at three-quarters of a mile
today on a wind that had taken two breaths to give him up.

The instrument worked.

She lay in the cold with her arm out of the blanket and counted
the working instruments she had, the way she had used to count
kit at the start of a contract.

*Left hand, shaking, usable. Right hand, shaking, usable. Legs,
tired, usable. Eyes, tired, good at distance. Ears, off, coming
back in a week. Nose, working. Arm, losing bark at the edges.
Knife, in pack. Rope, in pack. Lock-tools, in pack. The man at
three-quarters of a mile, catalogued. The party, here, sleeping.
The disc in the dark-haired woman's coat, across the fold.*

She got to the end of the list.

She started again.

She stopped at *nose, working* and did not go further.

*Nose, working,* she thought. And then, because the ledger was
broken in places tonight but was not broken here: *mine.*

She pulled the blanket up over the arm. She closed her eyes.
Across the fold, Aldric made the small motion a man made at a
wall on watch when the hour changed — a shift of his weight from
one hip to the other — and the fold took the sound and kept it
and did not give it to the country outside.

Somewhere out in the heath, at a distance she could no longer
smell past the fold wall, a contract man was going to bed down
cold and wake at first light and press his quarter-pace.

She would be awake before he was.

The tremor in her left hand started up again under the blanket.
She did not catalogue it. The cataloguing, tonight, had done its
work.

She slept.
