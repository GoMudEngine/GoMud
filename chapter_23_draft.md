## Chapter Twenty-Three — *Maren*
### East

---

Morning came grey and cold, the kind of cold a harbor city put down
before dawn — salt in it, no friendliness. Maren was awake before the
second bell. She had been awake in small pieces for most of the night,
surfacing and settling and surfacing again, and when the bell went she
opened her eyes and stopped pretending.

Vane was already up.

She stood at the window with her back to the room, dressed, hair tied
back, coat over her shoulders. She did not turn when Maren sat up. She
was doing something with her hands at the sill — the economy of the
motion told Maren what she had already guessed. Vane was repacking a
small thing for the third or fourth time. She would do it again.

"Water's cold," Vane said, without turning. "I didn't build a fire. The
room won't smell of us by noon."

"All right."

---

Maren dressed in layers. The coat Ulla had given her went on last. The
disc was in its inner pocket, where it had been for three months now, and
she checked the pocket with her knuckle the way she checked a knife: not
to see, only to confirm. The disc was warm against her knuckle through
the wool. Faintly, more than it had been a week ago. She noted it and
went on.

She catalogued the room before she left it. Two beds, one stripped.
Basin empty. A coin for the landlady flat on the table. Nothing in the
crack between the boards. Nothing under the bed.

She had slept here for three weeks. She had not become used to it. She
had become less tense in it, which was not the same thing.

"Pack," Vane said.

Maren picked up her pack.

---

They went down to the street by the back stairs.

The back stairs gave onto an alley that gave onto a lane that gave onto
a bridge across one of the lesser canals, and the canal ran south-east
toward the ford. Vane had chosen the way. She had not described it
before. She described it now in a half-sentence as they turned the first
corner.

"Three crossings. Alley. Bridge. Boatyard. If I stop, you stop. If I keep
walking, you keep walking."

"All right."

They walked.

The city at this hour was carters and bakers and a thin scatter of
people on morning shifts. The light was still in the west behind the
ridge. The canal lamps had not been put out yet — tired yellow, wicks
kept up out of habit. The canal smell was up.

Maren walked two paces behind Vane. She had put herself there because
Vane was scouting.

Vane was scouting differently this morning. Her head was not turning at
the rate it usually turned. Her stride was even, but it was a stride
chosen to be even — a slightly-too-deliberate pace, a body not trusting
itself to move on instinct and putting a measure on the movement
instead. Her right hand was at her coat pocket, not inside it. Her
thumb rode the seam and used the seam to know where the hand was without
having to look.

Maren did not comment.

---

At the boatyard they met the others.

Edvar's cart stood at the inland end of the yard, hitched to a pair of
shaggy short-legged horses. Small cart — two-wheeled, high-sided, a
board-and-canvas job built for a mapmaker who had not meant to travel.
The iron-banded trunk was in the bed, roped down against the front
board, a tarp over it. Two water-skins and what was probably blankets
beside it.

Edvar was on the bench. Tela was not with him. Edvar's face had the
settled look of a man who had argued the question and closed it, and
a look like that did not invite re-opening.

Aldric was on foot beside a deep-chested bay, older than she had first
taken it for. Saddlebags packed flat against the leather. A bedroll. A
small sealed case she would have bet was a letter-kit. He wore a plain
dark travel coat without a cassock under it. He looked cold and alert
and a little awkward in his body — a man who had not chosen plain clothes
for thirty years, moving in them with the small stiffness of a body
learning to occupy itself without its professional scaffolding.

He inclined his head to Maren. More than a nod, less than a bow — a
pastoral gesture flattened into a traveller's greeting. She returned it
as a nod. He registered the return and added nothing.

Davan was at the back of the cart adjusting a strap. His skin at the
collar did the small thing it did when he looked up and found her — a
wash of blue-through-green that held and then settled to the low steady
blue he had been holding since the cooperage. He lifted the hand with
the strap in it. She lifted her chin in return.

Vane had already gone past all of them to the far end of the yard. She
was looking at the lane that ran from the yard to the river road. She
was standing very still.

---

Edvar clucked to the horses. The cart began to roll.

They went out of the boatyard in a loose line. Vane first, on foot, ahead
by ten paces. Aldric leading his bay behind Vane, not mounted yet. Then
the cart. Then Davan walking beside the near wheel with his hand on the
sideboard, not carrying anything, his pack already stowed under the tarp
with the trunk.

Maren walked in the middle, next to the cart on the off-wheel side. She
had not been told to. She had walked to that position because the cart
was on her left and the road-side was on her right, and the disc was on
her inside, and anyone wanting to reach her from either side would have
to cross something first — a wheel, an elbow, a horse's haunch. The
arithmetic was so automatic that she registered it only after she had
already arranged herself to it.

The ford was an hour's walk east.

---

The ford crossing was not the east gate.

The east gate ran a toll house and a ledger. The ledger got read. The
ford was gravel and a plank walkway on pilings, and a road on the far
side that climbed through cow pasture to the highland road. No
toll-house. No ledger.

Two riders had gone through the east gate two hours earlier under papers
Marrick had drawn up — decoys, Tela among them, Maren understood without
having asked. They would ride north at the first side-road and come back
by the river without actually leaving.

"If anyone watches the gate," Vane had said last night, "they'll see
what they came to see."

Maren was inside the lie, and the lie was a lie in her favor. She walked.

---

They crossed the plank walkway in single file.

The river was low. The gravel showed in long pale tongues on either side
of the pilings. A woman was in the river to her knees to the north,
washing something in a wooden tub. She did not look up. A cart ahead of
theirs was crossing in the other direction, a hay-wagon with its load
tied slack, the driver sitting high on the bench with his hat pulled
down against a cold that the morning was only now arranging into itself.
The driver nodded at Edvar as the two carts passed. Edvar nodded back
with the professional courtesy of carters who did not know each other
and did not particularly want to and who were not going to add to either
of their days by pretending otherwise.

Vane crossed last. She stopped in the middle of the planks and looked
south toward the harbor mouth and the ships at anchor along the eastern
quays. Two breaths' worth of stillness. Her right hand went inside the
coat pocket for the first time all morning and came out again with
nothing in it.

Then she walked the rest of the crossing.

---

On the far side of the ford the road climbed.

It climbed gently. A pasture road, rutted by cart wheels and the hooves
of the sheep that had been driven down this way to market for centuries.
The pasture fences were stone, not wood. The stones were old — lichen-grey
at their tops, their mortarless joints fitted by the slow patient work of
hands that had gone back into the ground. A rook called once from the far
side of a field and nothing answered it.

When the road came out of the last pasture gate and on to the highland
road proper, the air changed. The canal smell dropped off. The harbor
smell dropped off. What was left was grass, and the stone of the walls,
and the faint cold top-note of a winter that had not arrived but had
begun its arriving — a dryness at the back of the nose, an emptiness
the air took on before frost.

Maren had grown up with that air.

She let it into her lungs and did not say anything about it.

---

By mid-morning they were walking through upland pasture, and the city
was a grey smear behind the last ridge, and Vane had dropped back to
walk alongside the cart.

Not alongside Maren. Alongside the back wheel, at Davan's shoulder. She
walked at his pace for the length of a field, then dropped to the back
of the cart, then moved forward again, then to the side Maren was on.

She was reading the road. Vane's way of reading a road was not by
looking at it but by moving along it at different positions and letting
the road give her what it was going to give her from each angle. What
she was reading for would not be a footprint. It would be a shape.
Something moving at her pace at a distance. Something that kept its pace
when she changed hers.

Vane fell in at Maren's elbow.

"Left shoulder aches," Vane said. Quietly. Conversationally.

"Do you need —"

"No. Just telling you."

The telling-you was a kindness. Vane did not talk about her body
unprompted, ever. That she had said it at all meant she was saying it
for a reason — that the shoulder ache was something Maren should log so
that later, when other things showed up alongside it, Maren would not be
starting from zero.

"All right," Maren said.

Vane's eyes stayed on the road ahead. Her right hand was, again, not
inside the pocket. The seam was where she was keeping it.

"Nothing on our back yet," Vane said. "Long sightline across that last
pasture. I looked. Nothing."

"All right."

She dropped back to the rear of the cart.

---

They stopped at midday at a wayside trough — a stone trough sunk half
into the hill at a bend, fed by a pipe that went up into the slope
somewhere and delivered water cold enough to make the horses stamp. A
pair of sheep in the next field watched the humans at the trough with
the distant insulted attention of sheep watching people.

Edvar got down stiffly from the bench and rolled his shoulders. Aldric
held the horses' heads while they drank. Davan sat on a stone at the
edge of the road and took a piece of bread out of his coat pocket and
broke it in half and held out a half.

Maren sat beside him and took the bread.

She bit into it. It tasted of the oven and of an hour that had passed
without her in it.

Her father had carried bread east ten years ago.

She caught the thought and set it aside. Not away. Aside. She had been
doing this with the thought of her father all morning — let the thought
come, set it aside, come back to what was in front of her: the bread,
the shape of Vane ten paces up the road keeping a sightline east,
Aldric murmuring to a horse, Davan eating beside her with his
skin-collar at the steady low blue he had been at since the boatyard.

Davan did not ask how she was.

She appreciated that.

She ate her bread. When she had swallowed the last bite she wiped her
fingers on the edge of her coat and stood up, because the cart had
finished at the trough and the afternoon's walk had started.

---

The disc pulsed.

An hour into the afternoon, on a stretch of road where pasture had given
over to upland heath and the wind had come up steady out of the north.
She was walking with her hand inside her coat, a habit she had started
in the city and had not unmade, her knuckle against the inside pocket
where the disc sat.

The pulse came.

Not a jump in temperature. A kind of *arriving* of the warmth — the
disc remembering, briefly, that it was a warm object, then forgetting
again. Through her knuckle, up her wrist, gone before she would have
been able to say aloud that it had happened.

She did not take her hand out. She looked at the road.

*Two pulses today,* she thought. *First was in the dark before the bell.
This is the second.*

It was more than yesterday. Not by much. Yesterday had been one pulse at
sunset and one just after sleep. The day before that had been one. The
day before that had been the cooperage.

She catalogued the frequency and did not allow herself to draw a
conclusion from it. Drawing a conclusion from it was work for a night
with a fire in front of her and nothing to decide in the next hour. Right
now she was walking east. The disc was walking with her. Both of them
were warmer than they had been yesterday. That was the fact.

She walked.

---

Vane came up beside her twenty minutes later.

Not on the cart side. On the road side, at her right elbow, the position
Vane took when she wanted to say a thing without the cart hearing. The
two of them walked in step for a moment.

"How often," Vane said.

Maren did not pretend.

"Twice today. Both times it's been warmer than before."

"Inside."

"Under my knuckle."

Vane nodded once. "Through the coat?"

"Through the coat. Not through the coat to anyone else. Me only. Knuckle
only."

"Good."

Vane did not ask anything else. She walked beside Maren for a few more
paces and then let her pace drop and let the cart catch her and passed
around the back of it and came up on the other side by Davan. Davan, for
his part, had gone still on the road side — which was, Maren saw after
the fact, a stillness in response to something his body had read and
hers had not, because Davan's empathic sense was closer to the ground
than any measurement Maren had, and the disc had pulsed, and Davan had
felt the pulse as a weather he did not have a name for.

He did not look at her. He did not speak.

His skin at the collar had gone from the low steady blue to a low steady
green.

She was not meant to see it. She had seen it anyway. The seeing went
aside, with the thought of her father, for later.

---

Aldric fell in beside her in the last hour before dusk.

He had been walking ahead, beside his horse, not mounted. He had not
mounted the bay all day. Deference, she had assumed — a Keeper of thirty
years did not enter a group of four people who were walking by mounting
his horse to the head of the line.
He had been walking his horse the way Edvar was driving his cart: at the
pace the group set.

Now he slowed until the cart caught him, and he let the cart pass, and
he matched her stride.

For the space of two hundred yards he said nothing.

Maren did not look at him. She had learned in the cooperage basement
that Aldric's silences were working silences, and the working was not
hers to interrupt. She walked.

At the two-hundredth yard he spoke.

"I have been counting," he said.

The sentence arrived the way his sentences did — formally, with the
first word doing more work than most people asked of a first word. She
had heard him speak two nights ago at the cooperage for the first time.
She had catalogued his cadence then. It was with her now. She recognized
the opening move.

"Counting what," she said.

"Moons."

"Three."

"Yes."

He walked. He was not looking at the sky. The sky above the heath had
begun to do the thing it did at this hour at this season — a high clear
winter-blue flattening toward the horizon, and in the east the clear
pale edge of a moon not yet risen but gathering.

"My counting has been a professional occupation of mine for two months,"
Aldric said. "I have been counting what my office taught me was not
countable. I have not found the counting useful. I mention this only
because I am not going to count aloud while we walk, and I wanted you to
know it is not because I am not counting."

"All right."

The two words. She said them the way she said them in rooms where
something had settled at depth. Whether something had settled at depth
in this exchange she would find out later.

Aldric walked with her for another hundred yards, not counting aloud.
Then he fell back to the cart and took a word with Edvar about the
bay's shoe, and the cart's pace adjusted a degree, and Maren understood
without being told that he had come up to do a piece of work and had
done it and had left. The work was arrival. He had needed one sentence
with her on a road in daylight that did not involve an object on a
table.

She filed it. She walked.

---

They camped at dusk in a sheep-fold Edvar had known about — a
dry-stone enclosure in the lee of a rise, three walls standing and one
fallen, a dry trough at the open end. A stand of stunted hawthorns
inside the ring, branches bare and north-bent. No sheep in it for a
season.

Edvar drew the cart up to the fallen wall. Aldric tethered the horses
at the windward side of the hawthorns. Davan came back from the stream
with two water-skins. Vane walked the perimeter once, stood a long
moment on a rise a hundred paces beyond the fold, and came back in
without reporting what she had seen.

Maren laid the fire.

She had done this often enough on the road north that her hands did it
without her supervision. Tinder. Sticks. A lean of larger ones. Steel
against flint. She built it small. The fold walls would hold the light
in, but the light of a fire carried at night in country like this, and
Vane had not spoken but Maren had understood.

The five of them gathered around it.

Edvar on the stone trough with his knees up like a man half his age.
Aldric on a folded blanket with his back against the dry-stone wall.
Davan cross-legged on the ground, his back to the open side of the fold,
keeping the lookout position without being asked. Maren opposite him
with the fire between them, her shoulders against the stand of
hawthorns. Vane at the end of the fire on the windward side, closer to
the flame than the others, her coat wrapped around her shoulders and her
hands inside the cuffs.

Vane kept moving closer to the fire. Maren did not comment.

---

They did not talk much.

Edvar sighed once, the sigh of a man at the end of a day he had agreed
to take on and had not regretted yet but might. Aldric took out a small
book from his coat — not a journal; an old traveler's almanac — and
opened it on his knee and did not read it. The book was a prop that
allowed him to keep his hands occupied while his thoughts did the work
his hands were not required for.

Davan carved.

He had a piece of pale wood between his hands that he had picked up at
the trough stop and had not shown to her at the time. It was hawthorn,
cut clean. His knife was small and steady. He carved without watching
what he carved. He had done this on the road north, she knew — had
carved something of her before he had ever seen her, a pine figure with
one arm held close to the body. The carving she had held in the
cooperage yard and had said nothing about for a long time.

She did not know what he was carving now. She did not ask. The steady
small sound of the blade on wood was, in the fold, a warmer sound than
the fire.

Vane was quiet.

She was closer to the fire than was useful, and her hands inside her
cuffs were shaking finely at the fingertips — Maren could see it in the
motion of the cuff-fabric. Vane's eyes were open, on the fire. Her jaw
was set the way a jaw set when a body was running a small internal
current and the jaw was one of the places the current surfaced.

Maren did not go over. Going over would have been a gesture, and Vane's
body was not asking for a gesture. Maren had watched her mother work
through things like this — not withdrawal, her mother had had other
demons, but the same shape. A body working through something alone in
company. The company mattered. To her mother, the alone had mattered
more.

Maren laid another stick on the fire. She set it carefully on the
leeward side so the fire wouldn't flare. The flame took it.

---

After a while Aldric closed the almanac.

"I will take the first watch," he said. "If the group will have it."

He did not look at Vane when he said it. He did not look at Maren. He
had pitched the sentence to the fire and let the sentence do its own
choosing of whom it belonged to. A Keeper's move — and also, the shape
of it arriving a beat late, the move of a man in a group in which
nobody would delegate to him, who was finding his footing by the oldest
move he had: volunteer for a duty before anyone asked him to take it.

He was not trying to lead. He was trying not to be waited on. The
effort of it was audible in the sentence.

Vane did not look up from the fire.

"Wake me at second watch," she said. Her voice came out even. The thing
in her hands did not show in the voice. "I'll take the dead hour before
dawn."

"The dead hour is hard," Aldric said.

"I know."

"I will wake you."

Davan did not volunteer. Maren did not volunteer. Edvar, already,
through the silence that followed, had begun to breathe the slow
evening breath of a man asleep against a wall.

That was the first watch sorted.

The second watch, Maren understood, was Vane's claim on doing work she
could still do. Maren had been making claims like that on the road
north, in the days after the ditch, when her ankle had been bad and she
had wanted to walk the second shift to prove to herself she could. The
claim was not about the watch. It was about not becoming, yet, the
person who was being worked around.

Maren filed the parallel. She did not extend it aloud.

---

Before she slept she looked at the sky once.

The three moons were up.

The Steady was low over the northern ridge, pale as milk-water. The
rust-tinted one was higher, east and smaller. The fastest had come
over the shoulder of the rise in the last hour and stood near the
zenith, small and hard and bright. Three. At their stations. Doing
whatever it was moons did, above a country that had stopped asking.

She looked at them without counting.

She had been avoiding the moons for three years, since the first night
on the road — eyes low, hood up, attention on the ground. A habit.
Avoidance was always a habit eventually. Whether tonight she was
breaking it or only putting it down for a minute to rest her neck she
did not know.

The disc, under her hand through the coat, pulsed once.

Three pulses today. More than yesterday. More than the day before.

Her father had walked under these moons ten years ago and had not come
back. He had carried this disc and left it behind for her to carry
after him, and she had carried it three years thinking she was carrying
grief, and then she had sat in a basement and understood she was
carrying a test, and now she was on a road east, first day of five,
with a cart and a trunk and a Keeper and a hunter in withdrawal and a
woodcarver who had read her before she knew she had been read, and the
disc under her hand was warmer than yesterday, and the
warmer-than-yesterday was not a sentence she had finished yet.

She was not walking toward a grave. She had stopped walking toward a
grave at the cooperage. What she was walking toward she did not yet
know. She had thought in the basement that she would arrive at a shape
for it before she left the city. She had not. The reframing was still
happening, the way some changes went on happening in the body for weeks
after the event that caused them, and the body was hers, and she did
not get to hurry it.

*All right,* she thought. Not said. The two words to herself, the
private register.

Not all right. Arranged. Put down.

She turned her face from the moons and put her head against the pack
she had rolled up for a pillow. The fire was low. Aldric was at the
wall on first watch, almanac closed on his knee, eyes on the open side
of the fold. Vane was on the far side of the fire with her hands in
her cuffs and her jaw set. Davan was already asleep, the hawthorn
piece finished in his palm.

Edvar snored once and settled.

Four pulses.

She had counted the fourth without meaning to. It had come in the last
breath before sleep, under her hand, against her ribs. Not loud. Not
urgent. Only, every few hours, the disc remembering itself a degree
further than it had remembered itself yesterday, and she the one
carrying the rememberer, and the road five days long, and she had come
one day.

She slept.
