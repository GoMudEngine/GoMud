## Chapter Twenty-Seven — *Maren*
### The Hill

---

Day five opened in a light that did not match the day.

The heath behind them had kept the grey overcast of the coast for two
days, a weather that did not break and did not deepen. That morning
broke. Maren came out from under the hedgerow where she had slept and
stood at the cart-pole and looked east and the east had a pale blue
edge above the ridge-line, thin and unconvincing, and the sky behind
her was still the sky of the coast. Two weathers. A seam between them
running north-to-south along a line Maren could not quite place until
the wind gave it to her — the wind had changed at some hour while she
slept, and was coming now off the east country, dry, with no salt in
it.

Dry air off dry land. That was the eastern weather line and they had
crossed it overnight.

She catalogued the camp. Cart in the lee of a long stone wall, Edvar
still asleep under the bench, the horses cropping at a forage she did
not know the name of. Aldric had the last watch and was sitting with
his back to an outcrop at the wall's end, the bay tethered at his
knee. Davan was at the water-skins. Vane was not visible.

Vane was not visible because Vane had been up and moving since the
watch changed, and Vane worked the perimeter by a pattern Maren had
stopped trying to track. Vane would be back when Vane was done.

Her hand was inside her coat at the pocket without her asking it to
be there.

The disc was warm against the pad of her thumb. Not the faint warmth
of the early morning. A steady warmth that had been there when she
woke — that had *woken her*, before the light had. She understood
this on the second breath. It pulsed once under her thumb and she
registered the pulse and took her hand out.

She did not look at the east.

Not yet. The east would be there when she was ready to look at it.
She went to the water-skins first, because the water-skins were what
you did before the day, and today was a day on which she wanted to
have done the things you did before the day.

---

Davan was filling the second skin at the stream.

He looked up when she came. His skin at the open collar carried a
sustained green — not the green-gold of the face, but a held lower
register, the green he had walked in for two days and had not
mentioned. The color at the wrist was deeper this morning. Not
fluttering. Set. As if something he had been holding at bay had given
up being held and had simply begun to be.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning."

He passed her the full skin without her asking. She took it and
shouldered it and stood beside him at the stream. The stream ran
clear over a bed of flat pale stones. Small.

"You slept," she said.

"Some."

"You're lit."

"I know."

He capped the second skin. His hands were steady. She watched them
because watching his hands was a way of watching him that did not
require him to say anything, and this morning he did not look like a
person she wanted to ask questions of. He looked like a person
carrying a weight with both hands.

"Since when," she said.

"Since the wind changed."

"The wind changed before dawn."

"Since then."

She nodded.

He did not tell her the rest. The rest would be what Davan brought to
the cart when Davan had finished circling it, and she had learned to
let him circle. She capped her skin. The water was very cold in the
bend of her arm.

"Maren."

She looked at him.

His eyes were not on her. They were on the east, above the ridge-line
where the pale blue had opened. He did not lift his hand to point.
Pointing was not the register. He said her name and he looked east
and his collar-color deepened by a shade she had not seen before —
not green-gold, not yet; a green with a warmer edge coming into it at
the base, the color of a thing beginning to be ready.

"Yes," she said. "I'll look."

She turned.

---

The ridge was ten miles off. Maybe less. The country east of the
heath rolled away in a long slow fall to a plain she could not see
the bottom of, and beyond the plain the ridge-line went up in the
pale unstained blue of the new weather. Forested at the bottom. Bare
rock toward the top. A long crest with three or four bumps along it,
the kind of ridge-line that resolved at closer distance into separate
hills.

Behind the ridge, further in, higher up, there was a hill that was not
any of the bumps.

She did not see it immediately. The light came first. The
sun was not up yet — the light was the pre-light, the grey-bluing
that filled the east before a dry clear morning — and along the ridge
the pre-light was picking out the usual edges: rock-face, tree-line,
snow in the higher gullies. Beyond the ridge, in what should have
been shadow, something caught a light that wasn't there yet.

A pale dull gleam. Not reflection. Not brightness. A not-quite-shadow
in a place that ought to have been shadow. The eye moved past it
because the eye was looking for something else, and then the eye came
back.

She looked.

The hill was long. She could see the length of it from the heath
because the ridge in front of it was lower than it was. Long and
low-backed and running roughly east-southeast, rising not in a peak
but in a long swell that came up from the land and settled back into
it without a summit. Her first thought was *esker* — the word Edvar
had taught her for the long glacial ridges south of Stillwater — and
her second thought was that eskers did not do that with light. An
esker took a light the way grass took it. Softly. This took it the
way a wet slate did. Dully. Held for a moment. Released.

It was the wrong shape for a hill.

That was the sentence she arrived at on the second look. Not too
large. Not too small. *Wrong.* A hill was a thing that had been
shaped by water and weather and the slow compression of the weight of
the country above it. This thing had been shaped by something else.
The proportions were too long for the height. The back was too even.
The flanks curved at a rate a hillside did not curve — curved at an
even rate, the rate of a thing that had been made at a scale and then
buried.

Centuries of growth. Vines and roots and sediment in every hollow.
The long overgrowth had given it the texture of a hill and almost the
silhouette of one, and from the valley road — Maren understood this
without having been on the valley road — from the valley road at any
hour but this one, at any angle but this one, at any light but the
sideways pre-light of the wrong weather, the thing would read as a
hill.

It was not a hill.

She had known this from Reth's report, carried to her through Aldric
on the third evening east. Four hundred meters. No seam. No grain.
Manufactured surface. She had heard the words and held them in a room
in her head where she held the words for things that were real.
Seeing the thing was not the same as having the words for it. Seeing
the thing was a door opening in the room, and the thing walking
through.

Her hand was inside her coat. The disc was humming.

Not pulsing. *Humming.* A low continuous warmth against her palm that
had not been there ten minutes ago at the stream. She closed her
fingers around it and opened them and closed them again and the hum
did not change, did not fall away at the breath between pulses as it
had always done. The breath between pulses had closed up. The disc
was doing the thing it had been doing more and more often all month,
now without stopping.

"All right," she said. Aloud. To no one.

It meant something had settled.

---

Aldric had come down off the outcrop.

He was on the heath ten paces from her with the bay at his shoulder
and he was looking east with both hands on the reins in front of him,
palms up, the reins laid across the palms the way a man laid a
sentence on a table for reading. She had not heard him come. She had
not needed to. She stepped across the heath to him and stood at the
bay's off-shoulder and did not speak.

He spoke first.

"Four hundred meters lateral extent."

His voice was measured. The voice she had learned from him was the
voice he used to count. A Keeper's voice, he had called it once,
without making it a joke — the voice of a man trained to receive an
account. He was receiving an account now. The account was the shape
on the ridge.

"Running east-southeast," he said. "Low at the southwestern end.
Rising to a crest at about the midpoint. The crest is not a summit.
It holds level for — I can't estimate from here. Then the curve comes
down at the southeastern end. Curves *down*. That is the piece Reth
marked. The surface goes into the ground there."

"Goes in," she said.

"He said it. *The thing goes into the ground, Keeper. It doesn't just
sit on it.* I did not understand at the time what he was describing.
I understand now."

She understood too. The long swell on the ridge was not a ridge-top
feature. It was a piece of a thing that had come to rest at an angle
in the country, the larger part of it buried under the hillside, the
smaller part of it showing. A thing lying down in the earth. A thing
that had *come down* and the earth had taken most of it, and the part
that still showed had gone under grass and vine and tree and had
become, for every eye that had looked at it without the words, a
hill.

"Reth's recess," she said.

"Southeastern end. Where the curve goes down."

"How far in."

"From the angle and the light — I can't be precise. An hour's walk
from the base. Perhaps less if the way is clear."

"It won't be clear."

"No."

He did not lift his hand to the disc in her coat. He did not ask. She
offered it anyway, without taking it out. "It's been humming since
dawn."

"Humming."

"Not pulses. Continuous."

He took this in. He had put his hand in his pocket for nothing twice
in the last hour — the shape of the lamp-tic without the tic, she
thought, not a reach but a ghost of one, fading. His hand on the
reins did not move.

"We should go," he said. "Before the rest of the day happens to us."

---

They broke camp without speaking.

Edvar was awake by the time she got back to the cart and did not ask
why they were packing early; Edvar had learned a great deal in his
sixty years about not asking. Davan folded the canvas. Maren rolled
the blankets and packed the kettle wet because there was no time for
the kettle to dry. Aldric tightened the bay's girth a hole tighter
than yesterday. The bay was losing weight.

Vane came back from her circuit as the last strap went down.

She looked at the east without going still the way Maren had gone
still. Maren understood this later; Vane had seen the hill already,
had seen it on her perimeter circuit in the pre-light and had walked
the rest of the circuit with what she had seen cataloged and put in
its proper slot, which was the slot for the thing they had come here
for. Vane's eyes came back from the east and went to Maren and did
not stay.

"He's closer," Vane said.

"How close."

"Half a mile. Maybe a little more. He pushed pace last night. He
knows we've seen the ground ahead."

"He can see it too."

"He's not looking at it the way you're looking at it."

Maren nodded.

"He won't close before the base of the ridge," Vane said. "Professional.
He'll stay at half a mile until he has cover. I'll take the back
line. Davan point. Aldric the cart. You with the disc."

"Me with the disc."

"That was the job from the ford. It's still the job."

A beat. Vane's left hand at her side was shaking at a rate Maren had
not seen it shake before. She did not hide it. She had stopped hiding
it three days ago.

"Can you work," Maren said.

"Yes."

"If you can't."

"You'll know. You'll put me in the cart and go on. The way we
agreed."

"All right."

Vane turned. She went to the back of the line and took her place
without further sentence. Davan took point. The cart rolled.

Edvar on the bench, the reins in his hands. Maren walked at the
off-wheel with her hand inside her coat. She did not take the disc
out. Taking the disc out was for the place. The place was ahead of
them across five miles of heath and two gullies and a rising tree
line and the curve of an approach she had seen all her life in
dreams her mother had not lived to interpret for her.

The wind was at her back, pushing her into the east.

---

They dropped off the heath an hour before noon.

The country folded. A dry creek bed in a trough of sedge gave the
cart half an hour of difficult going; Edvar worked the reins and
Davan walked at the near wheel to steady the trunk; the trunk, Maren
knew without being told, was the face in its oiled canvas, and the
face in its oiled canvas was part of this going-east in a way the
earlier going-east had not been. The face at the fold had been
evidence. The face in the trunk today was a piece of the ship riding
in a cart toward the ship.

She did not think this for long. She did not want to think it for
long. There was a register at which the trip became too clean — *the
piece returning to the whole* — and the register was not hers. She
was a girl walking east with a thing in her coat. The thing was warm.
The east was an hour away.

The ridge came up.

They took it where Edvar had picked the line off an older map —
Reth's directions through Aldric, layered with Edvar's road-sense,
laid onto a track that hunters had used and farmers before them. The
track climbed through the tree-line and out the other side onto a
shoulder of bare stone. They left the cart at the shoulder. Edvar
stayed with it. He had said, half a mile back, *I'll stand the cart*,
and nobody had said otherwise, and Maren understood why he had said
it — Edvar was not going the rest of the way; Edvar had gone far
enough.

She hugged him, briefly, before she went on.

He put his hand on her hair and took it away.

"Go," he said.

"I'll come back."

"You'll come back when you come back."

---

The approach was half an hour.

Davan walked point at a pace that was not his usual pace. He was
moving the way he moved when his skin had work to do and his legs
were trying to give his skin room for the work. His collar was green
now with the warm edge coming up from underneath — not green-gold.
Close to it. A green with a live under-register, as if the color
itself had a pulse, the pulse running at a rate that did not match
his heart.

Aldric came behind her. The bay had been left with the cart. Aldric
walked with a scholar's rolling stride, measured, the rhythm of a man
who had walked aisles.

Vane was behind Aldric. She had not changed position. The back line.

Maren walked the middle. She walked with one hand at her coat pocket
and the disc humming against her palm through wool and the other
hand at the knife at her belt, which she did not need and did not
take out, because the hand knew how to be there and she let the hand
do what the hand did.

The tree-line thinned.

The slope under her boots was the slope of a hillside but it was not
the slope of a hillside. It was too steady. Too even. The angle
varied within a range a natural slope did not vary within. The
ground was softer than it should have been where the growth had
pillowed over it, and harder than it should have been where the
growth had given way — and where it had given way, under a split of
moss knocked loose by some animal, something pale and flat showed
through, for the first time, that was not stone.

She did not stop. Davan, ahead, did not stop. He had seen it too.
There was seeing and there was stopping, and today they were going to
save their stopping for the thing Reth had described.

They climbed.

---

Davan's skin was green-gold by the time they came out onto the
shoulder of the hill.

Not sustained the way it had been at Edvar's. Deeper. A green-gold
that had a second color under it, the warmer color she had not seen
rise to the surface yet, the color she understood without language
was not going to surface until the disc was in the recess. His hand
at his side was trembling in the way Vane's had trembled at the cart
— not from cost, in his case. From the work of holding.

"Slow," he said. Not to her. To himself.

"All right," she said, because she had it to give him.

Aldric came up beside her. His breathing was the breathing of a
fifty-three-year-old man who had walked a slope. His hand at his coat
was closed on nothing. No tic. The hand was flat against the wool.

He looked along the hillside.

"There," he said.

She looked where he was looking.

At first only the green. The slope of the hill rolled away
from them under the dense overgrowth, vines ropey at the ankle,
deeper-rooted plants higher up, a thorn-cover in the middle-ground
that she would have taken for a briar-patch in any other country.
Under the green was the shape of the hill — curved, long, going down
and away to the southeast, the land folding to meet it in a way Maren
understood now was the land having folded *around* it, the way water
folded around a stone set in a stream.

She followed the curve with her eye down to the southeastern end.

Where the slope went down into the country, the overgrowth changed.
The thorn was sparser there. The grass ran in lines. Where the grass
ran in lines, there were longer lines underneath — longer than any
root, more even than any grass, more deliberate than the lines
erosion made in the sides of hills. The lines curved.

Three of them first. Three nested arcs, shallow, faint under the
grass, following the curve of the hillside's surface as it went down
into the ground.

She counted.

Not three. Four. The fourth arc was further out, drifting from the
three, the drift she had looked at on her own disc's edge for three
years and had thought was a flaw in the casting and had learned, at
the cooperage, was the shape the book of the world made when a
cartographer had stood far enough back from his own map to see it.

The inner orbit symbol. On the hill.

"Carved," she said, and as she said it she was already correcting
herself because it was the wrong word — her mouth had reached for it
before her eye had finished looking, and her eye was telling her a
thing her mouth had not caught up to.

"No," Aldric said. Quietly.

She looked longer.

The arcs were not cut into the surface. The grass had grown over
them. The roots of the larger plants had followed them — ran along
the lines, not across them, the way tree roots in an old orchard
followed the rows a plowman had cut two centuries ago. The plants
were growing along the lines because the lines had been there first.
The lines had been there before there was soil on the hill. The soil
had settled into the lines because the lines had been an *unevenness*
in the surface below the soil, a depression in whatever the hill was
made of, a shape pressed into the hull of the thing before there had
been any grass on Gaius at all.

The symbol was not on the hill.

The symbol *was* the hill.

Not the whole hill. The piece of the hill that showed. The material
of the hull had been marked at its making with the diagram of the
mission it had been part of, and the mark had been there before any
hand from this country had ever thought of making a mark, and ten
thousand years of roots and vines and sediment had grown across the
mark and into it and had become, to the eye looking from a valley
road in any ordinary light, *a hill.*

Her disc.

The inner orbit symbol on its edge. Three years of tracing it with
her thumb without knowing what she was tracing. A shape made at
manufacture — one thing to the maker, something else to all the
people who had come after. She
thought of her father, who had never seen the disc and the hill in
the same room, and who had walked east to try to put them in the
same room, and who had not.

Her eye went from the symbol on the hillside to the southeastern end
and down.

Down to where the curve of the hill went into the ground.

Down to where, in the quieter place below the drifting fourth arc,
the cleared ground Reth had described lay — a patch of earth not
overgrown, not by accident, where a hand had cleared sediment from
the surface a generation ago and left it cleared, and where the
surface below it showed.

A disc-shaped recess.

Dark. Small. Coin-sized at this distance, disc-sized when she got to
it.

Her breath went out.

She walked toward it.

---

She found him halfway there.

She was not looking for him. She was looking at the recess, and her
body was walking under the part of her that was looking, and her
foot at the fifth pace came down on something that was not the
springy compressed growth of the hillside. It was harder than that.
She looked down.

A leather strap. Flat under her boot. Cracked from years of weather,
the oil long gone out of it. She lifted her foot. The strap lay
against the ground curving slightly — the curve of a thing that had
once gone around another thing. A pack-strap. The kind a man took
east on a long journey.

She did not call out.

She went slowly down the slope from the strap, three paces, five, her
eye tracking what had been the line of the strap before it went
under the growth, and at seven paces the growth opened around a
scatter of dark objects arranged at distances no object placed itself
at.

A coat. Wool. Once grey, now the color weather made. The shoulders
of the coat were not in the shape of a body any more; the shoulders
of the coat were in the shape of the ground. Everything that had
filled the coat ten years ago had gone back to the hill at the rate
the hill took things back. Inside the coat — she did not look closely
into the coat — were the long pale shapes a body reduced to when the
country had worked on it for a decade. Bones. Not many, where there
should have been many. Animals had come. The animals had taken what
animals took. The rest had stayed because the coat had kept the rest
together while everything else went.

She knew the coat.

She had not seen it on him. She had been six. But she had heard Ulla
describe it the day Ulla had given her the letter — *your father's
coat was a soft grey, with dark facings at the cuffs, and a seam
along the shoulder where your grandmother mended it after the winter
of the deep snow.* The facings at the cuffs were black-weathered
almost beyond reading. The seam at the shoulder was a seam.

She went down to her knees beside the coat. She did not touch it.

The satchel was at what had been his hand.

A leather satchel, also gone dark with weather, the strap broken
where the hand had held it. She touched the strap. The leather came
apart in her fingers, not at the fracture but two inches above it,
like old bread. She drew her hand back.

Inside the satchel — she had to lean to see — were three things she
could make out without disturbing it. A small bound book wrapped in
what had been oiled cloth; the oil was gone and the cloth was not
protecting the book any more, but something in the shape of the
binding said the book had been kept dry for a long time. A fire-tin
with a flint. A stone.

The stone was the size of an egg. Round. River-smoothed. The kind of
stone a man picked up because the shape of the stone felt correct
under the thumb and the thumb wanted to keep it.

She looked at the stone and did not take it.

She looked at the coat and did not touch it.

She looked along the hand-line — the line of where his hands had
lain when he had settled, in whatever last minute of whatever last
hour, into the ground here — and she followed the hand-line down the
slope with her eyes.

The hand-line pointed at the recess.

He had been facing it. He had gone as far as he had gone, and he had
stopped where he had stopped, and he had stopped four paces short of
the recess, and he had lain down with his hands out in front of him
and his face toward the thing he had come across the country to put
the key into. The key had not been with him. The key had been in a
coal-miner's cottage in a hamlet called Ashwick, in the coat of an
infant nobody had named Maren yet, carried there by some route she
would not know the shape of for the rest of her life. The key had
been there, and the lock had been here, and the man who had known
about both had lain down four paces from the lock with his hands
out.

She did not say anything.

She did not weep. The weeping was a country she could not afford
today. She took a breath and did not take another, and then she took
another anyway because the body did what the body did, and she sat
back on her heels in the grass beside what was left of her father
and she put her hand inside her coat and she held the disc against
her palm and let it hum.

She had been carrying grief. The cooperage had taught her she was
carrying a test. The hill taught her what she had not had the words
for, which was that she had been carrying both, and both had been
true, and the reframe had not been an exchange. It had been an
addition. Her father had died here. Her father had also left her the
thing that opened the thing he had died beside. Both at once. No
contradiction.

*All right,* she thought. Not aloud.

She put her hand on the ground beside the coat — not on the coat —
and she held it there for the length of one breath. The ground was
warmer than the air. She did not know if that was the hillside or
the morning sun or the thing she was kneeling beside. She did not
look for an explanation.

She took her hand up.

She stood.

---

Aldric had come within a pace and had stopped.

His eyes were on the coat and the hand-line and the recess in one
long look that took them all in as a shape. He did not go closer. He
had the sense to know that this was not a room he entered.

"Ten years," he said. Quietly.

"Ten years."

"He knew where it was."

"Yes."

"He had nothing to put in it."

"He had me. He had me in Ashwick and the disc was with me. He came
east anyway."

Aldric closed his eyes for a breath. When he opened them he did not
look at her, which was a kindness.

"The recess," he said.

"The recess."

She stepped past the coat.

Davan was at the downslope, ten paces on. He had not come closer to
the remains. He had not had to — his skin had told him at the
distance his skin told him. The green-gold at his throat was no
longer green-gold. It was a color Maren could not name. It had the
green in it and the gold in it and something else under both, a warm
register coming up from the chest and the wrist both, held, not
pulsing, the way his skin had held the recognition at the face but
deeper and wider and at a register she had not known a body could
hold at. He looked like a lamp with a color inside it the lamp-maker
had never tried before.

"Davan."

He looked at her. His pupils were wide.

"Can you stand here," she said.

"Yes."

"All right."

She turned to the recess.

---

It was six feet below her. A short clear distance down the slope.

The cleared section was perhaps three paces across. The surface of
the hill in that cleared section was not green. It was not grass and
not soil. It was the dark material she had spent three months
learning the look of — the material of the face, the material of the
container, the material of her own disc — but at a scale that was
not possible for a thing to be. Not a coin. A continent of the
material, extending off under the soil in every direction, visible
only here where someone had scraped a window clear. The surface was
smooth. Not polished. Smooth the way the disc was smooth, at the
scale of a room.

The recess was in the middle of the cleared section.

Circular. Sunk perhaps a quarter-inch into the surface. The size of
the disc exactly. On the inner face of the recess — visible as she
stepped down into the cleared place and bent, because the angle
from above had not been the angle for reading it — was
the same symbol. The inner orbit. Four arcs. The fourth drifting
off-true.

Not carved into the inner face. Part of it.

She knelt.

The ground at the edge of the cleared section was firm under her
knee. The surface of the hill at the edge of the cleared section was
cool under her free hand — she put her hand flat against it, not
pressing, only resting, and the cool went into her palm and did not
travel further, the way stone took cool and kept it at the surface.
It was not stone. It felt like stone. It felt like nothing else she
had touched before.

She took her hand off.

She took the disc out of her coat.

It was warm. Almost hot. The hum in her palm had gone past the point
where she could call it a hum; it was a fine continuous pressure, a
thing-wanting-to-go-somewhere, and she had understood at the cooperage
that this was what the disc did when it was near the thing it had
been made to meet, but she had not understood *this*. The disc in her
palm was a thing asking to leave her palm. She had been a holder for
it for seventeen years and she was about to stop being the holder,
and the disc knew.

She looked at the symbol on the edge of the disc.

She looked at the symbol on the inner face of the recess.

The same symbol.

"Papa," she said. Very quietly. The old word. She had not used the
word since she was six. She used it as a small acknowledgement, the
way you tapped a door-frame as you went past a room somebody you had
loved had slept in. A courtesy
between her and the country.

She did not weep.

She held the disc over the recess.

---

The disc fit.

Her hand was steady. She had expected her hand to shake and her hand
did not shake, which was information she tucked in a pocket for
later. The disc went into the recess the way a key went into a lock
that had been made for the key. Not loose. Not tight. Exactly fitted.

The contact came before the seating. A moment where the material of
the disc met the material of the hill — not a click, not a sound at
all, but a joining, a thing-become-one-thing under her thumb — and
then the seating, a quarter-inch of settle, the disc sinking the
rest of the way into the recess and stopping at a depth that was
the correct depth.

Her thumb was on the symbol on the back of the disc. She pressed.

She had not been told to press. The pressing was what the hand knew.
The thumb went down on the inner orbit and the disc sat the last
fraction of its depth into the hillside and then the hum in her palm
that had been continuous all morning — *stopped.*

Not faded. *Stopped.*

The disc under her thumb went still. Completely. For the first time
since she had taken it out of her coat three years ago and held it
for the first time with a hand that knew what it was, the disc was
*quiet* in her hand.

A beat.

Her breath was out. She had not inhaled. She inhaled.

The quiet held for the space of that breath.

Then.

---

Under her knee, the hill.

Not a shake. Not a tremor. A *shift* at a register her body caught
before her mind caught it — a change in the way the ground was under
her, the way a room changed when a furnace came on in another part
of a house, before any sound from the furnace had reached you. The
change went through the hillside and through her kneecap and up her
thigh and stopped somewhere in her ribs.

The air changed.

Not the wind. The *air*. The air between her body and the hillside
went from morning-cool to a different cool — a dry held cool that
had no weather in it. The air an interior had. The air of a room
that had been closed for a very long time, and was no longer closed,
and was meeting the morning for the first time across a threshold
that had just gone un-sealed.

Somewhere below her — below the hillside, below the disc, below
depths she had no measure for — a sound started.

Not a sound at the ear. A sound at the bone. A low continuous
register at the edge of hearing, deeper than the disc's hum had
been, wider, the sound a thing the size of the hill made when the
thing the size of the hill began, after ten thousand years, to do
the work it had been built for.

Davan made a sound above her.

She did not look up. She could not look up yet. Her eye was on the
disc in the recess, and the disc in the recess was no longer dark.
Along the symbol on its face — along the four nested arcs, along the
drifting fourth — a faint light had come up. Not blue. Not the blue
the disc had given her in the woodcutter's shelter and the Stillwater
kitchen and the cooperage basement. A color she had not seen the
disc make before. Warmer. The color of morning sun through a leaf,
held at its brightest for a breath and then not fading but *settling*
— the color finding its working register and staying there.

The symbol lit under her thumb.

The hillside lit under her knee.

Not the whole hillside. The four arcs of the symbol she had seen
under the grass, the arcs that had been part of the surface before
any of this country's grass had ever grown — the arcs began, faintly,
to be visible through the growth. Not bright. Just a suggestion that
the material was no longer inert. That something under the hill
was waking to the signal at the recess and beginning, with the slow
patience of something that had been waiting, to answer.

Aldric, behind her, said one word.

She did not hear it. Her ear was full of the bone-sound. She
registered his voice at the level of vibration in the air and did
not turn.

Her hand was on the disc. The disc was seated. The seating was done.

The hillside had woken under her knee. The air had changed. The
bone-sound was sending. The symbol under her thumb was lit with a
color the disc had never produced in her hand before.

She knelt.

She kept her thumb on the symbol.

She did not take it off.

Behind her, down the hand-line that had pointed at this recess for
ten years, the coat lay in the grass with nothing in it, and a
father who had not reached this seating had reached, through her,
the thing he had walked east to reach. She did not look back at the
coat. She looked at the recess. She looked at the light coming up
under her thumb. She kept her thumb where her thumb was.

The hill answered under her.

She held on.
