## Chapter Twenty-Five — *Aldric*
### The Shape of Old Things

---

The second watch was not the cruelty people said it was.

At the Confluence Aldric had stood the watch before Lauds for thirty
years — the hour when the chapel was coldest and the sanctuary lamp
needed the longest sight to see to — and he had come to a private
understanding with the small hours that they were simply awake. Other
hours were fuller. Other hours were more pleasant. But the watch did
not pretend to be anything, and a priest could not, after a certain
point, tell the difference between the pretence of other hours and
their substance, and so the watch had become, in its way, honest.

He sat against the north-bent ash with Edvar's lamp hooded at his
feet and watched the open side of the cart-stand through the dark.
Vane had taken the first watch and gone to her blanket without
speaking; her breathing, after half an hour, had quieted into
something that might have been sleep and might only have been the
body finishing a day. Maren slept the watchful sleep of a person
who had done it in barns. Davan, a man whose body had worked all day
at carrying what his wrists had carried, slept without motion. Edvar
slept loudly and without apology.

Aldric's hand had reached once, in the first quarter hour, for a
wick that was not there. The ember at the lamp had wanted tending in
the old way — pinched between thumb and forefinger, the small
pastoral trim — and his fingers had come up off his knee and had
gone the short distance and had closed on nothing.

He had not caught himself.

His fingers had opened before they closed. Not because he had
decided. Because there had been nothing there to close on, and the
hand, finding nothing, had come back to his knee without being told.

He had not marked the motion. He had looked at his hand after, on
his knee, and the hand had done a thing without his supervision and
the thing had been a thing not done.

---

The eastern sky began to think about its morning a long time before
it committed to it. Grey at the lip first, then a paler grey, then
the thinnest suggestion of colour that was not yet colour. A rook
called from some field a mile off and another answered.

The thing the hand had done, which was the not-doing, was a small
thing. He noted it.

---

They broke camp in the pale.

Edvar rose stiffly and poked at the coals and found nothing but ash
under the stick. "Clean," he said, and the word meant the camp would
leave no trace. Vane was out on her perimeter circuit before Aldric
had found his second boot. On her return she gave Maren half a shake
of the head, no sentence under it — nothing on the back — and went
on with her pack.

Aldric put a hand against the bay's warm neck as he cinched. She
leaned her head into his shoulder briefly and he let her. He
tightened the girth a hole. The hole was new. Four days on the road,
three camps, and the weight that had always gone first on an old
horse in autumn was going first on her now.

Davan came past him with the water-skins. The young man's skin at
the open collar was pale, the ghost of last night's green-gold still
in it the way a stain stayed in a cloth after washing. He nodded to
Aldric. Aldric nodded back. Neither of them had spoken since the
lamp had gone down over the blanket.

The thing on the blanket was in the trunk in the cart. The thing in
Aldric's chest was not yet in any container he had words for, and
the morning was not going to put it in one. The cart was harnessed.
The fold was clean. He mounted the bay.

---

The road took them out of the ash-lee in a long climb.

They were at the foot of Irongate country. The name had always
sounded larger than the place. *Irongate* suggested an outpost, a
wall, a border-fort holding a line. The reality, as Brennan had told
him on the ride east to Greenford, was that Irongate referred not to
a construction but to a shape of country — a long narrow gap between
two shoulders of high bedrock, running east-northeast, a gate carved
by glaciers before there were men to call any of it by any name. The
iron in the name was the iron in the rock, a rust-red seam along the
north shoulder that bled down into the scree. No fort. No wall. Only
the country, narrowed by its own geology, insisting on a line of
passage.

They climbed into it through the morning.

The ground changed as they climbed. The coarse tussock of yesterday's
upland gave way to shorter grass with more stone in it — heather-ends
brown as the year turned, bedrock breaking the turf in wide bare
patches, the horizon coming closer on either side and further away
in front. Sheep thinned. A single hawk rode the updraft at the edge
of the north shoulder and did not cry.

He had been trained to note the change in country the way a geographer
noted it, because a geographer was one of the things he had been
trained to be among the others, and he found, after a while, that he
was also noting it in another register — the way stone *carried,*
the way Davan had named it last night at the outcrop without naming
it. He did not have Davan's instrument. His instrument was older and
clumsier and had been trained on a different sort of ground. But it
was not, he thought, a different question.

---

Midmorning they stopped at a bend where a small spring came up
through mossed stone and ran across the verge. Vane drank first,
cupped-hand, back to the road. The bay drank like a horse that had
been thinking about it for an hour. Edvar loosed the cart-harness
and rolled his shoulder.

Aldric dismounted. His knees were stiff. He had spent too many
hours at a lectern and not enough on a horse, and a man found out
what his body had been doing for thirty years when he asked it to
do something else for four days running.

Maren came up to the spring with her water-skin. She filled it
carefully, hand inside the mouth to keep the canvas from taking in
moss. When she stood, the disc under her coat pulsed — he could not
see the pulse, of course, but he saw its consequence: her free hand
went briefly inside the coat at the inner pocket, and her breath went
out in the short practical breath of a person registering a weather
and moving past it. She saw him watching. She did not pretend she
had not.

"Three this morning," she said. Quietly. Not to him, exactly —
pitched at the spring, for anyone at the spring who needed it. "Day
started at one. That was the third."

"More than yesterday."

"More than yesterday."

She capped the skin.

"The warmth?" he said.

"The warmth keeps up."

He nodded. He had no answer to give her — he did not yet know the
question well enough to answer it — but the exchange was its own kind
of work, and he did not want her to think her report had reached a
room without a receiver. He was the receiver the institution had
trained up. He would receive.

Vane went further up the road and stood at a knuckle of rock thirty
paces on and looked east under her hand. Her back was to them. Her
shoulders were set low, the way shoulders set when a body was
carrying a thing and deciding to carry it quietly. He did not look
at her longer than a stranger at a spring would.

Davan, at the cart-step, offered a piece of dried apple. Aldric
took it.

"Thank you."

"The bay work out the stiffness?"

"She does. I don't, at quite the same rate."

Davan's response was a small motion of the head that did not commit
to being anything. The apple was sharp and thin and very good.

The disc, under Maren's coat across the cart, did whatever it did.
Aldric was not close enough for anything to reach him. Davan, beside
him, was close enough; and Davan's skin at the wrist took on a shade
of the green and let it go again, like a word said once under the
breath and not repeated. Aldric did not ask. He had learned to
accept the half-answers of a man working a long question. He gave
the apple-stem to the bay, who took it with the dignity of an old
horse accepting a sweet.

They moved on.

---

By early afternoon the shoulders of rock had closed in.

The road went along the north side of the gap, along a ledge cut by
carters over generations and deepened by rain until it was less a
road than a long trough of stone. The south shoulder stood across
the gap at half a mile's distance, and the distance did not vary
for an hour — the country had found its shape and committed to it.
A stream ran at the bottom of the gap, small and loud, and the
sound kept up under the cart-wheels the way a low note kept up
under a hymn.

Aldric rode at Edvar's off-wheel. He had spoken perhaps five
sentences since the spring. He had been thinking instead — the way
he had been trained to think at a lectern, long architected clauses,
the scholarly way — and he had been thinking it on top of another
thing the scholarly way did not quite have a handle on.

The second thing was this:

The temple at the Confluence had been built over a piece of the
lander.

He had been carrying this since the lamp-circle last night, since
Davan had finished his sentence for him with the proper courtesy of
a man who had been finishing his own unfinished sentences for a long
time. *The temple is built on a piece of it.* *Above a piece of it.
But yes.* He had said *but yes* because the preposition mattered.
The temple was not *on* it the way a building was *on* a foundation.
The temple was *above* it. Placed to be over.

The distinction had been scholarly when he made it.

Riding east in the iron gap, under a sky that had got larger, it was
not scholarly any more.

He had spent thirty years of careful honesty inside an institution
that had been built, stone on stone, above a fragment of the thing
he was now riding east to find. Every morning of those thirty years
he had gone down to the chapel at Lauds and walked an aisle of
floor-stones laid above undercroft-stones laid above whatever else
lay below the undercroft — and the undercroft, he now knew, was not
the bottom. The chamber he had opened two months ago was not the
*foundation* of the temple. It was a place cut into the side of a
buried thing, and the temple had been put over it. His predecessors,
in some century he had not located yet, had understood well enough
what they were doing to do it deliberately.

They had not hidden the face in the temple.

They had built the temple to hide the face.

He let the sentence arrive. It arrived slowly, with a settling, the
way a vault-stone settled into a vault. He held the reins and let
the bay take her pace and let the sentence stand.

His hands on the reins did not fold.

---

They stopped where the north shoulder came down in a tumble and gave
the road a kind of shelter. A dry-stone fold older than yesterday's,
three walls and most of a fourth. Vane had walked the shelter before
they committed to it. She came back with a stone in her hand and
turned it over once before setting it down on another stone, and the
turning was all the approval anyone was going to get.

Edvar unhitched. The horses cropped the short turf. Maren laid a
fire small enough to hide in the lee of the wall. Davan filled the
kettle. Aldric unsaddled the bay and tethered her at the leeward
wall and rubbed her down with a folded sack.

They were an hour past the eastern edge of Irongate country now,
camped on heath that ran russet and wide around them, broken bones
of other folds scattered at intervals across it. The road behind
climbed back toward the gap they had come through. Ahead it went
on east toward a darker line in the far distance — a further ridge,
or the beginning of the coast weather a day ahead.

Vane stood at the mouth of the fold for a long time looking back.
Not forward. Back. West, the way they had come. He knew what she
was looking at. He did not ask.

---

Davan opened the trunk at dusk.

Edvar produced the key from the cord and passed it to him, and
Davan unlatched the trunk and lifted out the oiled canvas and laid
it on the blanket in the lee of the cart-wheel. Aldric had set a
lamp on the stone of the fold wall at the height that would let the
light come straight down across the canvas without glare.

The face came out of its wrap.

The undercroft had been the first seeing — his own chamber, his own
hand, alone, the candle going down. Last night at the ash-lee had
been the second, Davan's hand, Davan's light. This was the third:
a fold in Irongate country, a lamp in a wall-niche, four people
around a blanket and no stranger at the gate.

Maren sat cross-legged at the head of the blanket. Edvar took the
cart-step. Vane was at the mouth of the fold with her back to them
and her eyes east. Davan knelt at the blanket's edge and did not
put his hand on the face yet.

He looked at Aldric.

"You," Davan said.

Aldric did not answer. He looked at his hand on his knee. The hand
had not folded at the waist when he sat down; he had caught the
training in time; he had put his hands flat on his thighs as he had
learned to put them flat. The hand that Davan was offering the first
touch to — his right, the one that had reached a thousand times for
a wick that was not there — was not trembling. It did not reach. It
sat where he had put it.

"Together," Aldric said, after a moment. "If you will."

Davan nodded.

They laid their palms against the surface at the same time.

The face lit.

Pale. Cold. Familiar to Aldric in a way he had been trying not to
feel, because the familiarity was not nostalgia — it was *return,*
and he had not wanted to begin this work from a register that could
be made sentimental. He breathed through the familiarity. The image
assembled.

The blue-green world, first. Then the burning point at centre — not
the sun, he corrected himself even as the old word arrived, *the
star of this system,* because the old word meant a different set of
things than the accurate word and he was past a point in his life
where the old set could be allowed to do the work of the new — the
star. Then the arrangement.

He counted.

One. Two. Three.

Four.

The same count. The same arrangement in the near three. The same
drift in the outer one. It had been the inner three of his undercroft
count and it was the inner three of this face's count, and the
precision of the matching was one of the small cold mercies Aldric
had been granted in the past two months — that he was not mistaken,
that the data were the data, that somewhere above or between them
there was a system that had been keeping a faithful record and was,
through these small dark surfaces, still keeping it.

The fourth shape rendered in the lamp-light.

It was smaller than his had been.

Not by much. A hand-breadth's worth of smaller, if one were looking
at the two faces side by side. The shape was roughly the same — a
rounded irregular body, not circular, with an extension on one limb
that resolved when Aldric leaned closer as a — a *jag*; an angle of
broken profile; the asymmetry of a thing whose outline was not the
outline a maker would have chosen for it.

"Smaller," Davan said, quietly, his voice very level. "Further.
Distant. As I saw it last night."

"Yes."

Aldric did not lift his hand from the surface. He looked at Davan.
Davan was looking at the display.

"Mine," Aldric said, "showed it larger. Closer. Or — " he stopped,
because the word *closer* had arrived before the accurate word, and
he wanted the accurate word. "Not closer. The same. As if from a
nearer position. The same object. Nearer. And — " the liturgical
word was trying to come up to meet the scientific one, and he let
them come together this time; he was tired of keeping them in
different rooms " — and broken. Damaged. The jag, larger. The
asymmetry legible. From my face it looked as if something had
happened to it recently. From yours it looks like a point at the
limit of what the instrument can still see."

Davan looked up.

"Same object," Davan said.

"Same object."

"Different where the face is."

"Yes."

---

They did not speak for a long moment.

The lamp did its small work in the wall-niche. The kettle, at the
other fire in the lee of the wall, had gone off the boil and was
ticking down. Edvar had not moved on the cart-step. Maren had not
moved on the blanket. Vane, at the fold's mouth, had turned her head
a fraction — not to look but to hear better — and had turned it
back.

Aldric understood the shape of the silence.

It was, he thought, the silence of two scholars who had found the
same volume in two different libraries and had laid the copies side
by side for the first time and had discovered that the annotations
differed in the way annotations differed when the marginal hand had
been writing from different rooms at different times. Not a
contradiction. Not an error. A *position.* The two faces had been in
two positions when they had recorded, and the positions had produced
two accounts, and the two accounts together — this was the piece
that was landing in him with the slow settling of the vault-stone
again — the two accounts together were more information than either
alone, because the difference between them was itself data.

The faces had not been the same face.

They had been two instruments recording the same event from two
vantages. The vantages had been different because the *instruments*
had been in different places when they recorded, and the instruments
had been in different places because the debris field was wider than
either face could, on its own, see.

"The debris field," he said, aloud, because the word had to come out
for the sentence to become the sentence. "The debris field is not
the thing we've been looking at. The debris field is wider than
either of these surfaces alone could tell us."

Davan said: "Yes."

"A face at the Confluence. A face in a collapsed building in your
mapmaker's part of the city. Corvel's container in the marshland
south of the ridge. Reth's formation in the highlands ahead of us.
Brennan's metallic signatures under the Confluence itself. None of
these is the same piece. They are all — "

"Pieces," Davan said.

"Pieces."

Aldric lifted his hand from the face. The image held a beat after his
hand lifted — these surfaces did that, he had learned, they held an
activation the way an afterimage held in an eye — and then it began
to fade, slowly, the blue-green world going first, the star going
last, the four shapes all dimming together. Davan did not lift his
hand. He watched the fade. When the face had gone dark he lifted his
hand too and set it, palm down, on his thigh.

Maren, at the head of the blanket, spoke for the first time since
they had laid the face down.

"How wide," she said.

Aldric looked at her. She was not asking him specifically. She was
asking the air above the blanket, and the air was, at this moment, a
room the four of them were in together. But the question was his to
answer more than the others', and he answered it.

"I don't know."

"Guess."

He considered. He considered Brennan's scatter under the temple —
small signatures, a scatter, the sort of thing that resolved under a
surveyor's instruments as *debris*, not as *formation.* He considered
Reth's formation in the highlands — four hundred meters, a continuous
manufactured surface. He considered the marsh. He considered Edvar's
face in a collapsed building that had once been a house in the old
quarter of a port city that was, on the oldest maps, not there at all.

"At least four separate sites. That is not a guess. That is four
confirmed. The field is the distance between the furthest two sites
we know about. Confluence to the highlands. That is — sixty miles.
A little more."

"Sixty miles," Maren said.

"The places I can confirm. There will be others."

"How many others."

"I don't know."

She nodded, once, and did not push. She was a person, he had come
to recognize, who understood when a question had reached the edge of
its answer and did not pretend by asking again that the edge could
be moved.

The lamp did its work. The canvas, dark, lay on the blanket. The
face, under the canvas, was dark under it, the way a hearth-stone
was dark under a wrapped cloth.

Edvar shifted on the cart-step. Vane, at the mouth of the fold, took
a breath in and let it out and did not turn.

Davan wrapped the face in the canvas, slowly, with the care Aldric
had seen Edvar use last night with a candle in a draught. Edvar
lowered it into the trunk. The trunk closed. The key went back on
the cord into Edvar's shirt.

---

Aldric sat a long time at the fire after the trunk was in the cart.

Maren had gone to the mouth of the fold to speak quietly with Vane,
the wind and the stream for privacy. Edvar was asleep under the
cart-step. Davan was at his blanket with a piece of wood and a knife
and had not begun to carve yet. The lamp in the wall-niche was out.
A smaller lamp sat on a stone at the fire's edge, the size of a
man's fist.

Aldric looked at it.

He had tended lamps for thirty years. Chapel lamps, sanctuary lamps,
tallow dips at wayside-chapels on visitation years. His thumb and
forefinger carried a dozen small scars where a wick had done what
wicks sometimes did. He looked at his hand. It was on his knee. It
was not, at this moment, anywhere near the lamp.

He did not reach.

He sat with the not-reaching. The hand did not do its thing. The
lamp did its small yellow work and burned its small length of wick.

He had spent thirty years as a man who managed other people's fires.
The instinct that had kept his hand at the wick of every lamp in
every chapel of a minor provincial order was the instinct of a man
who had loved small tended things and had thought that the tending
of small things was what his hand had been given him for.

He had been wrong about what his hand had been given him for.

His hand had been given him for the work that was in front of it
now, which was not the tending of small lamps. It was the *opening*
of what the institution he had served for thirty years had been
built expressly to keep closed. The temple had been put over the
face to keep the face under a floor. The next Keeper, if he was
going to be honest, was going to be the one who took the floor up.

He did not yet know how a man took up a floor he had sworn to walk
on. The *how* was tomorrow's, or the week's, or the year's. The
*that* was this hour's, and this hour had given it to him, and he
accepted it.

He looked at the small lamp a last time. The wick did not need
trimming. The wick was doing its work well enough. The wick was
not his wick.

He leaned down and blew.

The flame went. The cold came in at the space where the flame had
been. The hand he had not used to put it out stayed on his knee,
open, palm up, the pads of the thumb and forefinger resting on air.
