

# What the Moons Keep

*Prologue: What Was Remembered*

Being Several Accounts of the First Coming,

as Collected, Contradicted, and Incompletely Preserved

*The following fragments were gathered from temple archives, traveling singers, condemned libraries, and the mouths of the very old. They do not agree with each other. They have never agreed with each other. This has not stopped anyone from dying over them.*

I.  FROM THE RECITATION OF FIRST LIGHT — GRAND TEMPLE OF THE CHRYSALIS, NEW PLYMOUTH

In the age before the Awakening there was only the hollow dark, and in the hollow dark there was nothing that breathed and nothing that changed and nothing that knew its own name.

And the Three Witnesses moved through the dark, and they saw the world below, and they said: here. Here is where it begins.

And the First Light came down from the Witnesses and touched the soil of Gaius, and where it touched, the Chrysalis waited. It had always waited. It was patient beyond the understanding of any living thing, because it was older than living things, older than the names for old.

The Founders arrived in the time of First Light. They were not yet themselves. They were seeds.

The Chrysalis entered them. The weak ones burned. The strong ones opened. The ones who opened became the first true people of Gaius, changed and awake and named by what they now carried inside them.

A small number would not open. These were not destroyed, for the Chrysalis is merciful. They were set apart, that they might perform the work that hands-which-change cannot perform. They tend the old things. They carry the old burdens. They serve.

The Three Witnesses remain above us still. They do not leave. They have never left. They watch our Bloomings and our dyings and our wars and they do not intervene, because they gave us everything we needed in the First Light and the rest is ours to do with.

This is the truth. It has always been the truth. To say otherwise is to invite the hollow dark back in.

*— Recited at every Blooming ceremony. First written down, approximately, seven hundred years ago.   Original oral composition: unknown.*

II.  AS TOLD BY NANA BRINE OF THE GREYWOOD VALLEY — TRANSCRIBED BY HER GRANDDAUGHTER, WILLA

She always started the same way. She'd bank the fire down low and say: the people didn't come from here. That's the important part that everyone forgets. The people came from somewhere else. She'd never say where. I asked her once and she said she didn't know, and nobody knew, and that not-knowing was a very old hole in the world that everyone had learned to step around.

She said they came in something. She couldn't say what. Something big. Something that moved through the dark the way fish move through water, she said, easy and purposeful and not concerned with the dark at all.

And then it stopped being easy. And then there was fire. And then there was Gaius.

She always made the landing sound like an accident. Not a punishment, not a miracle. An accident. I asked her if that made her sad, and she said: it made her think. Because accidents are no one's fault and everyone's problem, and there's something honest in that.

She said the people were very frightened. She said the world was not like wherever they'd come from. She said the ground itself got into them, the air got into them, the water got into them, and they were never the same after and most of them didn't mind because they didn't remember being different.

The ones who did remember different were the ones who didn't change. She felt sorry for them. She said they were like people who remember a dream so clearly it makes the waking world look thin.

I asked her about the moons.

She was quiet for a while. Then she said: your great-grandmother's grandmother's grandmother — some number of grandmothers back, she couldn't count them — said the moons were waiting for something. Not watching. Waiting.

I asked what they were waiting for.

She banked the fire back up and said it was time for bed.

*— Willa Brine, written down "so I wouldn't forget it wrong." Undated.*

III.  THE IRONGATE RECOUNTING — WARRIOR-CULT ORAL TRADITION, EASTERN MARCHES

Three gods fell. That is how it started.

The first god struck the earth and died and its body became the eastern hill country. Its blood went into the rock and from the rock into the rivers and from the rivers into the people. That is the Chrysalis. A god's blood, cut with river water and time.

The second, third, and fourth gods did not fall. They would not fall. They circled the sky where the first had fallen and they circle it still, and sometimes when the light is right you can see they are angry. You can feel it in your blood when they are full-bright.

The people who drank the god's blood changed. Good. Change is the only honest answer to a hard world.

Some people did not change. They were not gods. They were the servants the first god brought with it to do the carrying and the keeping. They are still servants. There is no shame in this. Someone has to carry things.

In the old songs they are called the Unblooded. The songs are not cruel about this. The Unblooded were useful. They could touch the god's old bones and weapons without burning. They kept the old things working until the old things stopped working anyway.

Then there was nothing for them to keep. So they became something else. What, the songs don't say.

*— Compiled from multiple singer-recitations in the eastern marches.   Estimated oral tradition depth: two to three thousand years at minimum.*

⁂

IV.  THE TIDEMARK TELLING — FISHER COMMUNITIES, COASTAL TRADITION

My father's father's people say it plain: the first ones came on ships.

Not like our ships. Ships that made our ships look like a child's toy boat pushed across a puddle with one finger. Ships that held hundreds of people. Ships that moved through the dark between the lights in the sky, because the people then knew how to sail that dark the way we know how to sail the gray water.

Four ships. One of them came down. Not a good landing. A bad one. The kind where you give up the ship to save the people, and even then you don't save all the people.

The other three ships — they didn't leave. Think about that. Every sailor knows what a ship does when she loses one of her own at sea. You don't leave. You stay. You circle. You call their name into the water until the water answers or it doesn't.

That's what they did. They circled. They called.

The answer, if there was one, none of us have heard.

This is why my family, when we lose someone to the water, we don't look down. We look up. And when all three lights are full-bright together, you can feel it in your chest if you're honest, that old patient grief that doesn't belong to you personally but is yours anyway because it's been handed down so many times that it's worn itself into the shape of a thing you were born knowing.

*— Spoken by a Tidemark harbor-master in his cups. Written down by a temple record-keeper   who included the note: "This man has had too much to drink. Also: I cannot stop thinking about what he said."*

V.  A CHILDREN'S COUNTING RHYME — WIDESPREAD, ORIGIN UNKNOWN

*Four fires in the sky,*

*one came down to die,*

*three stayed up to cry,*

*now you're born and so am I.*

*Skin like bark and eyes like glass,*

*roots go deep and years go fast,*

*but the ones who didn't change*

*walk the old halls, strange and strange.*

*Don't touch the dark, don't touch the door,*

*don't go asking what it's for,*

*some things hum and some things burn*

*and the rest of it you'll never learn.*

*— Used in skipping games. Regional variants recorded across Thera with minor differences.   Considered by the Temple to be harmless folk tradition.*

VI.  FRAGMENT FROM A CONDEMNED TEXT — AUTHOR UNKNOWN, TITLE UNKNOWN, DATE UNKNOWN   RECOVERED FROM A PRIVATE COLLECTION, BINDING FIRE-DAMAGED

The honest version, as best I can reconstruct it from what the stories agree on when you strip out the parts that feel like someone's wish:

People arrived. From somewhere not here. They arrived badly. Something was lost. A great deal was lost. The world affected them in ways they had no words for, and by the time they had words for it the words were already the wrong shape for what had actually happened.

The ones who were not affected by the world had an advantage: they could still use the things they'd brought with them. Tools. Objects. Things that required hands that had not been changed by the world to operate.

This made them valuable. For a while. Then it made them necessary. Then the things they could operate began to fail, one by one, over centuries, and their necessity became only the habit of having been necessary, which is a different thing entirely but looks the same from below.

I have watched a vine grow over a wall until the wall cannot stand without the vine. Pull the vine away and the wall falls. This does not make the vine the wall. It makes the vine something that arrived after the wall was built and found a use for the cracks. The unchanged found their use. The use became a claim. The claim became a bloodline. And no one corrected the record, because by then the record and the claim were the same document, kept in the same archive, guarded by the same hands.

The moons watch. They have always watched. I do not know what they see. I suspect what they see is people who have forgotten what they themselves were, telling other people confident and beautiful lies about it.

I do not think this is malicious. I think it is just what happens to a truth when it is left alone in the dark for long enough. It begins to glow. And everyone mistakes the glow for the original thing.

*— Remainder of text missing. Temple notation on the cover reads:   "Examined, condemned, destruction ordered." Below this, in different ink: "Why is this so hard to burn?"*

VII.  FROM THE PRIVATE DEVOTIONAL OF THE ROYAL HOUSE — NOT FOR DISTRIBUTION   RECITED AT SUCCESSION RITES. TEXT HELD ONLY WITHIN THE HOUSEHOLD.

We are the children of the Keeper. The first Keeper. She who held the keys to the fire and refused to let it die.

Before the Chrysalis. Before the people who changed. Before all of it. She was here, and she kept the fire, and the fire kept the people, and so we are here.

We do not change because we are the changers. We hold the old things because we are old things. We do not ask the question of what we came from because we carry what we came from in the metal and the light and the hum of the old weapon, which hums because it still remembers what it was made for and will not forget.

The changed ones are our people. We love them as you love anything that grows under your hand. We guide them. We protect them. We do not tell them everything, because a keeper does not give away the fire all at once. You give it in amounts that can be used.

The moons are the moons. They are ours. They are not for anyone else.

The hollow ones who are born outside our line are mistakes of nature, frightening to themselves, to be found and brought in or else handled. The Keeper does not leave the keys where anyone can pick them up. You tend the fire or you smother it, and there is no third option, and we have not survived ten thousand years by pretending otherwise.

This is not cruelty. This is tending. We have always tended.

Remember the first Keeper. Remember what she gave up. Remember what we carry for everyone who cannot carry it themselves, and be grateful for the weight, because the weight means you are still standing.

*— Passed down orally for most of its history. First written transcription within living memory.   Access restricted to blood members of the royal household.*

VIII.  UNDATED, UNSIGNED, FOUND SCRATCHED INTO THE BACK OF A WAYPOINT SHRINE EAST OF NEW PLYMOUTH

The moons are not moons.

Ask your blood what it remembers.

Then go east.

There is a hill shaped wrong.

## Chapter One — *[Maren]*
### The Ritual of the Second Skin

---

The herb woman's kitchen smelled of drying things — fennel and coltsfoot and the sharp ghost of whatever she'd pressed the week before, a scent Maren could never name but knew the way she knew her own hands. She was grinding dried bark into a paste that was supposed to help with joint-ache. She had been doing this since early morning and her arm was tired and she was thinking about nothing in particular, which was a skill she'd worked on for three years and was finally getting good at.

"You're too hard on it," Delia said, without looking up from the linen she was hemming. "The paste wants to be coaxed. Not beaten."

"I'm coaxing aggressively."

"That's just beating."

Maren eased off. Outside the window the valley was doing what it always did in early autumn — going gold-gray, the high grasses bending east under a wind that had traveled a long way to be cold by the time it arrived. Ashwick sat in a shallow bowl of plateau country, basalt ridges on three sides, the fourth open to a sky so wide it sometimes made Maren feel like the ground was tilting. She had grown up thinking this was normal. She still thought it was normal. It was just that she'd also learned to not look at the sky for too long on certain days.

Today was one of those days.

The Autumnal Rite was at sundown.

She put the mortar down and flexed her grinding hand and looked at the bandage on her left forearm. White linen, neatly wound, changed every third day because the deception had to be complete to be convincing and a clean bandage was one less question. Beneath the linen was not a wound. The wound was a story she'd told three years ago — a burning accident, a healer's visit, a scar that hadn't settled right, still sensitive to cold. People were incurious about other people's pain. She'd been counting on that and it had held.

Beneath the bandage, tonight, she would need to place the coal pouch.

She'd made it herself the first year. A small leather bag, flat, no bigger than her palm, filled with charcoal wrapped in wool-cloth so it held heat without smelling of smoke. She wore it close to the skin during the Rite, inside the bandage, against the forearm. If anyone touched her arm they felt warmth. If anyone looked at her face when the candles were lit and the changes were on display around her, they saw someone who was concentrating, which could mean anything.

It worked because nobody really looked. That was the first thing she'd understood: people at the Rite were looking inward, at their own changes, at the thing inside them that had opened when they were fourteen or fifteen and had been opening further every year since. They were not looking at a seventeen-year-old girl who smelled of fennel and kept her arm close to her side.

The second thing she'd understood: the Rite wasn't about proving anything. It was about belonging to the proving together. A hundred people in a field, feeling what they felt, and the particular comfort of not feeling it alone. She was never going to have that. She had the next best thing, which was knowing how to stand in the shape of it.

"Deacon's arriving at midday," Delia said. "From New Plymouth. He'll want feeding."

"I know. I put the salt-fish on last night."

Delia looked at her then — that particular look she'd been perfecting for three years alongside Maren's deception, the look that said *I am not going to say what I'm thinking about*. It was a warm look. It had a lot of weight underneath it. Maren had learned to receive it without flinching.

"He'll be stricter than Brother Aldous," Delia said. "They always are, when they come from the city."

"I'll be fine."

"I know you will." Another version of the look. "I'm just saying."

Maren went back to her grinding. She coaxed aggressively. The paste began to smell like something that might actually help a person's joints.

---

The deacon's name was Ferris. He was perhaps thirty-five, lean in the way of a man who traveled a great deal and ate when it was convenient rather than when he was hungry. He wore the Chrysalis mark tattooed on the backs of both hands — the spiraling symbol that priests got when they took their formal vows, meant to show the change-within-change, the Awakening that chose the devoted as surely as it chose everyone else. His own change, whatever it was, didn't show on the surface. A lot of the serious ones were like that. The mutations that went inward were supposed to be the deepest.

He ate the salt-fish without comment and asked about the village's yield and whether there'd been any trouble on the eastern road. He was pleasant. Maren watched him the way she watched everything she wasn't sure about, which was from slightly to the side, attention present and apparent interest withheld.

He asked about her arm over the meal.

Not aggressively. Conversationally. He asked what had happened and she told the burning story and he nodded and said he hoped it had healed well and moved on. She ate another bite of bread. Under the table she pressed her thumbnail into the pad of her finger until the small pain steadied her.

Later she overheard him speaking with Elder Torvann in the yard, and she heard him say, in the tone of a man making an administrative observation: "The girl. The herb woman's girl. She was here for the last Rite?"

She couldn't hear Torvann's answer. She went back inside and finished the salt-fish and washed the plate and told herself that a man from New Plymouth would ask questions about everything as a matter of professional habit. She told herself this was not specific. She had been telling herself things were not specific for three years. She was very good at it. She did not quite believe it today.

---

They lit the fires at sundown. Eight fires in a ring on the flat ground east of the village, and between the fires the people of Ashwick stood in their best clothes with their changes on display, and the deacon moved among them with the ceremonial candle and spoke the words of the Rite in the old formal accent that the city temples had preserved and the country temples had let soften into something more like ordinary speech.

Maren stood in her place in the ring. Coal pouch warm against her forearm. Bandage crisp and clean.

To her left: Bram the blacksmith, whose skin had been going gradually to a dark burnished copper for four years now, the change moving up his arms toward his shoulders, beautiful in firelight. He was proud of it in a quiet way that Maren found genuinely admirable. He'd never asked for a smith's change. He'd gotten one anyway. He'd built his work around it instead of the other way.

To her right: Jory, who was fifteen and newly opened and still adjusting. His eyes had gone reflective — not unpleasant, silver at the edges in low light, but they unsettled him in mirrors and he kept looking at the ground. His mother had her hand on his shoulder. He was shaking slightly in the way of someone trying hard not to.

Maren had watched people open their whole life. She knew what it looked like from the outside: strange and sacred and a little frightening and then, after, ordinary. A girl at the Rite two years back had grown a second set of small fingers partway through the ceremony, which had caused a great stir and much weeping of joy. The fingers were useful now. She sewed with them.

It was real. Maren had never once doubted that it was real. She had doubted a great many other things. She had doubted the stories about why it happened and what it meant and whether the three moons were witnesses or something else entirely. The children's skipping rhyme came to her sometimes, uninvited — *four fires in the sky, one came down to die* — and the temple said it was harmless folk nonsense and the temple was probably right, but the rhyme had four in it where the Recitation had three, and she had never been able to make that arithmetic feel settled. She had her private doubts about Deacon Ferris. She did not doubt the changes, because she had seen them happen to people she knew and they had not been pretending.

She just was not among them.

She never had been.

The deacon's candle moved through the ring. Each person held their hands out or showed their change or simply stood in the warmth of what they carried, and the deacon touched the candle briefly to their joined hands and said the words and moved on. The ritual had a rhythm to it. Maren knew the rhythm. She settled into it the way she settled into any performance: spine straight, face composed, attention on the specific moment rather than the whole shape of what she was doing.

He reached Bram. The words. The candle.

He reached Jory. Longer here — a first Rite, the boy shaking — and something the deacon said made Jory look up from the ground, and his reflective eyes caught the firelight, and his mother made a soft sound.

He reached Maren.

She held out her hands, the bandaged left arm extended, the warmth of the coal pouch radiating up through the linen the way it always did. She met his eyes because looking away was the wrong thing to do. She had learned this young: when you are hiding something, you look at people. Looking away is the thing that looks like hiding.

He looked at her hands.

He looked at her face.

He looked at her hands again, and this time the quality of the looking was different. Not the general pastoral attention of a priest moving through a ceremony. Something precise. Something that had already decided to look and was now simply confirming.

He placed the ceremonial candle very close to her arm.

Not touching. Close. The flame guttered in the wind and threw a little heat and that heat, added to the coal pouch's warmth, was one thing too many — the pouch shifted when she didn't expect it, and the small weight of it moved wrong against her forearm, and she felt it before she saw it, the slight change in how the linen sat, and she knew before he did.

The pouch slid.

The linen caught on itself and the small leather bag dropped, visible for just a moment at the cuff of her sleeve, before she got her arm down and her opposite hand over it.

Just a moment. A breath.

The deacon looked at her cuff. Then at her face.

She looked back at him. She had nothing to offer him. Her face was doing whatever it was doing and she had no instructions for it, no prepared expression, because she had not prepared for this because she had been doing this for three years without incident and she had let herself stop preparing.

The silence lasted long enough to be a different kind of word.

Then he said the words of the Rite in the normal voice, touched the candle near her hands, and moved on to Bram's cousin on her left.

Maren stood in the ring until it was over. She sang the responses. She bowed her head at the correct moments. Her hands were steady. She was very cold.

---

She did not go to sleep.

She lay in her pallet in the room off Delia's kitchen and listened to the house. She heard Delia bank the fire. She heard the footsteps of the deacon's allotted guest-room, overhead, pacing. She heard the wind off the plateau pushing at the shutters the way it always did this time of year, testing them with an impersonal thoroughness, not malicious, just thorough.

She heard, an hour after the house had settled, the low sound of voices in the kitchen.

She moved to the wall. The old construction of Ashwick's buildings was good for this: the interior walls were wood panel over stone and sound moved through the gap between them if you pressed your ear to exactly the right spot. She had found the spot at thirteen, before the deception, when she'd been a child doing what children do with walls. She had used it many times since.

Delia's voice first. Low and careful, the voice she used when she was frightened but was managing the fear with her hands, doing something while she spoke. The clatter of a cup.

The deacon's voice in response. She caught fragments: "...not certain, but the weight on the arm, the way it..." and then something she couldn't hear, and then her own name.

Delia said something sharp. Maren had never heard her speak that way. It lasted only a moment before she modulated back to careful.

The deacon said: "...bloodless."

The wind pushed at the shutters.

He said, or she thought he said — the wall, the wind, she could not be fully certain: "The bloodline has asked to be told."

Delia said nothing for a moment. Then she said something very quiet that Maren couldn't make out at all.

Then Delia cried. Not loudly. The way she cried when she thought she was alone, which was muffled and controlled and private, the grief of someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible with their pain.

Maren sat against the wall for a long time after the voices stopped.

*Bloodless.* She had heard the word before, always in the context of a bad thing, a sad thing, said with the slightly lowered voice people used for the afflicted. She had always understood it meant what she was. She had not, until now, understood that someone was supposed to be told.

She thought about her father.

He had left on a night like this one, windy, late autumn. She had been twelve. She'd woken to find him gone and her mother sitting at the kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood and her face doing something Maren had not yet had a name for. She'd been told, after, that he'd left. That people sometimes did. Her mother had never said more than this, and when she died the following winter there was no one left to ask.

She had told herself for five years that he had abandoned them. She'd built a whole private architecture of bitterness around it, well-maintained, load-bearing. It had been useful. It had kept her from a different and harder question.

What she remembered of him was small and specific: lamp oil and the sharper bite of something metallic, and his hands by firelight, turning some small bright thing with the patience of a man for whom patience was a tool like any other. A gear, maybe, or a catch-pin — something that wanted coaxing into place, something that would either work or wouldn't and required a kind of faith in the trying. She could not remember what the bright thing was. She remembered the turning.

He had also been bloodless. She had always known this and had not let herself think about what it meant. He had also never shown the change. He had also, when she was young, been careful in ways she hadn't understood as careful at the time. The way he sat at the Rite. The way he held his arm.

He had left on a windy night. He had left without waking her.

She sat against the wall and she let the different question in.

---

She got up and began to pack. She did not take very long. Three years of knowing this might happen had made her efficient about it in the abstract, and she found that in practice her hands knew what her mind had planned.

The coin purse. The small knife Delia had given her for cutting herbs, which was good steel and balanced well. Two changes of clothes, rolled tight. The dried herbs she'd been collecting since summer in a leather wrap — things useful on a road, things she knew. Her mother's comb, which she had kept for reasons she'd never examined. The disc, which she'd carried in her coat pocket since her great-aunt pressed it into her hand at thirteen and said *don't lose this* and *don't show anyone* and nothing else.

She stood at the kitchen shelf for a moment. Delia kept her money in an old crock behind the salt-box. There was more there than Maren had in her purse. She thought about it. She picked up the coin purse and found, under it, the small worn pouch she used to carry the charcoal, emptied now, just leather. She put it in her pocket. It smelled like Delia's kitchen, like drying things, like fennel and coltsfoot and the sharp ghost of last week's pressing.

She left the crock where it was.

She went out through the back, through the kitchen garden and the low gate, onto the path that ran north along the ridge. Three moons were up — all of them, which happened sometimes, their combined light enough to make shadows. The plateau stretched out pale and enormous in that light, the basalt ridges black and jagged against a sky that went on and on.

She walked north.

She did not look at the moons for long. She had always been vaguely unsettled by them without knowing why — her grandmother's grandmother's people had said the moons were not watching but *waiting*, and Maren had never been told what they were waiting for, and the not-knowing had settled into her young enough to feel like instinct rather than information. She did not have time for vague things. She kept her eyes on the path and walked at the pace that could be maintained indefinitely, the long ground-eating step she'd developed doing Delia's herb rounds, and she thought about what she knew and what she was going to need to do with it.

She knew: the deacon had seen. She knew: the bloodline was to be told. She did not know what that meant, not precisely, except that Delia had cried in the way of someone who could not prevent a thing, and Delia was not a woman who cried at things that could be prevented.

She knew her father had been bloodless. She knew he had left quickly and without waking her. She knew he had never come back and no one had heard from him — five years filed under abandonment, now being refiled under something else. Something without a name yet, that sat in her chest like a coal going from warm to hot.

The path curved east at the top of the ridge. She kept north, off the path, through the high grass. The wind was in her face. The grass bent around her like it was trying to direct her somewhere.

I have been hollow my whole life and it has never mattered before. Something has decided it matters now.

She wanted to know why.

She walked. The moons moved in their slow courses. The plateau fell away behind her and the scrubland opened up, and somewhere ahead, past the basalt country and the long descent, was a road.


## Chapter Two — *[Davan]*
### The Color of Hunger

---

Three weeks on the road and Davan had stopped counting the blisters.

He'd made that decision somewhere around day four, when the count had reached a number that felt more like a complaint than information. The blisters were there. They would either harden into something useful or they wouldn't. Counting them every night in the firelight was just a way of feeling sorry for himself without technically doing it, and he'd left Amber Valley precisely because he was tired of that particular kind of dishonesty.

The road north was well-traveled enough to have a surface — packed earth between shallow drainage ditches, marked at intervals by waystones carved with distances to places he'd never been. He walked in the early morning and the late afternoon, rested in the worst of the midday heat, camped just off the road when he couldn't reach a waypoint inn. He'd done this before, shorter distances, helping his father deliver furniture to the river towns. The rhythm came back faster than expected. Wake, walk, eat when there was something to eat, sleep when it was dark. The body was a practical thing once you stopped arguing with it.

He was twenty years old and he had never been this far from home, and mostly he felt fine about this, and sometimes, for about an hour after waking, he felt it as an ache he couldn't quite locate.

He carved while he walked. Not intricate work — he saved that for camp, when the light was steady. On the road he kept his hands moving on a piece of soft pine, shaping it into whatever the grain suggested, which was usually something simple: a bird, a sleeping dog, the curve of a riverbank. His father had taught him that the knife was a conversation, not a command. You didn't force the wood. You asked it what it wanted to be and then you helped it get there. He was not sure this philosophy generalized beyond woodworking but he'd never quite been able to stop applying it.

The valley he'd left was three weeks behind him and already changing shape in memory. The water-rights dispute had started the previous spring — a drying-out year, the river lower than anyone could remember — and by summer it had stopped being a dispute and started being something else, something that pulled from both ends until it didn't matter anymore what it had been holding together. His family was there still. His mother, his father, his two younger sisters who were twelve and nine and already showing the first signs of opening, a warmth behind the eyes, a restlessness at night. He had hugged them and told them he'd write. He had written twice. He didn't know if the letters had arrived.

He was not running away. He had decided this and he mostly believed it.

He was just walking north.

---

The men were around the bend before he heard them.

He came off a rise in the road — a long uphill stretch through pine scrub that had him looking at his feet — and when he raised his head the road ahead had four people on it. Three of them were mounted. One stood holding the bridle of a farm cart while its driver answered questions from the man on the lead horse.

Davan stopped.

He'd stopped without deciding to, some part of him reading the situation before the rest caught up. The mounted men were not soldiers — no uniforms, no militia markings. Their gear was plain and well-kept, the horses better than anything a merchant would keep. The lead man was heavyset, broad through the shoulders, with a pale scar running from his left ear down to the jaw — not a fighting scar, something older, the kind you got young and forgot about. He wore no insignia that Davan could see, but there was something about the way he sat the horse, the way his shoulders squared when the farmer shook his head, that said he was accustomed to having his questions answered. To his right rode a younger man with close-cropped hair and the sharp, economical way of moving that spoke of someone trained to notice exits. The third rider hung back, keeping the road behind them clear. Not soldiers. Something more specific than soldiers.

The farmer answered. Shook his head. The lead man nodded once, the precise nod of someone who has received the answer he expected and is already thinking ahead, and they moved on up the road without looking back.

Davan stood in the road until they were past the next rise. Then he breathed.

He looked down at his chest. The shirt was dark — good, he'd chosen the dark shirt this morning without knowing why, and now he was grateful for the instinct. Even so he could see, just at the collar where the fabric gaped, a faint pulse of amber light against his own skin. Slow, rhythmic. It faded as he stood there, as his heart found its normal pace again.

He had not chosen to do that. The light came when it wanted, on its own logic, connected to something underneath thought that he was still learning to recognize before it happened. Fear, apparently, settled into him as warm amber. He let it settle the way most things about the change settled — slowly, without resistance, the way you make room for something you didn't invite but can't refuse.

He waited another few minutes, then walked on. The farmer's cart was still pulled over, the man sitting on his bench looking at nothing in particular with the expression of someone deciding whether he'd just been inconvenienced or threatened.

"What was that about?" Davan asked, keeping his voice even.

The farmer glanced at him. Took him in — young, walking, no obvious trouble about him. "Looking for someone. Young woman, dark hair, wouldn't show the change. Said she'd come north through the valley country." He shrugged. "Haven't seen any such."

"Neither have I," Davan said, which was true.

He walked on. The amber under his shirt had gone quiet, but he kept his collar closed.

The waypoint inn at Colter's Crossing was called The Doubled Moon, a strange name for an inn since there were three of them and they were not particularly notable for doubling anything. The sign was old, the paint cracked, the moon depicted somewhat egg-shaped. He ordered soup and bread and a small beer he couldn't quite afford and settled into a corner bench with his carving.

The room was half-full. Late afternoon, the light coming in low and yellow through the one window that wasn't shuttered. Two merchants at the bar talking with the easiness of people who knew each other, had been talking since before he arrived, and would be talking after he left. A family in the far corner, parents and three children, the children at the age where eating a meal was a full physical ordeal. An old man asleep near the fire with his hat over his face.

The soup was thin but hot. He ate slowly, making it last, and worked on the carving, a smooth abstract curve that had started as an attempt at a fish and was becoming something he didn't have a name for.

He wasn't trying to listen to the merchants. They were just the loudest thing in the room.

"—quiet word, not an official notice—" one was saying. He was a heavyset man with the prosperous look of someone who traded in things that kept well. Salt, maybe, or cloth.

"—still means someone's looking," the other said. Thin, with a narrow beard that was trying hard. "The bloodline doesn't put out a quiet word for nothing."

"Could be nothing. Girl went missing, family's worried, family's connected—"

"The bloodline," the thin man said again, as if the repetition made the point, and it did.

A pause. The sound of cups.

"Gone north, they said." The heavyset man lowered his voice, not very much. "No change showing. Could be she's just young, hadn't opened yet—"

"At seventeen? That's late, isn't it?"

"Late. Not impossible."

"Or," the thin man said, and didn't finish the sentence.

The heavyset man said nothing for a moment. Then: "There hasn't been one of those in—"

"Long time."

"Long time," the heavyset man agreed, and the conversation shifted to road conditions and whether the northern pass would close early this year.

Davan ate his soup. He turned the carving over in his hands. The word they hadn't said sat in the middle of the room like something that had been put down and not picked up.

*Bloodless.*

He had heard the word a handful of times in his life. Once from his grandmother, who had known someone, a very long time ago. Once from a traveling priest who'd made it sound like a kind of sorrow. Once in a children's song that the older kids sang at the younger ones, not cruelly, just as children carry things they don't understand, preserving the shape without the meaning.

What would it mean to have no change? He tried to genuinely imagine it: waking at fifteen with nothing different, watching everyone around you open into something new and finding yourself exactly as you'd been. He couldn't quite construct it from the inside. His change had come on slow, just a warmth at first, something he'd mistaken for a fever. He hadn't known what it was until his mother had sat him down and shown him, candlelight against his forearm, the way the light moved.

He'd cried. He still wasn't entirely sure why. Relief, maybe. Or something about the being chosen that he'd always found faintly uncomfortable to examine.

He bought a second beer he also couldn't quite afford and took it to the corner and thought about the Chrysalis the way he thought about it when he was alone, which was honestly.

The theology of it, the version he'd grown up with, was simple and warm: the world had recognized the people and the people had recognized the world, and out of that recognition came the change, and the change was the shape your truest self took when the world was watching. His community had celebrated Bloomings with food and song and a complete absence of solemnity. They had treated the change as a natural thing, remarkable without being holy. When old Veth had grown a second set of arms at forty-two, they'd had a party. When the Crane girl's eyes had gone to a compound shimmer that let her read in near-darkness, her mother had wept and called it practical.

He loved this about where he came from. He had not understood, until he'd been walking for three weeks, how much of his internal life had been shaped by it.

The church's version was more layered, more careful. The Chrysalis as divine gift, the change as awakening, the three moons as witnesses to each Blooming, as present and personal as a hand on the shoulder. He had always felt, listening to traveling priests, that the church version was the valley version with all the mess and joy extracted and replaced with something that required more maintenance.

This was possibly unfair. He knew it was possibly unfair.

The old story said underneath both versions: the change gave you what you needed to become yourself. He had heard this his whole life. He believed it had been true for Veth and for the Crane girl and for his mother, whose change was a sense of direction so precise she had never been lost, not once, which she claimed was not remarkable and which everyone who knew her found extraordinary.

He looked at his hands in the lamplight. Turned them over. The bioluminescence was quiet now, just the faint potential of it in his skin, like the difference between a coal that's out and a coal that's banked. When he was calm it was nearly invisible in daylight. In this kind of light, a close observer might notice something.

The thing was, he wasn't sure the change gave you what you needed. He thought it might give you what you already were, just louder. And those were different things.

He had always felt things in rooms. Not anything he could name precisely — more like a sense of the weight of the air, the way a space felt used or unused, whether the people in it were at ease. He'd thought this was just attentiveness, something anyone could do if they paid attention, until he'd started paying attention to whether anyone else was doing it and had found that they generally weren't. That they would describe a room as fine when the room did not feel fine to him at all, and he was usually eventually proven right about this.

The change had taken that unnamed thing and made it louder. The warmth in his skin that corresponded to emotion. The amber light for fear, something bluer for calm, a green-gold shimmer when he was happy that he'd seen once in a mirror and thought looked ridiculous and wonderful in equal measure. It was him, expressed as light. It was him, expressed as something anyone nearby could read if they were paying attention.

He was not sure how he felt about being legible.

He finished the beer. Outside, the light had dropped fully dark, and the three moons were coming up through the one unshuttered window, two bright and one dim, their combined light enough to silver the road. He watched them for a while. He had always liked the moons, their slow reliable movement, the way you could track a month by them if you bothered to learn. His grandfather had taught him the names of the old moon-songs, counting rhythms built around the cycles, older than any temple.

The merchants were still talking, quieter now, or he'd stopped listening. The family in the corner had gotten the children to sleep somehow, all three of them piled together like a litter of dogs. The old man by the fire had begun to snore with great enthusiasm and total unconcern.

Davan gathered his carving and his coat and went out to the stable, where he'd paid a half-coin to sleep in the hay. The stable was warm and smelled of horse and he lay on his back in the dark and looked at the ceiling and thought about the girl they were looking for, dark-haired, northbound, no change visible.

He hoped she was walking fast.

---

In the morning he was on the road before sunrise, the cold blue hour when the ground gives up the last of the previous day's warmth and the sky comes in close. He walked north. The blisters were still there but he'd stopped counting them.

At some point, between one step and the next, he realized he'd been holding the carving for the last hour without working on it. He looked at it.

The abstract curve had become a figure. Small, rough, the proportions not quite right in the way of something made by hands moving faster than intention — but recognizably a person, standing, head turned slightly to one side in the listening posture his carvings sometimes arrived at without being asked.

The arm was wrong.

He'd been working the left arm for the last stretch without realizing it, trying to get it to hang naturally, and it wouldn't. It kept wanting to be held close to the body — tucked in, guarded, pressed against the ribs as though protecting something or hiding it. He'd tried three times to open the posture, to let the arm fall to the figure's side the way arms did, and each time his hands had brought it back. Not stiff, not injured — held. Deliberately, carefully held, the way you carry something you don't want anyone to see.

He hadn't decided to do that. He hadn't decided any of it — the grain had led him there, or his hands had, the long night's worry working its way down through his arms into the pine. He had not chosen this. The wood had arrived at it before he had any say in the matter, and now it sat in his palm and he had to reckon with it — a figure guarding an arm for reasons his hands understood and his mind didn't.

He pocketed it and put his hands in his coat and kept walking. The road stretched out ahead of him, pale and long, and the moons were going down behind him on the left, and ahead the sky was starting, just starting, to show the color that came before light.

The man on the horse had said: dark hair, no change visible. Had said it the way you say a thing that matters but you're not supposed to say why.

He thought about the word the merchants hadn't finished.

What would it mean to walk through the world with nothing showing. No light when you were afraid. No tell. Just a face, ordinary, offering nothing.

That might be the loneliest thing he could imagine.

He walked. The road curved north and the light came up, and the high country ahead was dark green under the new sky, and somewhere past it was the city where he was going, for reasons he had not quite finished explaining to himself.


## Chapter Three — *[Aldric]*
### What Lies Under the Stone

---

The pilgrims came in all conditions, and Aldric had made his peace with this long ago.

He had made his peace with a great many things long ago, which he understood was either a mark of spiritual maturity or a kind of accumulated resignation, and which he privately suspected was both, and did not examine this suspicion too closely. He had made his peace with the cold that came up through the temple floor each winter regardless of how many braziers they lit. He had made his peace with the particular smell of large numbers of unwashed people in an enclosed space seeking comfort, which was the smell of the world's need, and which he took as a reminder that the work was real. He had made his peace with the administrative demands of running a major waypoint temple — the accounts, the correspondence, the management of junior priests who were either too eager or not eager enough — accepting them as the unglamorous armature on which the actual work was built.

He had made his peace, over thirty years, with the specific loneliness of a celibate life, with the knowledge that his name would not be carried forward, with the dreams he'd had in his twenties of doing something historically significant, which had faded without drama into the ordinary satisfactions of a life of service. He was not unhappy. He was not unhappy in the way of a man who has stopped asking whether he is happy, which is a different thing, but it was not a painful difference. It was simply the texture of a life that had been lived mostly correctly, by his own accounting.

He had not made his peace with the dying woman's last words.

---

She had arrived two days before, in the way that the most profoundly changed sometimes arrived: in a cart, because walking was no longer practical, accompanied by a young grandson who had the look of someone managing a grief he hadn't yet been given permission to feel. Her name was Sella. She was somewhere past seventy and what she was past seventy of was difficult to say with certainty, because what remained of her was not precisely a woman of seventy. The Chrysalis had been working on her for decades. Her arms had gone beyond translucent — the skin had thinned to something closer to membrane, the bones beneath fully visible, blue-white and delicate as bird-hollow, the joints enlarged where new structures had been quietly building for years. Where the change reached her collarbone it had gone further still: a faint crystalline texture at the edges, the skin catching the lamp's light at angles that flesh had no business catching. Her face had retained its character — she was clearly, even now, the person she had been — but it sat in a body that had passed some threshold Aldric could not name, where the change was no longer happening to her but simply was her, as settled and irrevocable as bone.

This was not unusual. The most devoted, the most deeply changed, sometimes continued to transform beyond what any conventional theology could comfortably describe. The church spoke of it as the Final Chrysalis, the ultimate gift, the complete dissolution of the barrier between self and world. Aldric had held the hands of perhaps a dozen people in this state over his career and had found it, privately, more frightening than transcendent. He had never said this. It would not have been useful.

He had sat with Sella because she had asked for the Keeper of the temple specifically. Not a junior priest. Not a healer. She'd been lucid when they'd settled her, and her eyes, which had gone a uniform pale silver without pupil or iris, had tracked him when he entered the room with the particular attentiveness of someone who has decided that every remaining moment is worth paying attention to.

He had read the passages she requested. He had sat with her while her grandson slept in the chair by the wall. He had thought, as he sometimes thought in these rooms, about what it would mean to believe without remainder — to be so entirely certain of a thing that your body bent toward it, decade by decade, until the certainty was written in your bones.

He had thought this with respect and a certain wistful distance, as a man might admire a feat of athleticism he knows is beyond him.

She had been quiet for a long time when she took his wrist.

Her grip was still strong. He had not expected that. The translucent hand on his wrist, and then her eyes on him, silver and steady, and she said, with a clarity that did not belong to someone who had been unconscious an hour ago:

"The moons are not moons."

He had held very still.

"They are boats," she said. "Boats with the dead still inside."

He had said, gently, "Rest now, Sella."

She had looked at him for another moment. There was something in the look — not distress, not confusion. Something almost apologetic. As if she knew she was giving him a gift he hadn't asked for and wasn't sure what to do with.

Then she had closed her eyes, and by morning she was gone.

He had stood in the doorway of her room for a moment after the grandson was seen to. The room's lamp was low and guttering. He reached across and pinched it out between his fingers, and then stood there in the sudden dark and did not move for a little while.

---

He had dismissed it. This was the correct response, professionally speaking. People in their final hours said many things. The mind, moving toward its end, sometimes produced fragments of old stories, half-remembered songs, images that had accumulated in a life and were being released without particular order or intention. The Fathers' Boats tradition was old — pre-Chrysalis in origin, the church had long regarded it as colorful superstition from the early settlement days, the kind of story that arose in any community trying to explain how it came to be somewhere. *We came in boats.* Of course they had. Metaphorically. Everyone comes from somewhere.

He had dismissed it and gone to bed and lain awake until the false dawn watching the ceiling and thinking about what it would mean, specifically, if the moons were not moons.

He had slept for perhaps two hours.

Then the morning. The pilgrims' queue, long because it was always long at the Confluence, three rivers and four roads meeting in one valley making it the natural center of a very large region. He had heard confessions and granted blessings and settled two disputes about inheritance that had been brought to the temple in the apparently sincere belief that matters of property were appropriately resolved in a house of the Chrysalis, which was not true and which he had explained patiently and which he would explain again to the next three families who arrived with the same sincere misapprehension.

He passed through the side corridor on his way to the queue and pinched the wick of the lamp outside the record room between his thumb and forefinger — already cold, already out, as it had been since the previous night — and walked on without breaking stride. He did this every morning. He had done it so long he would have been unable to say, if asked, whether the lamp was generally lit or unlit when he checked it. The checking was not about the lamp. The tips of his first two fingers on his right hand carried a permanent pale callus from a lifetime of this habit, which he had never noticed, and which two successive physicians had noticed and declined to mention.

He had eaten lunch standing at his desk and written two letters that needed writing and reviewed the accounts for the eastern supply house. He had done all of this with the focused efficiency of a man who has learned to use work as a kind of weather, something you move through rather than experience.

In the afternoon he had gone down to the undercroft.

---

The east undercroft was the oldest part of the temple. The temple itself had been built in stages over several centuries, each generation adding to or reworking what came before, so that the current building was a palimpsest of architectural decisions made by people who were long dead and had not always agreed with each other. The oldest sections were in the east: low stone rooms beneath the main floor, used now for storage, for the keeping of records going back three hundred years, for casks of the communion wine that needed to sit in the cool dark for at least a year before they were fit for use.

He had been meaning to have the east undercroft re-surveyed for some time. Part of the rear wall had shown cracking that concerned him, and he had arranged for two of the younger brothers and a mason named Holst to assess it.

He had not expected them to find anything behind the wall.

He heard Holst's voice from the bottom of the steps, and the tone of it was wrong — not alarmed, exactly, but the voice of a man who has encountered something outside his professional scope and is not sure what the protocol is. Aldric went down.

The crack in the rear wall had opened into something. Not a crack: a seam. The wall at this section, it turned out, was not original stonework but a later addition, built against an older surface and mortared flush so that it looked continuous. Two hundred years of settling had pulled the mortar and the stones apart, and Holst's careful work with a crowbar had done the rest.

Behind it: a room. Low-ceilinged, dry, sealed.

Inside the room: five objects.

---

He sent Holst and the brothers away. He told them the room appeared to be a historical storage space, that he would review its contents and report to the appropriate archival authority, that they had done good work and should say nothing about this until he had had a chance to assess the historical significance of what they'd found. He said all of this in the calm, conclusive voice he had spent thirty years developing for situations that required a calm, conclusive voice, and they went.

Then he stood in the doorway and looked at the five objects for a long time.

The first four were containers. Each perhaps the length of his forearm, smooth, some kind of dull gray material that was neither stone nor metal nor any ceramic he'd encountered, and yet had qualities of all three. They were sealed. He did not try to open them. Perfectly smooth except for a narrow ridge running around the circumference of each, and a small recessed area on one end that might have been a fixture for something, a handle or a closure mechanism long since lost.

The fifth object was flat. It lay on a low stone shelf at the back of the room. Dark — not the dark of an unlit surface but something more specific, a darkness that felt deliberate, a surface that absorbed rather than reflected. Perhaps a hand's width thick, perhaps four times that across. The edges were sharp and regular in the way of something made rather than grown or found.

He stood in the doorway for a very long time.

Then he went back upstairs and completed the afternoon's administrative work, and ate a dinner he did not taste, and sat in the chapel for evening prayers with the collected brothers of the temple, and said the correct words, and went to his room.

He lay awake until midnight.

Then he got up, took his lamp, and went back down to the east undercroft.

At the bottom of the steps he stopped. He became aware, in the way you become aware of a thing you have already done, that his right hand was raised and his thumb and forefinger were an inch from the lamp's flame. He had been about to extinguish the only light he had, in a sealed underground room, out of pure habit. He lowered his hand. He stood still for a moment, aware of the gesture the way you are sometimes aware of a word you use every day and realize you have forgotten its original meaning. It had not always been a habit. He preferred the word *habit*. Then he went in.

---

The flat dark object responded to his touch.

Not immediately. He had reached out because he was the kind of man who, when confronted with a thing he did not understand, eventually had to touch it. He had expected nothing or cold smooth material. He had not expected what happened.

Light. From within the object, or from its surface, or from the relationship between them — he could not determine the source. A cold light, not warm like flame. It moved in the surface, or rather, the surface moved within the light, and what appeared was not a consistent illumination but a shifting series of images.

He stepped back. He stood very still.

The images were small and seemed to recede as if seen from a great distance. Moving points of light against a field of dark. Some of the points were fixed; some moved in slow arcs. He had been enough of a scholar in his youth to have spent time with astronomy texts, and something about the arrangement produced a recognition he could not quite close his hand around — a star map, perhaps, or something that functioned like one, though the specific relationship of the points to each other matched no chart he knew.

He stood and looked at it until the cold began to work through his feet and legs, which was perhaps an hour, and he understood almost nothing, and he could not make himself go back upstairs. The quality of his attention troubled him — not its intensity, which was natural enough given the circumstances, but its steadiness. His mind had settled into the shifting light the way a hand that hasn't held a pen in twenty years still knows the motion. Something in him had been built for this kind of looking, and he had spent a long time arranging his life so that there was nothing to look at.

He had been a priest for thirty years. He had a functional theology of mystery — he believed that the Chrysalis was vast and old and that human understanding of it was partial and would always be partial, and he had built a comfortable and honest intellectual life around this belief. He was not a man who expected to understand everything. He had made his peace with not understanding everything.

But there was a particular quality to the thing on the shelf that did not feel like mystery in that sense. It did not feel like the mystery of the deeply sacred, which was a kind of fullness, a sense of something enormous behind a veil. It felt like a different kind of mystery. The mystery of a question that has an answer, and the answer exists somewhere, and you simply do not have it yet.

He had not encountered that feeling in a very long time.

He went back upstairs. He sat at his desk. He reached toward the lamp — caught himself, withdrew his hand — and then sat with the lamp burning as it needed to burn, and wrote in his journal, which he had maintained for twenty years and which he wrote in a careful, oblique shorthand developed for the recording of thoughts not meant for other eyes. He wrote, obliquely, about the sealed room and the objects and the flat dark thing that made light. He wrote, less obliquely than he intended: *What is this and who put it here and how long has it been here and why did they put it here and what are the containers and what is this light.*

He crossed out the last sentence. He wrote it again. He left it.

Then he turned back a page, and found, below the entry he'd written the previous day — a routine accounting of the pilgrims, the services, the correspondence — the brief note he'd made about Sella's last words.

*The moons are not moons. They are boats. Boats with the dead still inside.*

He looked at this for a while.

Below it, in the deliberate shorthand: *Grief-speech. Delirium. Old folk tradition. Irrelevant.*

He looked at this also.

Then he closed the journal and sat in his chair and looked at the lamp, burning low, and thought about what it meant that the two things — the old woman's words and the light moving in the dark surface below — had connected themselves in his mind without his permission, like two hands reaching toward each other in the dark.

---

In the morning he sealed the room again as best he could. He moved a storage cask in front of the disturbed section of wall. He told Holst that the room had contained only some damaged record-casks from three centuries back, nothing of historical significance, nothing that required further attention. Holst accepted this without comment, because Holst was a mason and his professional investment in the contents of old rooms was limited.

Aldric went about his day.

He went about his day for approximately two hours before he sat down at his desk and pulled out the standard form for reporting an architectural discovery within a temple precinct. The form existed because discoveries happened — old rooms, buried foundations, sealed chambers from previous construction phases — and the protocol was clear: document, categorize, report to the regional office within thirty days, await archival assessment. He had used this form twice before, for a collapsed drain and for a cache of damaged vestment-cloth found during renovation of the south annex. Both times the process had been unremarkable. Document, categorize, report.

He wrote the date and the location and the description of the wall failure. He wrote *five objects recovered from sealed chamber, east undercroft, below current floor level.* He reached the field marked CATEGORY — the form offered seven options, ranging from *architectural remnant* to *liturgical artifact* to *material of unknown provenance requiring specialist assessment* — and he stopped.

He looked at the seven options for a long time.

None of them were wrong, exactly. *Material of unknown provenance* was technically accurate. But the form's seventh category fed into a specific institutional process — regional office, then the New Plymouth archive, then, for objects of sufficient interest, a formal assessment team dispatched to the site. The team would include church officials and, for material predating the current temple, a representative from the bloodline's historical commission. This was standard. He had known it was standard for thirty years and had never found it remarkable.

He found it remarkable now, in the way you find something remarkable when you look at it from a direction you haven't looked from before. The form was designed to move information upward through a system, and the system was designed to receive it, and he could not say, sitting at his desk with the pen in his hand, what the system would do with what he'd found. He could not say this because he did not know what he'd found.

He set the form aside. Not face-down — he was not hiding it from himself — but to the left of his desk, in the area he used for things requiring further thought. He would come back to it. He was a man who came back to things.

He did not come back to it. By the following week it had been covered by other papers, and by the week after that it had migrated to the bottom of a stack, where it would remain, blank below the third line, for the rest of his time at the Confluence.

He heard confessions. He settled a dispute about the boundary between two families' grazing rights that had been brought to the temple in the apparently sincere belief, et cetera. He ate lunch standing at his desk. He wrote the letters that needed writing.

In the afternoon he went, on the pretext of a pastoral visit to the market district, to the temple's small archive on the upper floor of the east wing. He had full access to this archive, as Keeper of the temple, which meant he also knew its gaps — the sections that had been removed, transferred, restricted over the years, and by whom.

He found the section on pre-Chrysalis settlement traditions. It was thin. It had always been thin, and he had always understood this as a reflection of how little had survived from that period rather than as a deliberate condition. He looked at it again with a different question in mind.

The Fathers' Boats tradition: two pages of notes from a previous Keeper, approximately ninety years ago, summarizing a folk-tradition collected from the river communities. People came from elsewhere, in vessels, in the old time before the Chrysalis. The vessels still watched from above. The translation of the term used in the oldest versions: *sky-boats.* The Keeper's assessment, written in the firm hand of someone disposing of a matter: *Colorful origin-myth. Pastoral. Not inconsistent with the Chrysalis narrative if understood metaphorically. No doctrinal concern.*

Below this, in a different hand, much later, a single line of annotation: *See also: Restricted Collection, NP Archive, shelf 7-eastern. Access: Bloodline approval required.*

Aldric read this several times.

In thirty years, the existence of a Restricted Collection in the New Plymouth archive was not news to him. Various texts had been restricted over the centuries for various reasons — heresy, sedition, material deemed destabilizing to public understanding of the Chrysalis. He had never had occasion to seek access, which required either bloodline approval or a direct petition through the High Keeper's office. He had never had a reason.

He wrote the shelf number in his journal. Then he wrote it on a separate small piece of paper, folded it, and put it in his coat pocket where he would feel it when his hand went in.

He went back to his duties.

That night, sitting in the chapel after evening prayers had ended and the other brothers had gone, he stayed. He sat in the dark with the lamp unlit and looked at the carved symbol above the doorway — the spiraling form that marked every Chrysalis temple, the sign of the change-within-change. He had looked at this symbol, or one like it, every day of his adult life. He knew it the way he knew his own face, which was to say he had long ago stopped seeing it.

He became aware, at some point, that his right thumb and forefinger were pressed together in his lap. The automatic reach, the pinch, the confirmation. Finding nothing. He had been sitting in a dark room with no flame burning and his hand had still gone looking for one to put out.

He uncurled his fingers and set them flat on his knee.

He saw it now.

The inner spiral of the symbol — the oldest part, the part that predated the standard Chrysalis iconography by some margin, which scholars assumed was absorbed from earlier regional traditions — was not quite a spiral. He had always read it as one. Up close, with fresh attention, it was a set of concentric arcs that described an orbit. Something circling something. Over and over, patient, elliptical, going nowhere and going there with great dedication.

He stood under the doorway for a long time, looking up.

Then he went to his room and sat at his desk and wrote, in his journal, in the oblique shorthand, the sentence he had been not-writing for two days:

*I do not know what I am looking at.*

And below it, after a long pause:

*I think I am going to have to find out.*

He slept better than he had in years. The decision, apparently, had been the thing keeping him awake.


## Chapter Four — *[Vane]*
### The Flavor of the Contract

---

She found him by his fear.

This was not unusual. Fear had a specific chemistry — something metallic and slightly sweet, like copper left in milk — and it intensified around the source the way heat intensified around a fire. You could walk toward it in a crowd with your eyes closed. She had done this, once, as a demonstration for a client who didn't believe her fees were reasonable. He had revised his position.

The man she was looking for was at the back of the tavern, in a booth with three other people who were not him, performing the very specific relaxation of someone who has decided that being visibly relaxed is the same as being unfindable. A common mistake. People in hiding generally made themselves smaller and quieter, which Vane had always found counterproductive. Small and quiet read as hiding. The performance of ease, poorly done, read as a kind of scream.

His name was Torben. He owed money to people who could afford her rates, which put the debt in a particular category. She'd had the file for nine days. She'd spent seven of them establishing his habits and two of them waiting for the right moment, which was this one: a busy tavern, three companions who were casual acquaintances rather than allies, exits she'd mapped on the first visit.

She sat down across from him. She put her hands flat on the table.

He looked at her and then at the hands and then at her face and then the fear-smell spiked sharply upward, almost sharp enough to taste.

"Torben," she said.

"I don't—"

"You do." She kept her voice at conversation level. There was no point in theater. "Your choices right now are: you come with me quietly and your friends have a normal evening, or you don't and we have a longer conversation." She paused. "I get paid either way."

He came with her quietly.

Outside, in the street, she handed him off to the client's man who'd been waiting at the corner and collected her fee in a sealed envelope without counting it on the street, because counting money on the street was how you ended up having a shorter career than you'd planned. She'd count it upstairs.

The docks district smelled of its usual compound — brine and tar and the particular sweetness of rotting wood that had been soaking in seawater for decades, underneath everything a warm animal smell from the livestock pens two streets over. She had lived here for six years. Her nose had mapped it so thoroughly that variations registered as information rather than sensation. A new ship in the harbor. Something dead in the alley off Cutter's Lane, probably a dog, two days ago. Rain coming, though the sky was still clear — something in the air pressure that expressed as a particular flatness to the smell of the street.

She went upstairs.

---

Her rooms were two: a front room with a table, a chair, a locked chest, a window that looked onto the street; a back room with a bed and a second locked chest and a lamp she kept turned low. The front room had the tools of the work: maps, contact lists written in a shorthand nobody else could read, a small collection of restraints and lockpicks in a roll of oilcloth. The back room had the things she didn't want to think about while she was working.

She counted the fee. It was correct.

She put the envelope in the locked chest and sat at the table for a while doing nothing in particular, able to sustain this for approximately ten minutes before the shape of the evening's quiet began to press in on her. Not loneliness. She had made her peace with solitude long ago, genuinely rather than nominally. Something else. A specific absence. The sensation of a thing she was about to address.

From the back room's chest she took the small tin.

The wafers inside were pale, slightly iridescent, thinner than a fingernail. They smelled of almost nothing — a faint warmth, something floral that wasn't quite any flower she knew. This was one of the qualities she had always found unsettling about Bloom, in the detached professional way she found many things unsettling: that the substance that rewrote the inside of your skull for four hours had almost no smell at all. Everything had a smell. The absence was its own kind of statement.

She placed one wafer on her tongue.

---

It began in her chest. It always began there, a warmth that moved outward, and then the warmth became something more specific — an opening, a sense of the walls becoming less definitive, the boundaries of her own skin turning generous. Her smell broadened. Not sharper: broader. The copper-milk of fear went quiet and underneath it the city opened up, layer on layer, every person in every room doing their human chemical work — the cocktail of comfort and worry and hunger and desire and boredom and grief that was the smell of a city at night. She could not separate them cleanly, not like this; it was more like hearing a choir than hearing individual voices. The city, all at once, in full throat.

And then the further thing. The thing the Bloom-devotees called communion and she had never called anything because naming it made it easier to need, and she had always tried to keep the needing at arm's length.

The sense of being continuous with all of it. Not dissolved — she was still herself, still Vane, still in a chair in a room above the docks — but connected at every edge to everything adjacent, and those things connected to everything adjacent to them, and so on outward until the distinction between what was her and what was the city and what was Gaius itself became a matter of technicality. For the duration of the Bloom, she understood without thinking about it why people called the Chrysalis a gift. It was the difference between looking at the world through a window and standing in it. She stood in it.

This lasted some portion of the night. She lost track of time in the usual way.

---

What she came back to was: the chair, the table, the low lamp, the window showing a sky that had moved from dark to a dark-gray that suggested approaching morning. The city quieter. The smell of it returned to information rather than song.

And the particular quality of the descent, never improved regardless of how many times she'd been through it: the sense of the walls returning, the boundaries re-establishing, the world stepping back behind the glass. For a few minutes she simply sat with this in the way she sat with most unpleasant necessary things, which was without drama.

Then the small inventory she always did after: the bark on her forearms, in the early years staying below the elbow and now at mid-bicep on the right side. She pressed her fingers against it. Cool, dense, the texture of old wood. It had spread another half-inch since the previous month, she thought, and then thought: you said that last month, and you were probably saying it the month before. She had learned not to measure things she was not prepared to act on.

She needed another wafer in three days. Probably two and a half.

She went to bed. She slept four hours, dreamlessly.

---

In the morning: a letter under the door.

She knew Horst's seal — a pressed coin rather than wax, clean and businesslike, the signature of a man who found theatrical gestures unnecessary. She'd worked four contracts through Horst over the past two years. He was organized, he paid correctly, he communicated clearly. She had no information about who he worked for and had not sought any, because that was the kind of information that created obligations she hadn't agreed to.

She read the letter at the table with a cup of something that approximated tea if you were not particular about what tea was supposed to taste like.

*Female. Young, approximately seventeen. Dark hair. No visible change. Last confirmed location: Sanctum Basin region, moving north. Do not harm — condition of contract. Deliver to the address below, Noble Quarter, New Plymouth. Standard discretion terms apply.*

Below this: a fee. She read the number twice.

She set the letter down. She picked up the cup and finished the approximate tea. She thought about the fee.

Not an unusual brief, except in two specific ways. The first: the Noble Quarter address. Not a guardhouse, not a constable's office. A private address. The second: the instruction regarding visible change, or the absence of it. Most wanted notices, when they described a person, mentioned change as a way of confirming identity — the copper skin, the reflective eyes, the height-stature someone had grown to after an Enhancement change, whatever was specific and visible and useful for identification. This notice described an absence of change as if the absence were the distinguishing feature.

She considered this for a moment and then filed it in the category of things that were the client's business rather than hers, which was the category that made the work manageable.

She wrote back to Horst: *Terms accepted. Advance as agreed. Will report when target located.*

Finding people was the one thing she had never needed to practice.

---

Cade's supply house was on the outer edge of the Crafting Quarter, in a building that appeared to sell fabric remnants and did, in the front half, do a modest legitimate trade in exactly that. The back half was reached through a door that looked like a storage cupboard and required a specific knock that Cade changed every few months with the somewhat paranoid regularity of a man who understood that his business model had structural vulnerabilities.

She went in the afternoon, because the morning traffic was too thin to be anonymous in.

Cade was a small precise man of indeterminate age who wore his gray hair long and kept his hands very clean. He ran the Bloom trade in the docks district with the methodical attention to logistics of someone who had understood early that the actual substance was the least interesting part of the operation, and that the work of supply chain and client management was where things were won or lost. She respected this about him while finding him personally unremarkable.

He had her usual supply behind the counter. He measured, he wrapped, he named a price that had not changed in the two years she'd been coming here.

She was paying when she heard the two men in the back talking.

Not clearly. The wall between the shop and the back room was thick. But her hearing was not, technically, enhanced — it was that her sense of smell had recalibrated her entire sensory processing in ways she'd never been able to fully articulate, and one of the side effects was a tendency to notice things at the edge of perception when her nose told her something was interesting. What her nose said now was: the men in the back were stressed. The sour-flat smell of sustained stress rather than acute fear, the smell of a problem that had been sitting for a while.

She heard: "—still haven't replaced the Hartley woman—"

And: "—can't exactly run an advertisement—"

And, more quietly, after a pause: "—three months at minimum before the new one's producing at volume, if she survives the first month—"

She paid Cade. He folded the wrapping shut and pushed it across the counter. His hands were, as always, extremely clean.

The phrasing: *if she survives the first month.* She turned it over once. She knew, in the abstract professional way she knew many things, where Bloom came from. The information had arrived cleanly, without detailed elaboration, and she had put it in the category of things that were the supply chain's business rather than hers.

She put it there again now. Quick and clean.

She went back to her rooms and put the new supply in the tin and the tin in the chest and went to the window and looked at the street for a while, which was something she did when she was thinking and preferred not to be aware of it.

The Noble Quarter address. The girl with no visible change. The fee.

She had a job. Jobs had their own momentum, which was one of the things she valued about them.

She began planning the route north.

---

That evening she sat at the table and opened her maps and made notes in her shorthand. The notes were in cipher — her own, built over years — but the cipher was only part of it. Each entry carried something else: a faint chemical record of the moment she'd written it, laid down by her skin without intention, as involuntary as breath. The stress-smell of a stakeout at hour eleven. The particular cold-salt of a dockside morning. The metallic thread of a room where someone had been afraid. Anyone who cracked the cipher would still only have the words. The context lived in the margins, legible only to a nose that could read it.

She had not told anyone this. It was not the kind of operational advantage you explained.

At the edges of her attention, the warmth of last night's Bloom was already receding — not a craving, not yet. More like a room you've left that you are becoming aware of having left. She noticed it because she'd had the files open for twenty minutes and the connections between them were coming slowly, one at a time, each link requiring conscious effort. This was how it always went, in the ordinary state: a patience tax, paid in full. She worked through it.

She had learned, early in her use, that the Bloom did something specific to the work. At the height of it, with the files spread around her, she could feel the whole picture without reading a line — the scent-record of every note rising to meet her at once, weeks or months of observations compositing into something that arrived less like thought than like recognition. Connections her waking mind would take hours to construct, given in a breath. She had broken three contracts open this way. Had walked into a room and known, from the smell of her own old notes, exactly where the gap in her map was.

She had told herself this was a tool like any other. She had been telling herself this for four years.

She noted the distance to Sanctum Basin. She noted the road conditions she'd heard about from a carter that morning in the market. She noted that *no visible change* at seventeen was late — late enough to be notable, late enough that someone who understood what it meant would have two possible explanations, and one of those explanations was rare enough to be worth a Noble Quarter fee.

She had heard the word *bloodless* perhaps four times in her life. Once from an old woman in her building who'd had a neighbor, years ago, and spoke of him the way you speak of a storm that passed through — with a kind of awed inconvenience. Once from a client who'd wanted someone found and wouldn't say why, and she'd declined that contract because declined was sometimes the right answer. Once from Cade, obliquely, in a different context she hadn't fully understood — something about the eastern highlands, a place the dockside old-timers called *the hill that moves in the light*, spoken in the particular tone that meant the name was a warning rather than a description.

Once from herself, in the mirror, at fifteen, the morning after a night that had produced no warmth, no light, no sense of opening — nothing except the ordinary face she'd had the day before, and the day before that, and every day she could remember.

She had been wrong, eventually. The change had come three years later, and it had come in a direction she hadn't anticipated, and she had made her peace with this in the only way that ever took: by finding use for what she had.

The smell of the city came up through the window. She identified its components automatically, let them settle, and went back to the maps.

The girl was three weeks ahead of her. Less, if she was on foot.

She was probably on foot.

Vane began to work out the math.


## Chapter Five — *[Maren]*
### North Is the Safest Direction

---

On the first day she made two mistakes and one good decision.

The mistakes: she walked the road in the early morning when the light was low and she felt safe in it, which was exactly when she was most visible against the empty pale country; and she stopped at a stream to refill her water skin in a spot with no clear sightlines, which meant she had to make a very undignified scramble into a clump of thorned scrub when a rider came over the rise to the east and she had nowhere better to go. She crouched in the thorns for twenty minutes. The rider passed without slowing. She had scratches on both forearms and her left knee from the scramble, and the water skin was only half full.

The good decision: she threw away the coal pouch at the first crossing.

Not the whole pouch — she kept the leather, because the leather was useful and smelled like Delia's kitchen and she was not going to examine what that meant. The charcoal she scattered in the stream. It had done its work. What it was now was evidence, and evidence was a category of thing she could not afford. She watched the black powder disperse in the current and felt something settle in her chest: the specific relief of setting down something you've been carrying so long you'd stopped feeling the weight.

After that she walked by night and slept in daylight.

The sleeping was not restful. She'd spent her life in a bed, in a room, with walls, and the open ground was a different proposition in every way she hadn't considered. The sounds were unfamiliar. The light came through her eyelids at angles that had nothing to do with windows. She slept in snatches and woke tense and lay very still until she'd catalogued what had woken her, which was usually wind, or a bird, or her own heart going too fast for the level of danger present.

By the third night she was sleeping better. By the fourth she was waking with the false dawn and feeling the particular quality of her own alertness as something close to useful. Her body was adjusting without consulting her. She found she was grateful for this, and then noted that gratitude — her body was adjusting to running. That was not the kind of progress she had planned to be making at seventeen.

She catalogued what she knew. It was a habit she'd developed in Delia's kitchen — making lists of the available, because Delia said that a healer who didn't know her stock was just a person with good intentions and some herbs. She knew: the road markers were accurate to within about half a day's walk, and she didn't need to be on the road to use them. She knew: watercress grew at the edge of any stream that moved fast enough to stay cold, and it tasted of almost nothing but it kept the hunger from turning sharp. She knew: dew was most reliable on the north-facing side of large stones, and there was always more of it than you expected. She knew the rough names and uses of most plants that grew in plateau country, and several that grew in the scrublands she was moving through now, and a few that grew in the lowland forest she could see in the far distance ahead.

She was, she realized, substantially less helpless than she'd felt when she'd gone out the back gate four nights ago. She had been thinking of herself as helpless for a long time without examining the assumption. Examining it now, running for her life across open country, felt belated.

She ate watercress and noted this.

---

On the third morning she made the worst mistake of the journey so far, and it was the kind that came from competence rather than its absence.

She found a stream running north — fast, cold, clean, the kind Delia would have followed without hesitation because a stream like that meant watercress and comfrey and the particular soft-stemmed violet that grew where the water slowed around stones, useful for poultice work. Every piece of knowledge Maren had said: follow the water. Water was supply, water was direction, water was the thread you followed through unfamiliar country because it always went somewhere.

She followed it for three hours, foraging as she went, filling the water skin twice, feeling the particular satisfaction of a plan that was working. The stream bent east around a low ridge and she bent with it.

On the other side of the ridge the stream opened into a broad shallow flood-flat — treeless, grassless, a wide pale expanse of gravel and standing water that stretched two hundred yards in every direction before the scrubland resumed on the far side. She was fifty yards into it before she fully understood what she'd done. She was visible from every direction. The ground was open and wet and offered nothing — no cover, no scrub, nothing higher than her ankle. The ridge she'd come around blocked her retreat; going back meant three hours of backtracking. Going forward meant crossing two hundred yards of open ground in full daylight.

She stood in the flood-flat and felt, for the first time since leaving Ashwick, genuinely stupid.

Not frightened — she'd been frightened since the first night. Stupid. The herb-woman's logic had led her here because the herb-woman's logic was about finding things, and a fugitive's logic was about not being found, and those were opposite problems and she had been solving the wrong one.

She crossed the flat at a crouch that did nothing useful — there was nothing to crouch behind — and her boots filled with water and the gravel shifted under her feet in a way that made every step feel observed. The far side took twenty minutes to reach. It felt longer. When she made the scrub she pressed herself flat against the ground and lay still for ten minutes, breathing too fast, listening for hoofbeats that didn't come.

No one had seen her. Probably. She couldn't know.

She spent the rest of the day backtracking to the point where the stream had bent east, and then continued north overland without following water, and the day was gone. A full day lost to a competence that had turned out to be the wrong competence applied to the wrong problem. She ate the watercress she'd gathered that morning and it tasted like evidence of her own miscalculation.

She noted this also, less comfortably than the previous noting.

---

The patrol found the road before she did.

She came up on a rise at dusk on the fourth day and saw them below: three riders, and a fourth man standing in the road beside a farm cart, answering a question she was too far away to hear. One rider held slightly apart from the others, a bearing to his posture that spoke of minor rank — the straight back of someone accustomed to authority rather than accustomed to following orders. Another had equipment she could make out even at distance: a coil of rope looped over the saddle horn, a long-handled tool she didn't recognize but which had the look of something specialized. A tracker's kit, maybe. The third sat easy, watching the road behind. The unhurried attentiveness of people conducting an organized search, not traveling through. They knew which direction she'd gone. They were estimating how far.

She sat down in the scrub and her hands were shaking.

Not the controlled kind. The real kind — the kind that started in the fingers and went up through the wrists and settled somewhere behind the sternum, where it became a vibration she could feel in her breathing. She pressed her palms flat against the ground, the way she'd seen Delia steady a patient, and told her body to be quiet. Her body did not listen. She was shaking and her vision had gone narrow and bright and she could hear her own pulse and none of this was useful. None of this was the calm assessment of a girl who'd been cataloguing her skills like inventory, and the distance between who she'd thought she was becoming and who she actually was right now — hands in the dirt, heart hammering, everything she'd learned in four days of careful adaptation gone — was the most instructive thing that had happened to her yet.

She made herself watch.

The farmer shook his head. He said something and pointed north with what looked like honest uncertainty rather than directed information. The lead rider nodded once and said something to the others and they moved on up the road without consulting the farmer further.

She stayed. The shaking took another two minutes to stop and she let it, because fighting it made it worse, and Delia had taught her that about fear the same way she'd taught her about fevers: you didn't suppress them, you sat with them until the body had done what it needed to do. She sat with it. The shaking passed. The breathing settled. The narrow bright vision opened back up.

Then she waited until they were past the next curve, then waited longer, then went wide around the road and cut north through a section of scrubland that looked rough and was, in fact, rough. Her ankle turned twice on the uneven ground. She kept moving. By the time the road was visible again in the lower country ahead, the moon had come up and the riders were gone and her knee from the thorn-scramble had stiffened into something she was going to have to think about tomorrow.

Three riders. For one girl.

They were not questioning whether she was worth three riders.

She thought about what her father had cost, and stopped thinking about it, and walked.

---

The boy's cart was a quarter-mile from a farmstead and his turnips were in the road.

He was perhaps ten, scowling at a wheel that had caught a rut and shed its load with the focused outrage of someone encountering a problem they can see the shape of but cannot yet solve by themselves. The turnips were everywhere. They were, she had time to think, impressive turnips — large and very purple, the good kind that stored through winter without going soft. Someone had grown these with attention.

She should not stop. She had evaluated this in approximately two seconds: stopping meant being seen and remembered; the farmstead was close enough that the farm wife would notice a stranger; the road was exposed; the riders were not so far ahead that they couldn't double back.

She stopped anyway.

"The wheel's not broken," she said, looking at it. "Just caught."

The boy looked at her with the specific suspicion of a child who has learned that adults offering help usually want something. "I can see that."

"Two hands on the back axle while I lift the front." She demonstrated without waiting for his agreement. The cart shifted and the wheel came free of the rut and she lowered it without drama. "Now the turnips."

They picked up turnips in silence. The boy was fast about it, clearly calculating whether she was going to steal any. She wasn't, and apparently this became evident, because by the time the cart was loaded he had moved from suspicion to a more neutral assessment.

"Where are you going?" he asked.

"North."

"That's not a place."

"No," she agreed.

He studied her for a moment. She looked as she imagined she looked: road-worn, thorn-scratched, carrying a pack that was not heavy enough to mean much either way. Nothing that was alarming and nothing that was comfortable.

"My mother makes a lot of soup," he said finally. "There's usually extra."

She should not go to the farmstead. She had evaluated this also, faster this time. She went anyway.

---

The farm wife's name was Betta, and she was a compact, capable woman in her late forties who had the quality Maren associated with people who had solved so many practical problems that solving them had become invisible, a permanent background process. She fed Maren soup without asking many questions, which Maren understood was its own kind of question — Betta was deciding something about her while appearing to simply ladle soup. Maren ate and let herself be decided about, because the soup was good and she had not had hot food in four days and some calculations were easy.

The boy was named Corvin. He ate soup and watched Maren with the focused attention of a child who has found something interesting and intends to extract information from it.

"Why is your arm wrapped?" he asked.

"Corvin," said Betta.

"It's an old burn," Maren said. "Mostly healed."

She said it the way she'd said it a hundred times, reflexively, and then heard herself say it and felt a specific interior lurch: the lie from before, still in her mouth out of habit, now pointless. There was no Chrysalis performance she was protecting. The deacon had already seen. She was already running.

She was carrying the coal pouch that wasn't a coal pouch anymore and the story about the burn and the reflex of performance, and none of it was necessary and she had not noticed she was still doing it until this moment.

"It's not a burn," she said.

Corvin looked at her with interest. Betta went still for a moment, ladling paused.

"There's nothing wrong with the arm," Maren said. "I just got used to keeping it wrapped."

She pulled the linen loose, unspooling it from her forearm, and set it on the table. Her arm looked exactly as it had always looked. She folded the bandage once, neatly, and put it in her pocket — linen was useful; she'd use it on her knee. She did not put it back on.

There was a pause. Betta resumed ladling. "More soup?" she said.

"Please," Maren said.

It was the most honest conversation she'd had in three years, and it had lasted approximately four sentences. She was not sure whether that was encouraging or depressing. She ate the second bowl and helped wash the bowls afterward and accepted, with genuine gratitude, the offer of the barn for the night.

---

Before she went out to the barn, Corvin found her at the door. He was holding something out to her, palm up, with the slightly aggressive generosity of a child who has decided to give a gift and is not going to be talked out of it.

It was a small wooden bird. Rough, clearly hand-carved — she could see the knife marks where he hadn't quite smoothed the wing joints, the tail a little asymmetrical, the body blocky in the way of something made by hands still learning their strength. But there was genuine care in it. The head was tilted slightly, as though the bird was listening, and he'd taken time with that, gotten it right.

"I made it," he said. Unnecessary, but offered as a kind of credential.

She took it. It sat in her palm, light and warm from his hand. "It's good," she said, and meant it.

He shrugged with the elaborate unconcern of a boy who cares very much. He went back inside.

She put the bird in her pocket alongside the disc and went out to the barn.

---

The barn was warm in the way of places where animals had lived for a long time — a deep settled warmth that had nothing to do with fire, that came from the accumulated heat of many large bodies over many years. There were two cows and a donkey and they assessed her with the incurious thoroughness of animals who had seen many things pass through a barn and found most of them uninteresting.

She lay in the hay and thought about what she had of value.

Money: enough for perhaps eight more days of careful spending, less if she needed a real bed or a meal she hadn't found on the road. Skills: herb knowledge, basic wound care, strong legs, a functional understanding of what not to eat and how to move quietly. The knife, which was good. Delia's empty pouch. Her mother's comb, which had no practical application she was willing to examine.

The disc.

She took it out and looked at it in the thin moonlight coming through the barn's gap boards. It was unremarkable: dull metal, lighter than it looked, perhaps the width of two fingers across. One side was perfectly smooth. The other had a shallow indentation she'd always taken for damage, a dent or a gouge from a drop, and three markings around the edge she'd always taken for decoration. She looked at the markings now with different attention.

Her great-aunt, when she'd passed the disc to Maren after her mother's death — Maren was thirteen, she'd been too raw and numb to ask questions — had said: *don't lose this, don't show anyone, it's been in the family a long time.* Maren had accepted this the way a thirteen-year-old accepts things from a grieving adult, which was without pressing further. She had kept it because it was a small metal thing that was easy to keep and because it was, in a family that had lost a great deal, something that had survived.

She looked at the markings and thought: someone made this for a reason.

This was not a thought she'd had before. She didn't know what to do with it. She put the disc back in her pocket, beside the small whittled bird the turnip boy had pressed into her hand, and lay looking at the ceiling for a while.

Through the wall she heard the farmhouse, the small sounds of a household settling. A window latch. Water in a basin. The creak of a chair. Then, later, something that broke the pattern: a door — the back door, not the front — and then Betta's voice, low, and another voice from outside, not a family voice, a voice that arrived at a particular register and then stopped.

She did not breathe.

Betta spoke again. The quality of it was what Maren had learned, in four days, to pay attention to: not the words but the shape of them. Careful. Controlled. The voice of someone managing a conversation they had not wanted to have.

The outside voice said something. Not a question. A statement made quietly, which was its own kind of pressure.

Then Betta. One sentence, also quiet. Then the door.

Then nothing.

Maren lay in the hay for a long time. The cows breathed. The donkey shifted and settled. Outside, whatever had been at the door was gone, or she couldn't hear it, which was not the same thing.

She was probably being paranoid. Three riders and a well-traveled road, and now she was constructing threat from the sound of a farmwife speaking at her own back door.

She was probably being paranoid in the same way she'd probably been wrong to notice the deacon's quality of looking.

She left before the false dawn, in the dark between the last moon and the first light. She did not take anything that wasn't hers. She wanted to leave something — coins, the herbs from her pack, a note — and did not, because leaving things behind was leaving a record of the direction she'd been going and she couldn't be sure about the conversation at the door and she wasn't going to choose sentiment over sense.

She was sorry to leave the warmth of the barn. She noted this as she was leaving it, the specific pang of it, and noted also that she was not sorry about anything else. Four days ago she would have been. She was making calculations now. Whether that was good or bad could wait.

She chose north and walked into the dark.

---

Two hours from the farmstead, in the first gray light, she reached a boundary marker.

She'd seen them before — squat stone pillars, waist-height, carved with the Chrysalis spiral and a designation for the nearest temple district. Usually she'd passed them without much attention. You paid attention to them if you were traveling on official business or settling a legal claim or making a pilgrimage, and she had never been doing any of those things.

She stopped at this one.

The spiral was worn smooth in the center from hands laid on it over years. Below it, on the facing side: a carved phrase in formal temple script that she'd been taught to read as a child. *In this territory, all who carry the gift are under the protection of the Chrysalis.* She had read it before. It had never made her feel anything particular. It referred to a category she was not in, and she'd always understood this the way you understood a rule that didn't apply to you — abstractly, without personal relevance.

She looked at it now.

*All who carry the gift.* She didn't carry the gift. This had been the fixed fact around which she'd organized her understanding of herself for three years — for longer, honestly, since she'd started to suspect at thirteen that she wasn't going to open and had known for certain at sixteen that she never would. She'd called it being hollow, which was the word everyone else used, which she'd adopted because children adopt the words adults give them even when the words fit badly.

Hollow. Empty of the thing that was supposed to be in her.

She stood at the marker in the early morning and considered this for what felt like the first time with her full attention rather than with the part of her that was managing the fact of it.

She was not hollow. She was the same as she'd been before she'd had a word for what she was. She'd been thinking since she was fourteen, making observations since she was young enough to make them, growing herbs and setting poultices and carrying things and walking fast and sleeping in barns when she needed to and picking up turnips when a boy needed an extra pair of hands. The hollow was not in her.

The hollow was in someone's plan for her.

Three riders for one girl who doesn't have the change.

The deacon didn't look at Bram. He didn't look at Jory. He didn't look at anyone's arm the way he looked at mine.

Her father had left in the night. He had no change either. He left a long time before she was old enough to need the coal pouch. No one was looking for a reason to look at him, back then.

Something the bloodline had wanted, back then, for whatever reason, and still wanted now. She was a seventeen-year-old girl walking north across plateau country in the predawn cold, and she was a piece of something she didn't have the shape of yet.

She took the disc from her pocket and turned it in her hand. The markings caught the growing light, just for a moment.

She was going to need to understand what she was a piece of.

She put the disc away and kept walking. The sun came up in front of her and slightly right, which meant she was drifting east and needed to correct, and she corrected, and the road appeared below her in the valley and she took it, because paranoia had limits and her knee needed even ground.

North was the safest direction. It was also the only direction she had.

She walked it.
## Chapter Six — *[Davan]*
### Confluence

---

The city announced itself by smell before it came into view.

Davan had grown up in a valley town that smelled of woodsmoke and river water and the particular sweetness of fermenting grain from the mill at the south end, and he'd thought of this as just the smell of a place where people lived, unremarkable. The Confluence had been teaching him, for the past hour of walking, that what he'd grown up with was small and specific. A city built where three rivers met carried three sets of river-smell and the commerce that traveled all of them: fish and wet wool and tallow and the sharp bite of a tannery somewhere upwind that he hoped was far away. Underneath it, people — more people than he'd been near at one time in his life, their aggregate warmth reaching him as something close to pressure.

He stopped on the ridge above the valley and looked at it.

Not the largest city on Thera. He knew this from asking about it on the road, where he'd learned that New Plymouth to the north made this look like what it was: a river-junction town that had grown prosperous and dense and somewhat tangled, buildings climbing the valley sides in an arrangement that suggested less planning than accumulated decision. Three bridges. A temple dominating the eastern bank, older than the buildings around it, its stone a different color — darker, rougher — than anything nearby. A market square he could identify from here by the quality of its noise, a particular active frequency distinct from the general roar.

He took a moment to get his bearings, the way you did from any high point after weeks on foot. He'd been walking for three weeks from Amber Valley, three hundred miles south in a straight line, though his route had not been straight. The Confluence sat roughly in the middle of the north-south road, where it earned its name. New Plymouth lay another two hundred miles north, more easily reached by river from here than by road. To the east the country rose into highlands — Irongate territory, he'd been told, closed and self-sufficient. He was not going east. He was going where the water went.

He had come here with no specific plan except that the Confluence was on the way north and large enough to be anonymous in, the two qualities he currently valued in a place. He had enough money for two days of simple living if he was careful, possibly three. After that he would need to find work or keep moving or both.

He went down into the city.

---

The market was everything he'd heard markets could be and had not, growing up in Amber Valley, had much reason to verify. He moved through it slowly, not shopping — he couldn't afford to shop — but taking it in with the attention that had become habitual over three weeks of walking. The available, the scarce, the priced beyond reason and the priced below it. Amber Valley had a market but it was a modest thing, the same faces each week, the same goods in roughly the same arrangement. This was different in kind rather than degree: a dozen people selling dried fish alone, three grades of lamp oil, cloth in weights and colors he'd never seen, a spice merchant whose stall reached him before he saw it, a wall of warm complicated smell that stopped him in the street.

The people were different also. In Amber Valley, the changes were common and visible and mostly familiar — he'd grown up with the same forty or so people's mutations, watched them develop over years, had a personal history with each one. Here he was passing strangers who carried changes he had no reference for. A woman whose fingers ended in small curved hooks, efficient and precise as she counted coins. A man whose skin had gone to a fine pale iridescence that caught the light when he moved and threw small rainbows onto the stall canvas beside him, apparently unaware of this effect, or aware and indifferent. A child, perhaps seven, who was probably pre-change but whose eyes tracked movement with an acuity that suggested otherwise.

He looked at all of it. He was not sure if what he felt was wonder or envy or simply the peculiar emotional weather of being a long way from home — something that arrived as a kind of humidity in the chest, not painful, just present.

Then the woman selling river-eel two stalls down had a brief sharp argument with a customer that produced a spike of anger so specific and so localized that Davan turned without meaning to, looking for the source, as if the anger had been a sound rather than — what? He stood very still for a moment. The argument resolved itself, the customer paid and left, the woman's anger dropped away. The pressure in Davan's chest dropped with it.

He walked on. He did not look back at the eel stall.

He had been telling himself for two weeks that the empathic bleed, or whatever it was, would settle as the change matured. He had no basis for this. No idea if it would settle. No one to ask.

He was at the far end of the market row, near the cloth sellers, when he felt the other thing.

Not anger — he'd learned to recognize anger's specific spike. Something lower and wider, a compound he didn't have a name for yet. Unease and something harder underneath, something with an edge to it. Contempt, maybe, but with fear stirred through it, the way certain poisons worked by mixing two things that were harmless on their own. It was coming from several directions at once, converging.

He looked for the source and found it quickly, because the crowd was making it visible. A gap had opened in the foot traffic near a dry-goods stall — not a large gap, just the particular widening that happened when people moved away from something without quite deciding to, the body's verdict arriving before the mind's. In the gap: a man, perhaps forty, thin, unremarkable clothes, carrying a canvas bag he'd been filling with purchases. He was standing at the dry-goods counter waiting to be served. The vendor was serving someone else. The vendor continued serving someone else. Then a third someone else. The man waited. He had the quality of someone who was accustomed to waiting like this and had a practiced way of standing through it — straight-backed, face neutral, hands still, offering no one anything to react to.

The vendor ran out of other customers. She looked at the man and then past him and called to a woman browsing the next stall over, who had not been waiting, and served her instead.

The man said nothing. He stood. The gap around him widened slightly. A woman with a child crossed to the other side of the row rather than pass behind him. She did it casually, the way you'd step around a puddle — not dramatically, not with hostility, just the small efficient avoidance of something she didn't want her child near.

Davan watched this from three stalls away and the compound feeling was clearer now. Not one person's emotion but many, overlapping — the vendor's rigid refusal, the mother's quick uneasy calculation, and from the ring of people nearest the gap, a low-frequency hum of something Davan could only call *wrongness-directed-outward.* As if the man's presence, his ordinary unremarkable presence, was producing a reaction in the people around him that had nothing to do with anything he was doing and everything to do with something he wasn't. Something missing. Something that should have been there and wasn't — the baseline warmth, the Chrysalis signature, the thing that meant *one of us* at a level below thought.

He wasn't showing a change. Davan looked for one — the hooks, the iridescence, the reflective eyes, any of the dozen variations he'd seen in the last hour — and found nothing. The man looked simply, entirely, human. Unchanged. And the crowd around him was responding to that absence the way a body responds to a splinter — with a local inflammation, a focused rejection, an effort to push out the thing that didn't belong.

The man bought nothing. After a long moment he shouldered his bag, which already held purchases from elsewhere — other stalls, other vendors, ones who had apparently been willing — and walked on. He did not look at anyone. He walked the way Davan imagined you walked after a lifetime of this: steadily, without expecting anything different, the posture of someone carrying a weight that didn't show but had settled into the bones.

What Davan felt from him, as he passed, was the worst part. Not anger, not humiliation, not even the flat endurance he'd expected. Something quieter than all of those. A tiredness that had worn through tiredness and come out the other side into something that functioned like calm but wasn't. The emotional equivalent of scar tissue.

Davan stood in the market and watched him go and felt the scar tissue pass through him like cold water, and he thought about the girl on the road ahead of him, dark-haired, seventeen, no change visible, and he understood for the first time that what she was running from was not just the riders.

---

He found the temple in the late afternoon, drawn there in the practical way — the temple district in a city this size would have a hostel for travelers with legitimate reasons to use it, which included pilgrims but also, at a sufficiently senior cleric's discretion, wandering young men willing to help stack records crates and carry water. He had done this in two towns already on the road north and the strategy had worked both times.

He was crossing the temple's outer court when he saw the Keeper.

Not up close. The man was across the court, speaking with two junior priests in the quiet decisive way of someone accustomed to resolving small problems before they became large ones — a quality Davan associated with his father, who had run a woodworking shop with a similar economy of intervention. The Keeper was large, bearded, perhaps fifty, with the particular kind of tired that came from administrative work rather than physical labor. He was not looking at Davan. He walked inside without noticing him.

Davan watched him go and then turned to the door.

Above the door: the Chrysalis symbol, carved in the stone lintel in the old formal style, large enough to see clearly from the courtyard. He had looked at this symbol in some form his entire life — above the village shrine, on the traveling priests' vestments, pressed into the wax of official documents. He had always read the interior spiral as a spiral. Every Blooming ceremony in Amber Valley had begun with the Recitation — *And the Three Witnesses moved through the dark, and they saw the world below* — and the spiral was supposed to be the Chrysalis descending, the gift entering the Founders, the beautiful orderly story of a world being given to the people who deserved it.

He looked at the symbol now and something in him responded — not his eyes, not his mind, but the part of him that had flinched from the eel-seller's anger. The empathic sense, or whatever it was becoming. The symbol felt like something. Not sacred, not decorative. Lonely. The loneliness of a thing going around and around and around, held in place by something it couldn't leave and couldn't reach. Not a spiral. Not a descent. A vigil. He stood in the courtyard with the feeling of it washing through him until one of the junior priests asked if he needed assistance, and he said yes, and went about the practical business of negotiating a night's lodging in exchange for the record crates.

He did not stop thinking about what he'd seen in the symbol.

---

He ate in the temple hostel with a dozen other travelers, none of whom he spoke to at length: a family going to New Plymouth for a Blooming ceremony; two merchants who talked to each other exclusively; an old man who ate with fierce concentration and then immediately fell asleep at the table. The food was plain and filling. Davan ate his portion and half of what the sleeping man had left, without guilt, because his body had been making reasonable demands for three weeks and most of those demands had been only partially met.

After eating he went down to the river.

The Confluence was best understood from the bank, he thought. Three rivers arriving at the same place from different directions, merging not violently but with a kind of patient inevitability, the water from each direction taking time to fully mix, so that in the right light you could still see the separate currents moving alongside each other in the main channel, not yet the same thing. It was the kind of sight that made him want to carve something, his version of wanting to think.

He sat on the bank and worked on the piece he'd been carrying for a week — a section of hardwood he'd found on the road, closer-grained than pine, more resistant to the knife in a way that demanded more attention. He was making something he couldn't name yet. A shape that opened at one end and closed at the other. A form that had something to do with a container but wasn't one.

He sat until the light went and then stayed longer, because the river at night was its own thing.

The barge came in with the last of the twilight — a long flat-bottomed vessel, river freight, heading north with a load of clay casks and four passengers visible on the forward deck. It tied up at the downstream dock with the competence of a crew that had done this a thousand times, no drama, just the particular choreography of ropes and fenders and shouted numbers.

He had been thinking about the barge north for two days. He had the money for a passenger fare if he didn't eat much before he got to New Plymouth, and the road north from the Confluence was, by all accounts he'd gathered, less interesting than the river for getting from one place to another. He watched the barge crew work and confirmed his decision, made before he watched them.

He went to speak to the captain about a fare.

---

The barge left at first light.

Davan stood at the bow and watched the Confluence shrink behind them and the northern valley open up, the river widening as the merged currents found their new course. The other three passengers were aft: a merchant couple who had brought their own food and showed no interest in company, and a woman traveling alone sitting on a folded blanket with her back against the cargo and her eyes closed.

Her name was Lera. She told him this when she opened her eyes and found him looking at her, not with annoyance but with the simple directness of someone for whom social conventions around privacy had become largely theoretical.

She was perhaps sixty, or perhaps older in ways that made the number less useful — the Chrysalis had been working on her long enough that her arms, where they were visible below her rolled sleeves, had gone translucent at the wrists, the tendons visible beneath skin that had grown faintly luminous. Not light exactly. The suggestion of it, the way certain river stones seem to glow from within when wet. The change had reached her hands fully: the fingers longer than they should have been by perhaps a joint each, elegant and strange, the nails gone to a dark iridescence. Her face was still entirely her own, a face that suggested a lifetime of paying close attention and a comfortable relationship with what she'd found. Her eyes were pale, not yet the full silver of the most advanced changes, but on their way — the brown she'd presumably been born with had gone to a gray-gold that caught light differently than ordinary eyes, like water over a copper basin.

"You've been looking at me since the dock," she said. Not an accusation.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Don't be. I look at people also." She considered him with the same open attention. "You're a long way from home."

"How do you know that?"

"You're watching the river like it's doing something new," she said. "People who grew up with this river don't watch it anymore."

He sat down on the deck. Not because she'd invited him to but because it seemed like the right response to her manner, and she didn't indicate otherwise.

"Where are you going?" he asked.

"New Plymouth. The final Rite." She said it without drama, the way you'd say you were going to market. "I've been meaning to go for some years. My daughter kept finding reasons to delay me." She seemed amused by this. "She's stopped trying."

He knew what the final Rite was. He'd known people in Amber Valley who had gone — not often, the most profound changes were not common, but occasionally. It was the rite for those in the last stages of the transformation, a ceremony held at the Grand Temple, a formal witnessing of what the change had made of a person's life. He looked at her arms again, briefly, and she allowed this.

"Does it hurt?" he asked. "The change, at this stage."

"Not anymore." She looked at her own forearms with honest assessment rather than performance. "The first years hurt. When it's working against what you expect, what you've decided you should be. Then it stops hurting and starts just being what you are." A pause. "That's the theology. It's also my experience, which I can only speak for myself."

They rode in silence for a while. The river bent east around a long curve and the sun came off the water at an angle that turned it briefly white.

"Can I ask you something?" Davan said.

"You've been asking me things."

"Something I don't know how to ask politely."

She made a small gesture that meant: ask it.

"The old stories about the Chrysalis," he said. "The Founders. The first people. The church says the change was given, that the moons witnessed it, that the Founders were — chosen, somehow, for this world." He stopped. "What if they weren't?"

She looked at him. "What if they were what instead?"

"Just people," he said. "Who came from somewhere and found themselves here and the world affected them and they made a story about it later to explain what had happened."

It was not a question he'd ever asked aloud. He was not entirely sure where it had been living, except that it had apparently been living somewhere and the river and the morning and the transparent-armed woman with the direct manner had constituted the right conditions for it to come out.

She considered this for long enough that he started to regret asking it.

Then she said: "What would be the difference?"

He blinked. "What?"

"The change is real. The transformation is real. The world entered them and made them into something they hadn't been before, something more than they'd been before, and it has continued to do this with every person who has carried the Chrysalis since." She was not arguing with him. She was reasoning alongside him. "Whether someone chose them for this or it was an accident of being in the wrong place — or the right place — what does that change about the thing that happened?"

He thought about it. "It changes what we're supposed to do with it."

"Does it?" She tilted her head. "I've always thought that part was ours regardless. The story about where it came from doesn't tell me what to do with my arms. I worked that out myself, over fifty years." She looked at the water for a moment. "The story is a useful envelope. What's inside the envelope is what matters."

"But if the story is wrong—"

"Lots of useful things are technically wrong," she said. "A map is wrong. A map is a simplification of the territory that lets you move through the territory without getting lost. The getting-lost or not is the point, not the map." She paused. "Though I'll admit that a map that was *very* wrong, fundamentally wrong in a way that sent you off a cliff, would be a different problem."

He sat with this.

"You've thought about this before," he said.

"I've been thinking about it for forty years," she said. "You have the advantage of being earlier in the process."

The river moved. The merchant couple ate their packed food and ignored them. The morning grew into afternoon.

---

He slept on the deck that night under his coat, adequate for the temperature and not for the stars — more than he'd expected, the moons all showing and the cloudless sky giving him more of the dark between them than he'd noticed before. He lay on his back and looked up.

Three moons. All of them up at once, more common than it seemed like it ought to be given their different cycles — he'd been tracking them for weeks without intending to, absorbed their patterns the way you absorbed any sufficiently regular thing in your environment. He could predict when Swiftmoon would be full now without counting on his fingers. He was not sure why this felt important.

He did not notice when his skin-light began.

He became aware of it gradually, the warmth of it before the visible part, a gentle pulse that went with his breathing rather than against it. Blue-calm, he thought — the bluer register, the one that came when he was neither afraid nor particularly joyful but simply present, existing in a moment without urgency. He'd been beginning to map the colors to their meanings the way you learned a language you'd been hearing for years before you started studying it. The grammar was becoming legible.

He heard her shift.

He turned his head. Lera was sitting up on her folded blanket, a few feet away, watching the light move in his skin. Not with alarm. With the same open attention she'd given him all day.

"Sorry," he said. "I'm not — I can't always control—"

"I know," she said. "I've watched others learn. It comes when it wants, at first."

He looked back up at the sky. After a moment she spoke again, not unkindly.

"Your mutation will become what you believe it is."

He thought about this. "The way your arms became what you believed?"

"Eventually. It took time. The first years I believed they were frightening, and they frightened me. Then I believed they were strange, and I lived strangely for a while." Something like amusement in her voice. "Then I spent a long time believing they were mine, and that seemed to do it."

"What do you believe about my mutation?"

"That's the wrong question," she said. "What do *you* believe about it?"

He looked at the light moving in his skin. Blue-calm. Present. Not performing anything, not managing anything — just the body's expression of itself in a quiet moment, its own legibility.

He said: "I think it shows the truth."

"Yes," she said, simply. As if he'd gotten something right.

He lay and watched the moons and let the light do what it did. Overhead, the three bright shapes moved in their slow arcs, going nowhere and going there with great dedication. He traced the paths of them against the dark until his eyes wanted to close.

He was almost asleep when the thought came back — the same one he'd been carrying since the inn at Colter's Crossing, since the counting rhyme had surfaced in his mind and he'd pushed it down because it didn't make sense. *Four fires in the sky, one came down to die, three stayed up to cry.* He'd been counting three every night for weeks. Three was what was there. Three was what everyone knew.

But the song said four, and the symbol above the temple door had felt like something going around and around something it couldn't leave, and Lera had said *I think it shows the truth*, and now, in the space between waking and sleep where things connected without asking permission, the counting rhyme and the symbol and the old woman's certainty were fitting together in a way they hadn't before. Not a new thought. The same thought, arriving with its full weight for the first time — the way a word you've known all your life suddenly means what it says.

He tried to hold onto the shape of it — four, not three, one came down, the vigil in the stone — but it was the kind of thought that couldn't survive being looked at directly. By morning there was only the absence of it: the knowledge that something had been there and was not, not nothing but not much better.

---

New Plymouth announced itself the same way the Confluence had, by smell before sight, but the Confluence's smell was a market town's smell and this was something else entirely: a city, a real one, the accumulated chemical fact of a hundred thousand lives lived in a small area for a very long time. It hit him a mile out and didn't diminish.

The river widened. Buildings appeared on the banks and didn't stop appearing. The barge captain called instructions and the crew moved with practiced efficiency. Davan stood at the bow and watched New Plymouth come at him.

The skyline was dominated by two things: the temple in the east district, enormous and very old, and the palace on the northern rise — also enormous, considerably newer, considerably more concerned with demonstrating that it was where it was. He had grown up hearing about the palace the way you heard about things that were real but too large to have a personal relationship with, the way you heard about the sea or the high desert. From the river it looked like the kind of building made to be looked at from this distance. He found himself thinking not about the stone but about what three hundred years of unbroken occupation implied — the particular leverage of a bloodline that had never needed to be replaced.

He thought about the Wound-maker. That was what they called it in Amber Valley — his grandmother's name for it, and she had said it with the weight of someone who believed the thing was real, not story. She had gotten this from her own grandmother, who had heard it from someone who claimed a cousin had been in New Plymouth during the Stilling of the Brace, three hundred years back or more, when a river-lord had marched on the city and the bloodline had brought the thing out onto the palace steps and used it once, just once, and the river-lord's army had stopped and then quietly gone home and the river-lord himself had not been seen again. The story had at least four versions even in Amber Valley. In two of them the weapon sang. In one it wept. In all of them it left a mark on the stone that was still there, black and clean as a burn, and no one who carried the change could look at it directly.

A relic, a royal secret, a cautionary tale, a children's story depending on who was telling it and why. He thought about it from the comfortable position of someone for whom it was all of those things at once and none of them quite real.

Then the barge docked, and he said goodbye to Lera, who pressed his hand and said nothing further, and he went into the city with his pack and his knife and his father's carving and approximately enough money for three days if he was careful, possibly four.

The city received him without comment. Cities, he was learning, were like rivers: they moved whether or not you were part of them, and the getting-in-or-not was entirely up to you.

He found a flophouse near the docks. He paid for a bunk. He lay in the dark with the noise of the city all around him and the empathic impressions coming through the walls — the sad person two rooms over, the angry person downstairs, the child two floors above dreaming something vivid enough to reach him at this distance — and he pressed his hand flat to the wall beside the bunk and tried to think of it not as intrusion but as information.

He did not sleep well.

By morning, he had decided to stay at least a week.

He was not ready to examine why.


## Chapter Seven — *[Aldric]*
### The Investigator

---

He had been lying to people for three weeks and was finding it more sustainable than he'd expected.

This was not a comfortable thing to discover about himself. He had spent thirty years in a profession that treated honesty as both a moral foundation and a practical necessity — people came to the temple in distress, and distress required truth to be useful, and he had believed this with the uncomplicated conviction of someone who had never seriously tested it. He had heard a great many confessions in his career and had always found the lies people told themselves more damaging than the ones they told each other, and had said so, and had meant it.

And now here he was, lying with moderate fluency to a mason, to a junior brother who had asked why the east undercroft smelled of old air, to the Confluence's district warden who had inquired about unusual activity in the temple precinct following a noise complaint from the neighboring house. Each time he had said the appropriate thing in the appropriate tone and the lie had held, and each time afterward he had sat with the particular flat discomfort of a man discovering the distance between his principles and his behavior.

The worst had been Petra.

Petra was the junior archivist — twenty-three, careful, devoted to the work in the way of someone who had not yet learned to separate devotion from expectation. She had come to him on the second week with a question about her own conduct: she had found a discrepancy in the archive catalog, a gap where records should have been, and she was unsure whether to report it through the standard channels or raise it with him directly, and she wanted his guidance because she believed — she had said this, looking at him with the particular trust of someone who has decided a person is worth trusting — that he was the most honest man she knew.

He had given her the correct advice. He had told her that discrepancies should always be reported through proper channels, that the integrity of the archive depended on transparent accounting, that she should never allow convenience or uncertainty to compromise her professional obligations. He had said all of this while sitting three floors above a sealed room he had not reported, containing objects he had not cataloged, which he was visiting in secret at three-day intervals. She had thanked him and left, and he had sat at his desk for a long time and had not been able to determine whether what he felt was hypocrisy or the particular cruelty of a man who still believed the principle he was in the process of violating.

He had also been going back to the chamber every three nights.

This he had not lied to himself about, which he supposed was something.

---

The glass — he had come to think of it this way, because it was the nearest word he had for something that showed you things it had no light source to show, in the particular way of objects that produced light from within and had no obvious reason to — had shown him different things each visit. Not randomly. He had begun to believe there was something like responsiveness to it, a quality of attending to what he lingered on. When he pressed his fingers near the orbital arrangements and held them there, the image stayed and sharpened. When he lifted his hand and came back to a different area, a different image surfaced: not the stars now but something closer, a curved blue-green shape hanging in the dark that produced in him, each time, a recognition he had no basis for and could not dismiss.

He had a basis for it, actually. He had been declining to.

The shape was in the old astronomy texts. Not prominently — a detail, a diagram, one of the bodies depicted in charts of the known lights with a symbol beside it that had always been glossed as *the wandering one* or *the distant blue* and which he had never examined closely because it had not been relevant to any question he was pursuing. He had not been pursuing questions of this kind. He had been pursuing questions of theology and administration and pastoral care, sufficient questions that did not, generally, require him to look very hard at old astronomy.

He had pulled the astronomy texts from the archive the previous week. He had looked at the diagram. He had looked at the shape on the glass.

He had put the texts back in exactly the position he'd found them.

He had gone to morning prayers and pinched the corridor lamp out of habit — already cold, had been cold for hours, he did it anyway — and stood in the chapel and said the words with the same attention he'd given them for thirty years, and had not been able to determine, afterward, whether the attention was the same or only looked the same from the inside.

---

The Prioress arrived on a Tuesday, a market day, and the timing meant that half the temple's staff were occupied with the increased pilgrim traffic and the other half were managing the administrative surge that market days produced. Her arrival was noticed by fewer people and with less ceremony than it might otherwise have been. Aldric suspected this was not an accident.

Her name was Prioress Crane. He had met her twice before — once at a regional convocation fifteen years ago, when she'd been a senior archivist and he'd been a recently-appointed Keeper still learning the administrative registers; once at a ceremony in New Plymouth that he'd attended as part of a temple delegation, at which she had been pointed out to him as someone of consequence whom he should know by sight. She was in her middle years, spare and precise in the way of someone who had decided long ago what was essential and had organized her person accordingly. She wore the Prioress's marks on her vestments without ostentation. Her change, if she had one, showed nowhere visible.

She arrived in his office with two junior priests in tow and the specific pleasantness of someone who had decided that pleasantness was the most efficient mode for this particular meeting.

"Keeper Voss," she said. "I hope the intrusion isn't — "

"Not at all," he said, which was the appropriate thing to say and was also completely untrue. "Please sit. I'll have tea brought."

She sat. He sent one of his own juniors for tea. The two priests she'd brought remained near the door in the practiced way of people whose function was presence rather than participation.

They spoke about the road conditions south of the Confluence, which had been poor since the autumn rains. They spoke about the Blooming numbers for the year — up slightly, a sign of a healthy community. They spoke about the pilgrimage traffic, increased following the death of a well-regarded traveling preacher whose route had passed through the Confluence and who had acquired, in death, the particular gravity that the living version of him had not quite managed.

She asked how he was finding the work.

"The usual richness," he said. "A temple at a junction sees everything eventually. I find it — sustaining." He paused with the right timing. "Occasionally exhausting."

"Of course." She accepted the tea that arrived and wrapped both hands around the cup in the gesture of someone who was cold, though the office was not cold. "I ask partly for administrative reasons and partly because I've heard you've been keeping unusual hours."

He met her eyes. "Seasonal administration," he said. "The pre-winter inventory runs long."

"The pre-winter inventory," she repeated, pleasantly.

"There's more to account for than it appears. Storage conditions in the undercroft in particular. I've been doing some of it myself rather than assign it to the junior brothers — they have enough to manage this time of year."

She nodded. She looked at him the way someone looks when they are deciding how much they already know versus how much they are being told. He held the eye contact because looking away was the wrong thing to do.

"I'll be here for some weeks," she said. "A doctrinal review — nothing alarming, standard circuit. The Confluence district is overdue." She smiled. "I hope we won't be in each other's way."

"The temple is yours, Prioress," he said. "I'll arrange suitable rooms."

After she left he sat at his desk for a while without doing anything. Then he reached toward the lamp and caught himself and lowered his hand and thought about what a doctrinal review meant in practice: access to the archive, access to the financial records, access to the correspondence logs, and the presence of a senior church official with the authority to request explanations for things that required explaining.

He thought about the sealed room two floors below him.

He thought about the note he'd found in the archive three weeks ago: *Restricted Collection, NP Archive, shelf 7-eastern. Access: Bloodline approval required.*

He thought about the letter to his former student at the New Plymouth archive — two weeks in composition, still unsent, because sending it would be the kind of action that could not be unsent. Some doors, once opened, rearranged the rooms on the other side.

He took out the letter. He read it. He revised two sentences that were more direct than they needed to be, replacing them with the oblique scholarly language of someone pursuing an innocent academic interest. He read it again.

He sealed it and put it with the outgoing correspondence.

Then he went and told the hostelmaster to prepare the east guest rooms for the Prioress's use, putting her on the opposite side of the temple from the undercroft — which was not why he chose those rooms and which he declined to examine.

---

The weeks of Crane's presence established a new rhythm. She was everywhere and nowhere — present at morning prayers, absent for stretches of the day, reappearing at meals with the pleasant undemanding manner of a very good house guest, the kind who requires nothing and therefore cannot be anticipated. He began to sense where she had recently been by the alertness she left behind, a quality that lingered in a room the way important things tended to.

She was reviewing the archive. He knew this because the junior archivist, Petra, had mentioned it with the bright careful neutrality of someone who had been told to be helpful and was being helpful in a way that communicated exactly how helpful she was being and nothing further. Crane was working through the eastern catalog. She had questions about certain acquisition dates. She was very thorough.

He had tried to stop.

This was the thing he did not tell himself often enough, perhaps because admitting it would require admitting that the attempt had been genuine and the failure had also been genuine, and those two facts together described a man he was not entirely prepared to be.

He had stopped for eight days. He had told himself, on the first morning, that the correct course was to complete the discovery report still sitting blank beneath other papers on his desk, to file it through the proper channels, and to allow the institution he had served for thirty years to do what institutions did — receive information, process it, determine its significance through the accumulated judgment of people whose job it was to determine such things. He had believed this. He had gone to morning prayers and said the words and pinched the corridor lamp — cold, as always — and had felt, for the first time in weeks, the specific relief of a man who has decided to behave correctly.

The relief lasted four days. On the fifth day he found himself standing at the top of the undercroft stairs at half past ten in the evening with no memory of having decided to go there. He had been reading in his room. He had set the book down. He had, apparently, walked the length of the eastern corridor and descended one flight of stairs while his conscious mind was still considering the next chapter of the pastoral commentary he'd been reading, and now here he was, at the door, with the cold draft coming up from below and his hand already reaching for the latch.

He went back to his room. He sat on the edge of his bed. He held his hands together in his lap and examined them as though they belonged to someone he was only beginning to know, and thought about the specific quality of a compulsion that did not feel like compulsion — that felt, instead, like a question the body had already answered while the mind was still formulating its objections.

On the eighth day he went down. He did not pretend, this time, that it was a considered decision. He went because the not-going had begun to feel less like discipline and more like a man pressing his hands over his eyes and calling it dark, and because the light in the glass below him was patient in the way of something that did not require his participation but was available for it, and the patience was worse than urgency would have been.

He continued his nights.

He had been going below every three nights now, with a regularity he had not intended. The glass was showing him more. He had developed, over the weeks, what he thought of as a tentative method: he would sit with it in the dark for the first hour, letting his eyes adjust to the cold light it produced, learning what was available on this visit, and then in the second hour he would work more deliberately, returning to specific arrangements and staying with them. The structure had come to him without effort — darkness first, then focused attention, then the slow layered deepening — and he had not examined where it came from, in the way he had learned not to examine certain things about himself. The star chart had become more legible to him — not as an expert would read it, but he had begun to locate consistent features, recurring shapes, to orient himself within it.

There was one arrangement he kept returning to.

A burning point at the center — unmistakably a star, the kind that things moved around — and around it: several bodies in various orbits. Most were unremarkable in the way of background detail. Two or three were larger, closer to the center, with markings near them that meant nothing he could determine. But one — small, blue-green, third from the star — had more markings around it than the others. More notation. And, on this most recent visit, something he had not noticed before or which had not been visible before: four small shapes in tight orbit around it. Small. Regular. Arranged at different distances.

He counted them several times. Four.

He had grown up, as everyone had grown up, with three moons. The three Witnesses. *The Three Witnesses remain above us still. They do not leave. They have never left.* He had spoken those words at the start of every Blooming ceremony for thirty years, and they had always sounded like the bedrock kind of truth — the kind you don't argue with because it isn't making a claim, it is simply describing the world as it is. Their cycles were in every calendar, every tidal table, every agricultural guide, every set of planting instructions distributed by every temple on Thera. Three was not a question. Three was the number moons were.

He counted the shapes on the glass again. His lips moved.

Four.

He sat back on his heels and looked at the blue-green shape for a long time. The cold worked through his knees. He did not move.

He was a careful man. Careful his entire adult life, with a robust appreciation for the ways in which the human mind found patterns when it very much wanted patterns to be there. He held all of this in one hand.

In the other hand: the blue-green shape. The symbol in the doorway lintel that described an orbit. The dying woman's grip on his wrist, her silver eyes, the apologetic quality of her look. The note in the archive: *Restricted Collection. Bloodline approval required.*

He had not drawn the conclusion. He was aware of having declined to draw it, actively, for three weeks. Sitting on the cold stone floor of a sealed room beneath a temple, he understood that declining to draw a conclusion was not the same as the conclusion not being there.

He got up. His knees protested in the way they had begun to protest all floor-level activities since he'd passed fifty, and he ignored this in the way he ignored all indications that his body had opinions about what he asked of it. He looked at the glass for another long moment. The four small shapes around the blue-green world. The one closest — the one that would be largest in the sky — had a quality he had not noticed before, or was only now in a position to notice: it was different from the others. Less regular in shape, as though something had happened to it.

He pinched the lamp out without thinking, which plunged the room into the cold faint illumination of the glass alone, and in that light he looked at the one irregular shape and thought: *damaged*.

Then he thought about what would have to happen to a moon to make it irregular.

He stood in the dark for a long time with the light of the glass on his face, and then he went upstairs and sat at his desk and wrote to Brennan, his old friend from seminary, now a scholar in Greenford. He had not been composing this one. It arrived in twenty minutes, unplanned — driven by the cold stone floor and the four small shapes and the thing he could no longer pretend not to see.

He did not write: *I think I know something terrible and wonderful and I need someone to tell me I'm wrong.* He wrote the scholarly equivalent, which was longer and meant the same thing. It was, he noticed sealing it, considerably less oblique than the careful letter already in the outgoing post.

He sealed it. He looked at it.

Then he did something he had not done in thirty years of correspondence: he checked the seal twice, pressing it with his thumb long after it had set, making sure of something already sure.

He put it with the outgoing post.

---

He was on his way back from the undercroft two nights later when he heard footsteps in the eastern corridor.

He recognized the quality of them before he recognized the source. Not a junior brother — the step was too considered, moving with the quiet purposefulness of someone navigating a space they were still learning. He was at the top of the undercroft stairs with the door not yet shut behind him, and the footsteps were coming from the direction of the archive room, and they were heading toward him.

He shut the door quietly. He had perhaps fifteen seconds.

He moved to the record crates stacked against the eastern wall — a genuine storage project he'd been meaning to address since autumn — and lifted the lid of the nearest one as Prioress Crane came around the corridor's turn.

She stopped.

He looked up from the crate as if mildly surprised. He kept his hand inside the crate, resting on a stack of papers, because removing it abruptly would be wrong. "Prioress," he said. "I didn't hear you."

"I couldn't sleep," she said. "I was reviewing the acquisition ledgers." She looked at the crates, at him, at the corridor with its single lamp. "I didn't expect company."

"The pre-winter inventory," he said. "There's always more of it than I plan for."

She looked at the crate, the undercroft door behind him — closed, unremarkable, the kind of door that blended into a corridor of doors. Then at him again.

"I was about to head down," he said, and he heard the fraction of a second too late that *down* was the wrong word, and moved past it without pausing. "The lower storage. Some of the climate records from the Brace period need re-housing. The moisture down there this autumn has been a concern." He replaced the crate lid. "I won't keep you if you're working."

"No," she said. She had not moved. "I was just finishing." She looked at the corridor one more time — not at him, at the space — and then she smiled. "The eastern wing has interesting architecture, doesn't it. The foundations look older than the rest of the building."

The sentence landed exactly as it was meant to.

"The temple has been built and rebuilt over several centuries," he said. "The foundations in this section do predate the current structure. If you're interested in the building history, I believe there's a survey in the archive from the last major renovation — the Keeper before me commissioned it."

"How thoughtful of you," she said. "I'll look for it." She turned with the unhurried manner of someone who has learned what she came to learn. "Good night, Keeper Voss."

"Good night, Prioress."

He waited until her footsteps had gone fully out of range before he allowed himself to move. He set the crate lid back in place with unnecessary care. He stood in the corridor for a moment with the lamp's thin light around him.

She already knew the foundations didn't match. She had been down this corridor before, possibly more than once, at hours when she expected to find it empty. The building history survey in the archive would not contain what she was looking for — he had already checked, three weeks ago — but she would look, and the looking would confirm what she suspected, and she was a patient woman who had not become Prioress by moving before she had what she needed.

She had not asked him directly. She was waiting for him to make a mistake, or to decide to trust her, and she appeared to be comfortable waiting for either.

He went to bed and lay awake until the early hours, when he heard, from the far side of the temple, the sound of the archive room door closing. Quiet, unhurried. Crane, working late. Or arriving early. Or neither — a door closing told you only that a door had closed, and he was becoming too alert to gaps in information to assume the rest.

He lay in the dark and pressed his thumb and forefinger together and found nothing to pinch and released them and looked at the ceiling.

Thirty years ago, when he was young and full of the dream of doing something historically significant, he could not have told you what he meant by that.

He was beginning to think the answer had not been waiting for him to want it.

He listened to the temple settle around him. Stone and timber, centuries of accumulated decision, the weight of a place where a great many people had come to find something they needed and had sometimes found it. He had served this place faithfully. He still believed in the service. He was less certain, with each passing night, what he was serving it toward.

In the morning he was in the corridor before the dawn bell and the lamp outside the record room was cold and he pinched it anyway, thumb and forefinger, the familiar nothing of it, and walked on toward the chapel to say the words he had been saying for thirty years and meant and was not sure anymore in what direction.


## Chapter Eight — *[Vane]*
### Trail

---

She was twelve days behind when she left New Plymouth, farther back than she liked.

The delay had been unavoidable — two days sourcing the right road information, one day acquiring gear for an extended northern route, another half-day dealing with the paperwork that a legitimate skip-retrieval earlier in the week had generated, because the docks district constabulary had an appetite for documentation bearing no relationship to the actual complexity of the work involved. She had done all of this with efficient irritation and then set out north on a clear morning with her pack and her maps and the working assumption that a seventeen-year-old girl on foot, rationing money, sleeping rough, was not moving as fast as she looked.

She moved fast. The journey from New Plymouth to the area around Stillwater was four or five days of hard travel at her pace. Twelve days behind at departure meant the gap was closeable — it had to be, or the job was already done poorly before it had started.

The first three days produced nothing — no trace at waypoint inns, no reliable witness accounts, the usual noise of rumor that accumulated around any active search and had to be filtered for signal. She filtered methodically. The bloodline's agents were ahead of her in some places and behind her in others, casting wide rather than following a specific trail. They didn't have one. The girl was not predictable. She was making good decisions about when to be visible and when not to be.

On the fourth day, at a crossroads village south of Stillwater, she found the kitchen.

---

The tavern-keeper confirmed a dark-haired girl had worked the kitchen for three days, left abruptly, owed nothing and was owed a half-day's wages she hadn't collected. The last detail was interesting. People who left wages behind were either frightened into moving fast or principled about not taking what they hadn't fully earned. Both possibilities said something about the girl.

Vane stood in the kitchen doorway for a moment before she went in.

The kitchen was currently occupied by a different girl doing the morning prep — chopping root vegetables with the mechanical rhythm of someone who had done this particular task approximately ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more. She looked up when Vane appeared and then looked back at her chopping with the practiced incuriosity of someone who had learned that looking at strangers too long in a tavern kitchen led to conversations she hadn't chosen.

Vane was not interested in her. She was interested in the air.

Three days since the girl had been here — long enough that most of it had faded into the general layered smell of a working kitchen, fat and smoke and old wood and the sharp green note of herbs. But there were traces if you knew what to look for, and she knew what to look for. She found nothing wrong. Nothing frightened. The emotional residue left in a space by someone who'd spent time in it was, in her experience, a reliable record even after the person was gone. Fear left a specific cold-metal signature that clung to surfaces for days, and someone managing sustained terror while performing normal work left a distinctive compound of suppressed adrenaline and enforced calm she'd learned to identify years ago.

The kitchen smelled of someone who had been alert. Watchful. Not frightened.

She stood in the doorway for another moment and revised her working model of the girl.

Then she went out the window. Not because she needed to — she could have used the door — but because she wanted to see what the drop looked like. About a body's height to the ground, onto flagstones, awkward in the dark. She could smell the stone below, the faint iron trace of an old landing, recent enough to still carry something. A stumble on the left side. Recovered quickly. She noted the stumble and went around to the yard.

---

The road north of the village had a section where the agents had stopped.

She found it an hour later — boot prints, the weight of horses standing for a period, the residue of a conversation with someone on foot. A farmer, from the second set of prints. She looked at where he'd been standing when he'd talked to them, and the direction his feet had pointed, and then walked fifty yards down the road to where a slight embankment ran alongside a drainage ditch, heavy with the dead scrub of late autumn.

She crouched at the embankment and looked at it.

The disturbed area was small — a narrow gap where someone had pushed through quickly, the branches scratched at approximately the height of someone crouching, the inner bank scuffed where a boot had slipped and caught. She stayed crouching there for a moment.

No fear-smell. The residue was hours old and faint, but she could work with faint. Alert. Watchful. The specific compound of a person holding very still while something dangerous moved nearby — heightened attention, suppressed breath, the low physiological register of someone who had decided to make themselves small and was doing it well. And underneath all of that, something she'd noticed first in the kitchen and hadn't known what to do with yet.

Ordinary. Simply, completely ordinary. No trace of the thing she'd learned to detect in infected people — a particular baseline warmth, present even in the non-mutated and the barely-changed, that came from whatever the Chrysalis did to the body's basic processes.

It wasn't there. The girl smelled like a person and nothing else, and Vane had been tracking people long enough that she only now realized she'd been filtering for that baseline warmth unconsciously, using it as part of her overall read on a subject. Now that it wasn't there the absence was something her nose kept returning to with the confused insistence of a tongue finding the gap where a tooth used to be.

She stood up and walked north.

---

She found the farmstead by the boy.

He was perhaps ten, coming back up the road with an empty cart and the slightly proprietary air of a child who has completed something he'd been trusted with and knows it. She smelled him before she reached him: woodsmoke, turnip earth, and something else — a warm particular note she'd learned over years to read as protectiveness. Recent. Directed at someone specific rather than general.

She slowed her pace and let him come to her.

He assessed her with the frank efficiency of a child who spent time alone on roads and had developed views about strangers. She let him look. She kept her hands visible and her posture without urgency, and she watched his face while he decided, and her nose confirmed what his face said: cautious, not closed. He'd made a distinction between her and whatever had worried him previously. She couldn't have said what put her in the better category — perhaps simply that she wasn't on a horse, wasn't asking questions before he'd had a chance to assess her. She put him in the middle category and walked on.

She asked about the road conditions north. He answered. They talked about the weather, briefly, in the way of two people who have established they are not a threat to each other and are now deciding what else they might be.

She bought a turnip from him. He named a price that was ambitious and she paid it without negotiating, which landed with him as a kind of respect rather than naivety. The protectiveness-scent hadn't diminished but it hadn't sharpened either.

She asked, as she put the turnip in her pack, whether there was somewhere north she might find a meal and a barn for the night.

He thought about it. The scent shifted slightly — a small complicated calculation being made. He mentioned a farmstead, his family's, his mother wouldn't mind. He described the road without quite meeting her eyes.

He didn't mention the girl. She hadn't expected him to. She noted the shape of what he'd left out and walked on.

---

The farm wife's name was Betta. She was perhaps late forties, compact and capable, with hands that spoke to a lifetime of physical work and eyes that had decided something about Vane within approximately three seconds of the door opening and were not revising it.

Vane smelled her immediately: clean work-sweat and lanolin from sheep and something that sat unresolved, somewhere between the throat and the chest — a decision recently made and not yet settled into. Not fear. Something more deliberate than fear. The smell of a position being held.

"I'm looking for a young woman," Vane said. "Dark hair. She may have passed through a few days ago."

"A lot of people pass through," Betta said.

"This one would have been traveling alone. On foot."

"People do that also."

She was not going to be helpful. Vane had understood this before the first sentence — confirmed now by what she was breathing. She stepped back from the door slightly and waited.

Betta did not fill the silence. The mark of someone who had understood that talking was usually the problem rather than the solution. She held it longer than most. Vane gave her credit for this.

She let the silence run for longer than was comfortable. She monitored the scent — no spike of fear, no anxious acceleration. Steady. The held position.

Eventually Vane said: "I'm not with the bloodline."

Something shifted in Betta's expression. Not much. A recalibration.

Vane said: "She was here. She helped your boy with his cart. She slept in the barn. She left before dawn." A pause. "I'm not going to bring her back to anyone. I want to know which way she went."

What she didn't say: she'd already read the answer in the kitchen and the ditch embankment and the boy's careful omission. She knew the direction. What she was doing now was something slightly different — establishing whether Betta had a reason to lie that would indicate a reason to worry.

Betta looked at her for a long moment. The scent held steady. The decision already made, just waiting to be spoken.

"North and east," Betta said finally. "She was heading for the road."

"Did she say anything? About where she was going?"

"No."

"Did she seem to know?"

Betta's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "She seemed like she'd decided north was enough."

Vane nodded. She put two coins on the doorstep — not so many as to be an insult, not so few as to be meaningless. Betta looked at them and didn't move to take them.

"She didn't take anything that wasn't hers," Betta said. "In case that's something you need to know."

It was. "Thank you," Vane said.

She went north.

---

She was an hour past the farmstead when the world opened a register wider than she'd asked for.

Not the full experience, nothing so organized — just the leading edge of it, the walls going briefly generous. She stopped walking because walking while this happened was inadvisable. She stood in the road and waited for it to pass and while it passed she smelled, with perfect clarity, something that did not belong to this road or this countryside or this cold afternoon.

Copper and something like flowers, neither real, both insistent.

Someone else's smell. The Bloom-giver's, she understood — the psychic contamination she'd been told about when she first started using, which she'd dismissed at the time as something that happened to people who were less careful than her. She was apparently not less careful than those people. She was just further along the same road.

With the smell came a fragment of sensation: a ceiling. Low, stone, air that had not moved in a long time. The weight of feeling thoroughly and irrevocably stationary, different from simply being still.

Then it was gone.

She stood in the road until she was confident the ground was the ground and the sky was the sky. This took a moment longer than it should have.

She took the wafer from her coat pocket. She'd taken to carrying one loose rather than going into her pack for the tin — a habit that had developed without her noticing it, the way the wafer in the pocket had preceded the tin, and the tin had preceded the question of whether she needed it. She held it between her fingers. The faint floral smell reached her. Almost nothing. The familiar absence that was its own kind of statement.

She thought about what she kept finding in the girl's traces: that same quality of almost nothing. No baseline warmth, no Chrysalis signature, just a person moving through the world and leaving the ordinary record of a person. An absence her nose couldn't stop returning to.

Two absences. One in her pocket. One she was following north. She noted the parallel and put the wafer away without using it.

She made a note in her shorthand when she stopped for water — three words and a symbol, the kind of entry that would smell, when she read it later, of this road and this afternoon and a thought she hadn't finished having.

---

Toward evening she came over a rise and saw two of the bloodline's agents on the road below her, heading south at a pace that said destination, not search. They'd been working this section and found nothing. Whatever Betta had decided to tell them would have been less than what she'd told Vane, or nothing at all. She waited on the rise until they were gone, then came down to the road.

She found a woodcutter's shelter at full dark — old, unused for the season, sound enough to keep the wind out. She ate from her pack and checked her maps by lamplight. Two days behind, probably less. The girl's gait had been uneven since the ditch — favoring one side on rough ground, not badly but consistently. It would cost her on anything uneven.

She lay on her back in the shelter and looked at the dark ceiling.

She had a job. The job had its own momentum, one of the things she valued about jobs. Simple machines: input, process, output. You located the target and you delivered the target and you collected the fee and the job closed and you moved to the next one. The mechanism was clean. She had run it for ten years without serious difficulty.

The difficulty she was having now she identified carefully, because she was a professional and professionals identified their difficulties: a working assumption had turned out to be wrong, and she hadn't yet replaced it with a better one. She'd assumed the girl would smell like someone in trouble. People in trouble smelled like trouble. The girl smelled like someone making decisions, and the decisions were, by the evidence, mostly correct.

She thought about the Noble Quarter address. The size of the fee. The instruction: *do not harm.* Which implied harm was a concern, which implied whoever was waiting at that address understood what they were asking for and had included the caveat deliberately.

She thought about what waited at a Noble Quarter address for a girl with no change.

The Bloom-giver's ceiling rose briefly in her memory, stone and low and still. She pressed it back down.

She put her hand in her coat pocket and felt the wafer's small weight and took her hand away from it and lay still.

At her current pace she'd reach the road north of the girl's likely position by morning. The ankle she'd read in the ditch-embankment scruff would slow the girl on anything rough. Vane had already pulled ahead; she knew it by the quality of the traces, the way the evidence kept arriving fresher without closing on the source. She was ahead. She was ahead and she had not decided what to do with that yet.

She was not sure in which direction the job's momentum was supposed to carry her.

She would figure it out in the morning.

She generally did.

---

## Chapter Nine — *[Maren]*
### The Hollow

---

The ankle announced itself two hours north of Betta's farmstead.

Not dramatically. A wrong step on a stone hidden under leaf-duff, a small lateral give that shouldn't have given, and then the specific hot ache of something that had been borderline for days and had now arrived at a decision. She stopped and stood on one foot and breathed through her nose and waited for the ache to report on its own severity.

Moderate. Not a break — she'd seen breaks, set two of them under Delia's instruction, and this didn't have that quality. A sprain, probably the same joint she'd rolled two nights back getting out a tavern window in the dark — three days of kitchen work in exchange for a bed, a good arrangement until the barman started asking questions she didn't like and she'd left through the nearest exit that wasn't the front door. She'd walked hard on it since without giving it the consideration it deserved. She could continue. She would continue more carefully.

She cut a section of her extra shirt-linen and wrapped the joint tightly, which helped, and found a stick at the right height and density for a walking staff, which helped more. Then she did the mathematics of her situation, which she had been avoiding and could no longer reasonably avoid.

Food: half a portion of dried herbs that weren't particularly edible, one withered apple Betta had pressed on her at the door, and a turnip from Corvin's cart that she'd tried to pay for and he'd refused, pressing the coin back into her hand with the brief finality of someone who has already decided. Her hands had been doing things lately without waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

Money: not enough for an inn, probably enough for one more meal if she found somewhere to buy one.

The ankle: now a factor.

She looked at the road below her, the one she'd been paralleling from a safe distance, and did the final calculation: she needed to cover ground faster than she could currently cover it off-road. That meant using the road. That meant being visible. That meant trusting that she looked like what she was — a road-worn girl with a walking stick and legitimate reasons to be going somewhere, a description that applied to a large number of people on any road at any time.

She came down to the road.

---

She covered four hours before the ankle became something she could not manage with grim persistence and a walking stick. Not suddenly — it wound down gradually, the hot ache climbing from the joint to the whole lower leg, the staff increasingly load-bearing rather than supplementary. She was listing slightly. She caught herself listing and corrected and caught herself again ten minutes later.

The woodcutter's shelter appeared on the left side of the road as the light was going — an old thing, low-roofed, the door hanging off one hinge and wedged open with a stone. She went in. It smelled of old wood and mouse and the cold specific smell of a space that had not had a fire in it for several months. A rusted hinge on a broken crate. A pile of split wood in the corner, dry despite the season because the roof, at least, was sound.

She sat on the crate and unwrapped the ankle and looked at it.

Swollen. More than she'd thought, because she'd been not-looking with some intentionality. The joint was puffy and the bruising had started, not dramatic but present, a yellowish spreading at the edges that would be properly purple by tomorrow. She wrapped it again, tighter this time, and elevated it on the crate's corner, which helped with the throbbing but left her in an awkward position she'd have to sleep in.

She ate the turnip. She saved the apple.

She built a fire in the shelter's small hearth, which required some creative arrangement of the broken hinge as a spark surface and resulted in two burned fingers before the tinder caught, and she sat in the fire's small circle of warmth and thought about what she was going to do.

Immediately: nothing. The ankle needed at least a day, preferably two. Moving on it tonight or tomorrow morning would be the kind of decision that turned a moderate injury into a genuine one, and a genuine one into a problem that resolved badly. She had seen this play out with farmhands in Ashwick, the ones who kept working through the first injury and wound up laid up for a month. She was not going to make that mistake because she was in a hurry.

She was in a hurry.

She stayed.

---

The first day was fine, or at least manageable. She slept heavily, which she hadn't done in two weeks, and woke with the ankle complaining less than expected, and spent the day in reasonable occupation: tending the fire, finishing the herbs she couldn't eat by making a poultice for the joint, taking stock of the shelter's limited resources, and doing the slow methodical work of cleaning and sharpening her knife with a flat stone she found by the door.

She did not think about anything in particular. This was the skill again, the one she'd been working on for three years. She deployed it.

She ate the apple in the afternoon, in small sections, making it last. It was floury and slightly fermented, past its best by some weeks, and it was the best thing she'd tasted since Betta's soup.

She slept again before dark. Her body had opinions about what it needed and she was temporarily in a position where she had to listen to them.

The second day was harder.

---

The food was gone. The ankle was better — she tested it in the morning and the verdict was: walkable but not comfortably, not off-road, probably fine on even ground if she was sensible about it. The firewood was running low. The shelter was cold in the way of a space that had absorbed the cold of several months and was not going to warm thoroughly for any short-term occupancy. She was hungry in a persistent and low-level way.

Not objectively dire. She had been hungry before. She could go three or four days without food before it became a physical problem rather than a discomfort problem, and she was at discomfort. The road was an hour's walk. There was almost certainly a waypoint inn within half a day's travel. She had enough coin for a meal.

She knew all of this and it didn't particularly help, because the hunger was not the problem. The hunger was just the thing that took up the space where her defenses normally lived, and with the defenses down, other things moved in.

She sat by the dying fire and let them.

---

She thought about her mother.

Not the death — she'd thought about that enough, it had its shape, she knew all the angles of it. The other parts. Her mother braiding her hair with the particular impersonal efficiency of someone who had a task to complete and was completing it, not unkind but not tender either, just thorough. The smell of her: woodsmoke and the specific clean soap she made herself from ash and rendered fat and a handful of dried lavender from the kitchen garden. The way she'd said Maren's name when she was displeased — three syllables, clipped — and the way she'd said it when she wasn't, the same three syllables landing completely differently.

Her mother had known.

She had known about the bandage, the pouch, the performance. She must have. She was not a stupid woman and she had watched Maren closely in the years after her husband left, with the particular close watching of someone monitoring a situation they had already assessed and decided they could not change. She had never said anything directly. She had let the deception stand and had not helped with it and had not undermined it and had died without telling Maren what she knew.

Maren had always resented this as a kind of abandonment. Sitting with it now, in a cold shelter with a bad ankle and no food, she found it felt different. Not a choice to abandon. A choice not to add weight to something already heavy. Her mother had looked at the situation and decided that she could not make it easier and could certainly make it harder and had elected to do neither. This was, in its way, a thing that required a certain amount of love.

She let this sit for a while.

Then she thought about her father, which was where things got complicated.

She had the revision going now, the one that had started at the boundary stone. Not the man who left but the man who ran. Same action, different story. He had been what she was — bloodless, hollow, the thing without the gift. He had been managing this the way she'd been managing it, the careful arm, the performance, the watching of the deacon's eyes to see when they were paying the wrong kind of attention. He had been doing this longer than she had. He had known what it meant when the attention arrived, and he had gotten out in the night without waking her, which was the choice of a man who had very little time and had used what he had to make sure she didn't know he was gone until it was too late to follow.

She thought: he left me.

She thought: he ran so they'd follow him and not stay to look for me.

Both true. She sat with both of them.

The fire went to embers. She didn't have wood left to build it back.

---

In the afternoon she found herself doing something she hadn't consciously decided to do: she took the disc from her pocket and sat with it in the gray light from the door and looked at it with the focused attention she'd been applying to everything lately, the attention that had recently started producing actual information.

The markings she'd always taken for decoration. Three of them around the edge, and now that she was looking at them rather than past them, they were not decorative in character — too regular, too precisely placed, the lines too clean for ornament. The central indentation she'd taken for damage was not a dent but a recess, shaped too deliberately for anything accidental. It was designed to receive something. A key, maybe. A finger. Something specific.

She pressed her thumb into it.

Nothing happened.

She tried each finger in turn, which felt slightly ridiculous. Nothing happened to any of them.

She turned the disc over and looked at the smooth side. Nothing there. She tilted it toward the light and the metal did something she hadn't noticed before — a faint iridescence at certain angles, not random, a pattern that appeared and disappeared as she moved it. Not decoration. Structure, something in the metal itself.

She didn't know what any of this meant. She knew how to identify plants and dress wounds and set a bone and make a fire and walk very long distances without complaining. She did not know how to read a metal disc that had been in her family for generations with no explanation except *don't lose this, don't show anyone.*

Her father had known how to fix small precise things. He'd had patience for mechanisms, for the particular coaxing-into-place that small broken things required. If this disc was a mechanism, he would have understood it better than she did.

He wasn't here.

She put the disc back in her pocket. She turned over Corvin's whittled bird in her other hand — he'd pressed it into her palm the same moment he'd refused her coin, and she still wasn't sure if he'd done it deliberately or if it had simply migrated in the transaction. The carving was a small thing, a bird with its wings tucked, smooth where his hands had worked the wood down to the essential shape. She'd been carrying it because it had a quality of intention to it she found, obscurely, reassuring.

She put it away also and sat with the cold and the hunger and the feeling she was starting to recognize as the specific weight of a question she didn't have the tools to answer yet. She'd been carrying it since childhood without knowing that's what it was. Now she knew, and knowing didn't help, but it was better than not knowing. She found she believed this genuinely rather than as a thing she'd decided to believe because the alternative was worse.

The light shifted. Outside the road was quiet. No horses, no voices, no indication that anyone was looking through this particular stretch of country. She could not know if this was safety or the absence of evidence.

She stood up and tested the ankle. Better. Not good, but better.

She thought: I have been calling myself hollow since I was fourteen, and hollow is a word that means empty, and I don't feel empty. I feel like a person who is cold and hungry and has a bad ankle and does not know what the disc is and cannot answer the most important questions about her own life, and all of that is very full. Hollow is wrong.

The old eastern songs — the Irongate kind, the ones the traveling singers brought through Ashwick every few years — had a different word. *Unblooded.* And the songs said the Unblooded could touch things the changed ones couldn't. The old things. The god's old bones and weapons, the songs said, though they said it casually, the way songs said everything, as if the information were decoration rather than the load-bearing wall it might actually be. She thought about the disc in her pocket — cold metal that had never burned her, that had sat in her father's bloodless hands and his father's before that, patient and inert and *waiting for the right fingers.* The Recitation called her kind servants. The songs called them keepers of old things. The deacon had called her bloodless and the bloodline had asked to be told.

Three words for the same absence, and each one told a different story about what the absence meant, and she was beginning to suspect that the variety was not an accident.

Whoever decided she was hollow was wrong, or lying, or both. And their being wrong or lying about that meant she should be considerably more skeptical about whatever else they'd decided about her.

She wanted to know who decided it and why.

Not a new thought. She'd had it at the boundary stone. What was new was that it was the only thought left, now that the food was gone and the fire was out and the defenses she maintained without knowing she maintained them had been worn down to something more fundamental. Under the competence and the anger and the three years of careful performance: this. Just this. A question that had been waiting for her to be quiet enough to hear it.

She put on her coat and her pack and picked up her walking stick and went to the door.

The road was empty in both directions. The afternoon light was pale and flat, making everything look equally far away. She stepped out and tested the ankle on even ground — manageable — and turned north, because north was still the only direction she had, and a question required movement toward an answer.

She walked.

The road was even and the ankle held and the light lasted longer than she'd expected, the sky doing the thing it did in open country where the sunset lingered in the west long after it had any business being there. She watched it go from pale gold to a flat gray-orange to nothing, and kept her eyes on the road, and did not think about the disc or her father or the word hollow or any of it — just the road, and the next step, and the one after that.

The question was still there. It would be there in the morning.

It had waited this long.

## Chapter Ten — *[Maren / Vane]*
### The Wrong House

---

She almost walked past her.

The woman was off the road's edge, in the ditch-grass where the ground dropped slightly before leveling into a field, and in the gray morning light she was easy to mistake for something fallen from a cart — a bundle, a rolled canvas, something inert. Maren was two steps past before the something inert made a sound.

She stopped.

She turned back.

The woman was perhaps mid-thirties, well-made gear, a pack that spoke to someone who traveled regularly and knew what to bring. She was on her side in the grass with her knees drawn up and her hands pressed flat against the ground in the way of someone trying to determine whether the ground was still doing what ground was supposed to do. Her eyes were open. She was breathing fast and shallow.

Maren stood at the road's edge and looked at her. The woman had been moving faster — that much was plain from the gear and the posture of someone whose body had been pushed hard. She'd been ahead. Maren had seen no one pass her in the night, which meant this person had gotten out in front some time ago and had simply been here when Maren arrived. She looked up and down the road, which was empty in both directions, and looked back at the woman.

"What's wrong with you," she said. Not unkindly. Just the first question.

The woman didn't answer. Her hands pressed harder against the ground. Her jaw was tight and her eyes were tracking something Maren couldn't see — not the sky, not the field, something with no location in the physical world. Her bark-patched forearms were rigid, and the bark itself looked wrong — darker than it should have been, the edges raised and angry where they met skin, as if whatever lived in it had been pulling and the thing that usually held it in check had stopped holding. A fine tremor ran through her shoulders in waves, not shivering, something deeper, the body arguing with itself at a level below the voluntary.

She smelled of copper and something faintly floral that wasn't quite any flower Maren knew. Underneath that: sweat, and the sour-flat chemical note of a body that had been expecting something it hadn't received.

Maren came down off the road.

---

She'd seen this before, or something like it. Not Bloom — she hadn't known what Bloom was two weeks ago, wouldn't have known the name if she'd heard it. But she'd seen the dissociated states that the most extreme mutation-expressions could produce in people, the ones where the change pushed into territory the mind struggled to stay present through. Delia had treated two people in states like this over the years, and what she'd done was specific and practical: reduce the sensory noise, give the body something real to hold onto, wait.

She set her pack down. She sat in the grass beside the woman, close but not touching, and spoke in the low even tone Delia had used — not soothing exactly, more like a rope thrown into water. Something with a voice at one end and something real at the other.

"You're in a field," she said. "North road, about a half-day south of Stillwater. It's morning. The ground is solid."

The woman's breathing didn't slow.

"You're in a field," Maren said again. "The ground is solid. You can feel it."

A long moment. Then, incrementally, some of the rigidity went out of the woman's hands. Not all of it. Enough.

Maren reached into her pack and found Corvin's whittled bird — the small carved thing he'd pressed into her palm the same moment he'd refused her coin, a bird with its wings tucked close, the knife marks still rough at the wing joints, the body warm from her pocket. She pressed it into the woman's hand.

The woman's fingers closed around it slowly, then with more purpose. She pressed her thumb along the carved surface the way you'd test a thing's realness. Her breathing began to slow.

Maren sat in the grass and kept talking — the field, the road, the morning, the solid ground — until the woman's eyes focused and came back to the world and found her.

---

They looked at each other.

Maren did what she'd learned to do when she didn't know what she was looking at: kept her face neutral and gathered information.

The woman was collecting herself in the way of someone who had done this before and had a procedure for it. Methodical. She pressed both palms to the ground, sat up, tested her own steadiness before she tried speaking. Her eyes were alert and slightly too bright, the look of someone whose body was still running even though the rest of her had stopped.

She looked at Maren.

She looked at Maren for a specific moment, a beat longer than the first look, and something moved across her face — quickly managed and then gone. Recognition, or something that lived in the same neighborhood.

Maren felt the back of her neck prickle.

"Thank you," the woman said. Her voice was level. You would not have known, from the voice, that she'd been on her hands and knees in ditch-grass thirty seconds ago. But her hands were still shaking — a fine persistent tremor she was controlling at the wrist but not at the fingertips — and the bark patches on her forearms had not settled back to the color they'd been before. Whatever had been happening to her was not fully over. It had merely become something she could manage standing up.

"What happened?" Maren asked.

A pause. The briefest possible pause, the kind that was itself an answer. "Episodes," the woman said. "I get them sometimes. They pass."

She was lying. Not about the episodes — those were evidently real. About the *sometimes*, and about the *pass*. Maren had watched Delia treat people whose bodies were fighting their own changes, and this looked the same — a body negotiating with itself, the terms recently shifted and the settlement not yet reached. She noted this without comment.

The woman looked down at her hand and saw the whittled bird. She turned it over once. Her fingers closed around it again, and she held it, and her breathing steadied the rest of the way down.

She stood up cleanly, without requesting help or signaling that she needed it. She picked up her pack and brushed the grass from her coat with the brisk efficiency of someone closing a topic.

Maren stood also.

They were on the road's edge, two feet apart, and the morning was quiet around them, and the prickling at the back of Maren's neck had not gone away.

"You're going north," the woman said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"Stillwater?"

"Past it."

The woman nodded once. She had the quality, Maren thought, of someone very good at waiting — not patient exactly, more like a mechanism coiled and still and capable of ceasing to be still at any moment it chose. She also smelled things. Maren couldn't have explained how she'd arrived at this conclusion. Something in the way the woman's attention moved, something that was slightly ahead of where her eyes were pointed.

"You're the one they're looking for," the woman said.

Maren's hand moved toward her knife and stopped. She made it stop. The woman had not moved, had not reached for anything, was standing with her hands visible and her posture without aggression. She was simply saying a thing that was true.

"Yes," Maren said, because denying it was wasted effort and she was tired of wasted effort.

The woman looked at her with that compound attention — not quite the eyes, not quite the nose, something compound.

"You could have walked past me," the woman said.

"I could have."

"Most people would have."

Maren said nothing. This was true and did not require her response.

"I've been following you for nine days," the woman said. "You're better at this than you should be."

"You're the one who ended up in a ditch."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment. "Yes," she said. "I am."

A cart appeared on the road to the south, unhurried, a farmer and a load of timber. They both watched it come and pass without either of them moving or speaking. The farmer glanced at them — two women standing at the road's edge in the morning, nothing alarming — and moved on.

When the cart had rounded the bend the woman said: "My name is Vane. I was contracted to find you and deliver you to a private address in the Noble Quarter."

Maren looked at her steadily. "Was."

"The contract is still open." A pause. "I'm telling you what I know."

"Why?"

Vane looked at her. "Because you sat in a ditch with a stranger and talked her back into herself, and most people wouldn't have done that, and I don't work well with incorrect information about the people I'm dealing with." She paused. "And because the Noble Quarter is not where you go when the bloodline wants someone questioned."

The prickling at Maren's neck spread.

"Then where is it where you go?" she said, though she thought she knew.

Vane said nothing. She didn't need to.

---

They stood in the road for a moment that lasted long enough to be a kind of conversation.

Maren thought about the three riders. The farmhouse door at night. Delia crying through a wall. Her father, gone before dawn, never coming back. She thought about the Noble Quarter address, private, a door that didn't open back out.

"How many are ahead of me?" she said.

"I counted three on the road south of here. There will be more in the city. The contract came through a handler who works for the bloodline's domestic office." Vane's voice gave her information the way a person hands over something they've decided to hand over — fully, without performance. "They've been searching wide. They don't have a trail."

"You do."

"I did." Vane looked at her with that compound attention. "You don't smell like what I expected."

Maren blinked. "What did you expect?"

"Something frightened." A pause. "You're not."

"I'm frightened," Maren said. "I'm just also angry."

The corner of Vane's mouth moved again. This time it made it to something that was, briefly, a smile. It changed her face considerably. Then it was gone.

"I'm going to walk north," Vane said. "I need to resupply in Stillwater. I'm going to think about some things." She held out Corvin's whittled bird.

Maren looked at it. "Keep it," she said.

Vane looked at the small carved bird in her own hand for a moment. She pocketed it without further comment.

"If you're heading for New Plymouth," Vane said, "don't use the east gate. The guards change at midday and the midday shift has a specific brief." She said it in the tone of someone providing operational information. Nothing warmer than that. "There's a river crossing two miles west of the east gate. No toll. The guards are interested in the gate traffic, not the ford."

She walked north without waiting for a response.

Maren stood in the road and watched her go.

She did not know what to make of this. She was becoming increasingly aware that there were categories of person she simply had not had enough experience with to read correctly — people for whom the professional and the personal were not separate territories but different expressions of the same thing, people whose kindness, if it was kindness, arrived in the form of tactical information and didn't announce itself as kindness at all.

She thought about what Delia had said once, about the best healers she'd known: that they weren't warm, exactly, but they were present in a way that meant the same thing in the end.

She walked north.

She kept Vane in her peripheral vision until the road curved and took her away, and she noticed she was doing this and kept doing it anyway, because peripheral vision was information and she was not going to stop gathering information now.

Ahead: Stillwater, and whatever was in it. New Plymouth beyond that, enormous and unknown, a city she'd never seen, the Noble Quarter with its private address and its door.

North was the safest direction. It was also, increasingly, the most complicated one.

She kept walking, and the morning opened up around her, and the moons — all three visible still, pale and fading in the growing light — moved in their slow and patient arcs above her, going nowhere in particular and doing it with great dedication.

She did not look up at them.

She had road to cover.

## Chapter Eleven — *[Vane]*
### Uncontracted

---

She walked north for an hour before she stopped walking.

Not because she'd reached anywhere. Because the decision she'd been treating as unmade was, she understood, already made, and continuing to walk away from it was simply theater she was performing for herself — the kind of inefficiency she found intolerable in other people and was apparently capable of herself.

She stood in the road.

She put her hand in her coat pocket and found the wafer and held it between her fingers without taking it out. She had four left in the tin. At her current rate — one every two and a half days, sometimes two — that was nine days, possibly ten. After that the episodes would come faster and stay longer and the bark would do what it did without the Bloom to hold it in check, which was spread, and the spreading was not the problem, the problem was what happened to her senses when the Bloom receded and the world went from choir to silence and the silence was so complete it felt like a kind of deafness. She had been through partial withdrawal once, two years ago, when Cade's supply had been interrupted for a week. She had spent the last three days of that week in her room with her hands pressed flat to the floor, unable to identify the smell of her own skin. She had not been functional. She had not been a person who could walk a road or find a girl or make a decision more complex than whether to breathe.

Nine days. Possibly ten.

The girl — Maren, she had a name now, Vane had found it in Horst's brief two weeks ago and had been not-using it with some deliberateness, because names were a kind of weight — was approximately a mile south. Walking north. Probably watching Vane's back until the road curved. Possibly already moving faster, taking the opportunity, because she was the kind of person who took opportunities.

Vane thought about the whittled bird in her coat pocket, next to the wafer.

Then she thought about the Noble Quarter address and the specific thing that happened at Noble Quarter addresses to people with no change. Then she thought about the Bloom-giver's ceiling, which she'd been thinking about at intervals for two weeks and had been filing each time and which was apparently unwilling to stay filed.

She had a contract. The contract was with Horst, who worked for the bloodline's domestic office, who wanted a girl delivered to a private address and had paid an advance she had already spent on gear and road provisions, which meant she owed either the work or a refund she didn't currently have. Completing the contract meant returning to New Plymouth. Returning to New Plymouth meant Cade's back room and a full tin and the withdrawal clock reset to zero. Walking away meant nine days, and then the floor, and then whatever came after the floor.

She'd walked away from contracts before. Not for this reason. Not with a countdown in her coat pocket.

She turned around and walked south.

---

She found Maren where she expected to — moving north at a solid pace, not running, not dawdling, the walk of someone who had made a practice of covering ground without wasting energy. She heard Vane coming and clocked her at thirty yards and did not stop walking, just adjusted her posture — not frightened, but remaining appropriately alert. The knife didn't come out. Vane noted this as another data point in the ongoing revision of her working model.

She fell into step beside her.

They walked together for a moment without speaking. Maren smelled of road and dried herbs and something angular that was not fear but was working hard in the same neighborhood.

"I'm going to Stillwater," Vane said. "I have a supply contact there."

"All right," Maren said.

"It's faster together. And safer."

"I know."

"You don't trust me."

"No," Maren said. "But I trust that you turned around, and I'm going the same direction, and the road has three of the bloodline's people on it between here and Stillwater." She paused. "So."

Vane considered this. It was, in its way, the most accurate possible assessment of their situation. She found she had no productive response to accuracy.

They walked.

---

She set the ankle at midday, when they stopped at a stream.

Maren had been managing it well — the right staff, the right weight distribution, not favoring it more than necessary — but she'd been managing it since yesterday's shelter and management was not the same as treatment. Vane had the supplies: a strip of proper support bandage, a small pot of drawing salve intended for her own use on the bark-patches but equally applicable to an inflamed joint.

Maren sat on a rock with her boot off and watched Vane work with the expression of someone who had not expected this and was deciding what to do with not having expected it. She said nothing, which Vane appreciated. People who talked while you were working their injuries generally made the work harder.

"You know what you're doing," Maren said, when she was done.

"I've had to be functional in rough situations. You learn."

"Delia taught me to set a bone," Maren said. "I've treated worse than this. I should have done it better myself two days ago."

It was the first time she'd mentioned Delia by name. Vane kept the name without commenting on it. The herb woman, presumably — she'd noted the smell of the kitchen, the specific knowledge in the grounding technique, the quality of Maren's herb selection in her pack. Someone had trained her carefully.

"She know you're running?" Vane asked.

Maren looked at the stream. "Yes."

"She tell them?"

A pause. "I don't know." Another pause. "I don't think so. I think she tried not to."

The distinction was careful and honest. Vane gave her credit for not defending the woman with more certainty than she had.

She shared her food — dried meat, hard biscuit, a small block of dense seed-cake that was not enjoyable but was caloric. Maren ate without excessive gratitude, which Vane found correct. Excessive gratitude for basic necessities was a form of performance, generally. You ate because you were hungry. You said thank you once and meant it and moved on.

"Why are you doing this?" Maren asked.

Vane had expected the question and had not prepared an answer for it, because she didn't have one she was satisfied with. "I told you. I don't work well with incorrect information."

"That's a reason to release a contract. It's not a reason to walk back."

This was also accurate. Vane looked at the stream for a moment.

"The address in the Noble Quarter," she said. "I looked into who owns the building. It's held through three separate property arrangements that nominally belong to different people." She paused. "They all trace back to the bloodline's domestic office."

"You already knew that."

"Knowing it from the contract and knowing it from the property records are different kinds of knowing." She paused. "There are other buildings held the same way. I've tracked people to two of them before. Those contracts closed. I collected the fee and moved on."

She stopped.

Maren was watching her. Not with the wariness she'd been wearing all morning — something more direct than that.

"What happened to those people?" Maren said.

Vane said nothing. She had an answer and it lived in the same category as the Bloom-giver's ceiling, the category of things she'd been filing for years that had run out of room.

"All right," Maren said, in the tone of someone who already knew the answer and had asked the question to see what would be done with it.

They packed up and walked north.

---

Stillwater was a lake town, modest and well-organized — a place that had been doing the same things for a long time and had developed a particular efficiency around those things. Fishing boats on the water. A processing district that smelled of it. A market, small but stocked, the goods oriented toward local need rather than trade. Vane's supply contact was a woman named Ori who ran a general goods house near the dock and asked no questions in exchange for a modest premium on her prices, an arrangement Vane had always found reasonable.

While Vane resupplied, Maren went looking for her uncle.

She came back an hour later. Vane didn't need to ask. She could smell it from ten feet — the flat specific quality of someone who has just learned something they already feared, and found that the fearing had not, in fact, prepared them.

"Two years ago," Maren said. "Fever."

"His wife?"

"Ulla. She's still at the house." She stopped. "She'd been visited. A month ago, someone came asking about me. She didn't tell them anything." A pause. "She said she didn't know anything to tell. She didn't know I'd be coming."

Vane thought about the timeline. A month ago meant the bloodline had been building the net before the deacon had even gone to Ashwick. Before Maren had run, before anyone could know she'd run north. They'd been triangulating potential refuges in advance.

She kept this thought to herself, because Maren was already working through the same calculation from the look on her face and arriving at the same place, and saying it aloud would not help anything.

"She gave me this," Maren said. She held up a coat — heavier than her current one, properly weatherproofed, sized close enough to work. "And this."

The second thing was a letter. Small, folded twice, the paper gone soft with age and handling, sealed with wax broken and re-pressed at some point by someone other than the original sender.

"From my uncle," Maren said. "Ulla said he'd kept it for years, told her if Maren or Maren's father ever came looking, she should pass it on." She looked at it. "It's been opened."

"By Ulla?"

"She said not. She said it arrived that way."

Vane thought about what that meant: someone had read the letter before it reached the uncle, or after. The bloodline's agents, possibly, on their triangulation visit. Or someone further back.

"Have you read it?" Vane said.

"Not yet."

"Do you want to be alone?"

Maren looked at her with a slightly startled quality, as if the question was not what she'd been expecting from this direction. Then she shook her head.

She unfolded the letter. Vane watched her face rather than the letter and read it from there — the small careful movements of someone going through something dense, line by line, with the particular attention of someone receiving more information than expected, in a different shape.

When she looked up her expression was one Vane had not seen on her before. Not frightened, not angry. Something more like someone revising a map in real time, the landmarks not where they'd been before but the terrain itself unchanged.

"My father came through Stillwater ten years ago," Maren said. "Heading east, traveling fast, didn't stop more than a day." She paused. "He said he was going to the hill. That there was something there he had to see before they caught him."

Vane was still.

"My father told my uncle that the disc would open it. Whatever it was." Maren folded the letter again, with some care. "He said it once and then he left and my uncle never saw him again."

The hill, Vane thought. East of the main road, in the foothills before Irongate country. The name Cade had mentioned, that the dockside old-timers spoke of in the particular tone that meant a name was a warning rather than a description. She'd filed it at the time the way she filed everything that didn't belong to an active contract. It belonged to one now.

She looked at Maren.

Maren was looking back at her with the expression of someone who has just received, from an unexpected direction, a piece of something they didn't know they'd been looking for, and has not yet decided what to do with it.

She had the disc in her hand. She'd taken it from her pocket without Vane noticing, was turning it over with the automatic gesture of someone who had carried a thing for years and reached for it in moments of significant thought. The metal caught the afternoon light flatly, without drama.

Vane looked at it. She thought about the Noble Quarter address, the bloodline's property arrangements, the two contracts she'd closed and not looked behind. She thought about her supply tin in her pack and the wafer inside it and the ceiling made of stone.

She thought about the boy who had whittled the small bird now in her coat pocket, and the girl who had been carrying it without knowing why, and the woman she'd been following north for nine days who smelled like a person doing something correctly even when she didn't know what she was doing.

The whittled bird was a practical thing — a reminder of Maren's steadiness in the ditch beside the road, the quick competence of her hands, the way she'd kept moving when moving was the only right answer. It was not mystical. It was evidence.

This was the moment where a professional made a clear assessment and acted on it.

She made one.

"The ford crossing I told you about," she said. "It comes out on the river road, two miles west of the New Plymouth east gate. From there the Temple District is accessible without passing through the main checkpoints." She paused. "And I know someone near the docks who arranges rooms and papers for people who need them. No questions asked."

Maren looked at her steadily. "You're coming to New Plymouth."

"I need to resupply," Vane said. "And I need to understand some things about the contract I'm walking away from before I decide what to do about it."

"That's not the reason."

"No," Vane said. "It isn't." She put the salve back in her pack and shouldered it. "The reason is that your father went east ten years ago to see something, and whoever had his letter has known about it for ten years, and they've been looking for you specifically, not just for someone uninfected — for you, with your name, which means they know about the disc."

Maren went very still.

"I have a nose," Vane said. "And I'm very good at finding things. And I think you're going to need someone who is very good at finding things before this is over." She looked north, toward the road. "Also your ankle is going to be giving you trouble again by nightfall and someone should be there when it does."

She walked north and didn't wait to see if Maren would follow.

She heard her fall into step behind her within thirty seconds.

She did not let this show on her face.

But she noted it, the way she noted everything — took it in with the particular scent of this afternoon, this town, this road, and the quality of the decision she had just made for reasons she understood with her nose better than with any other part of herself.

It smelled, she thought, like something that was going to be complicated.

She was probably right about that.

She walked anyway.

## Chapter Twelve — *[Davan]*
### New Plymouth

---

He found work on the third day, which was later than he'd hoped and earlier than he'd feared.

The docks district ran on casual labor the way a mill ran on water — constantly, without much concern for where it came from, as long as it arrived. He spent two days learning the rhythms of it before he approached the right foreman at the right time, a technique his father had taught him: watch how a system works before you try to enter it, because every system has a current and swimming against it is wasted effort. The foreman's name was Jesset, a large weathered man with a change that had given him an additional joint in each finger, making his grip extraordinary and his handshakes memorable. He hired Davan for three days' unloading work at a rate that was fair and told him where to be at dawn.

The work was straightforward and physical and occupied exactly the right amount of his mind — most of it, but not all. The part that wasn't occupied watched the docks.

New Plymouth's waterfront was a city's worth of information compressed into a quarter-mile of dock and the streets immediately behind it. He had grown up in a town where he knew everyone's business and everyone knew his, the social geography legible in ten minutes. This was different in kind. In three days of unloading crates he took in: seven distinct neighborhoods visible from the dock, each with its own quality of foot traffic and its own smell; at least four languages being spoken, two he couldn't identify; a shadow economy operating openly enough that he could see its outlines without being able to read its language; and a stratum of people who moved through the docks district without being of it — the ones with clean clothes and the specific purposeful walk of people passing through rather than arriving.

He kept all of it in the back of his mind, available for later.

Pip he met on the fourth day, when a crate slipped on a wet dock plank and Davan caught it and Pip caught Davan, because the crate was heavy and the momentum of saving it would have taken him over the edge. They were briefly a chain of two people keeping each other upright on a wet dock, and then they were not, and Pip said something profane and specific about wet dock planks, and Davan agreed, and they ate lunch together.

Pip was twenty-three, born in the docks district, and had the particular expertise of someone who had grown up in a place and paid attention. His change had manifested at sixteen as an ability to find things — not by magic, he was careful to say, not like a bloodhound, more like a persistent accurate intuition about where things were likely to be given everything he knew about the situation. He'd lost three months' wages at cards before he understood this didn't extend to predicting other people's choices. He'd made considerably more than that back once he'd stopped trying to use it that way and started using it for what it was actually good for, which was finding things that had been lost, misplaced, or hidden by people who didn't know him well enough to account for him.

He knew New Plymouth the way a good river pilot knew a stretch of water — not by map but by attention, by having been wrong enough times to understand where the current actually ran.

---

The flophouse was better than the stable had been, not high praise, but Davan was sleeping well and eating adequately and had established, by the end of the first week, a functional daily life. Work at dawn when there was work. The market at midday for cheap food. The evenings his own.

He spent them carving and watching and thinking about the symbol.

He had not been able to stop thinking about the symbol since the temple doorway at the Confluence, the inner orbit he'd seen and couldn't unsee. He'd been looking for it here, in the temples and shrines and official buildings, finding it on every lintel, and every time reading it the same way he'd read it that afternoon in the temple courtyard — not a spiral, something else. The geometry of a circular path.

He carved it into wood. He dropped the pieces in the river. He carved it again.

"You do that a lot," Pip said one evening. They were sitting on the dock with their feet over the water, Pip eating something that had been sold to him as a meat pie and which he was revising his assessment of. "The carving."

"When I'm working something out."

"What are you working out?"

Davan looked at the symbol in the wood. "I don't know yet. That's why I'm carving it."

Pip looked at it. He had the quality Davan had noticed early — a visual attention that moved differently from most people's, landing on specifics rather than impressions. "That's the Chrysalis mark."

"Part of it. The inner part."

"What about it?"

Davan considered how to say what he was thinking, which was the usual difficulty. He'd been thinking it for weeks and it had not resolved into language. "It doesn't look like the rest of the symbol. It's older, scholars think. Absorbed from something that existed before the Chrysalis theology."

Pip looked at it some more. "Looks like something going around something."

"Yes," Davan said. "That's what I think too."

They sat with this for a while. Below them, the river moved with its usual indifference. Three moons were up, their combined light making silver of the water.

"There's a man," Pip said eventually, "who collects things. Edvar. Lives in the Crafting Quarter, sells maps, but that's not really what he does." He looked at the symbol again. "He has old things. Things that don't fit anywhere. He might be worth talking to." He paused. "He's careful about who he talks to. I'll put in a word."

---

A week passed before Pip said anything further about Edvar, and another three days after that before he mentioned, with studied casualness, that Edvar would see him on Thursday afternoon if he wanted.

Davan understood from this that something had happened in the interval — that Pip had done some form of work on his behalf, something beyond a casual word, and that Edvar had assessed the report and arrived at a provisional conclusion. He was being given an appointment, different from being welcomed, and the distinction felt important.

He went on Thursday.

---

Edvar's map shop was narrow and deep, scrolls in racks along both walls, the smell of old paper and the particular mineral scent of the inks used in serious cartographic work. The maps themselves were extraordinary — Davan lingered on one, a coastal survey of the northern reaches that showed features he'd never seen documented, fjords and headlands sketched with the precision of someone who had actually been there and looked.

Edvar was sixty or so, lean in the way of someone for whom food had always been an afterthought, with a scholar's hands and a merchant's eyes — the combination of someone deeply interested in things who had learned to be careful about showing it. He came out from behind a worktable when Davan entered, looked at him with a brief and thorough attention, and said: "Pip speaks well of you."

"He's been generous," Davan said.

"He's accurate, which is different." Edvar looked at the carving Davan had set on the counter without quite deciding to. "He said you'd been working this form for weeks."

"I saw it differently in a temple doorway. I've been trying to work out what I saw."

Edvar picked up the carving. He turned it in his hands with the specific handling of someone who was not looking at the object so much as reading it — the pressure points, the grain direction, the way the form had arrived through the wood rather than been imposed on it. He set it down.

"The inner form," he said. "You've separated it from the Chrysalis surround."

"The surround felt like it had been added later. Like two things that had been joined."

Edvar looked at him with a quality of attention that was different from before. Not warmer. More present. "Most people who notice that decide they've made an error and correct back to the standard reading."

"It didn't feel like an error."

"No," Edvar said. "It isn't one." He was quiet for a moment, his hands still on the worktable, deciding something. Then he went to a rack at the back of the shop and spent some time there before returning with a scroll, unrolling it on the worktable.

It was a map — old, the paper gone brown at the edges, the ink a faded reddish-brown rather than black. It showed a section of the eastern highlands in considerable detail, the kind of detail that only came from having walked the ground and measured it. In the margins, in a small precise hand, a notation. The inner orbit symbol, isolated, with a number beside it.

"Four," Davan said.

"Four," Edvar agreed.

Davan looked at the map more carefully. He could feel the land taking shape in his mind the way warmth spread from a fire — the northern coast where New Plymouth sat, a river system running south and branching toward the Confluence, the glint of the lake at Stillwater marked to the south. And east: foothills rolling toward Irongate country, the highlands rising in stages, the ground going rougher and less traveled the further you went. If the legends placed something in those eastern foothills, it would be five or six days' travel from where he was standing. He traced the distance with his eye and felt the scale of it settle into him, solid and real.

"What does it mean?" he asked, pulling his attention back to the margin notation. "The four."

Edvar rolled the map back up with deliberate care and returned it to its rack. He came back and stood with his hands on the worktable and looked at Davan for a moment.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I've been working on it for twenty years." A pause. "Come back when you've been here longer. There are things I show to people I know. You're someone Pip vouched for on a Thursday." He said it without unkindness. "That's a start, not an arrival."

Davan looked at the rack where the map had gone. Then at Edvar. "Fair," he said.

He thanked him and left. Outside, in the Crafting Quarter's particular compound of sawdust and metal and oil, he stood for a moment and thought about what had just happened.

He'd been assessed and found not-yet-sufficient, which was a different thing from being turned away. The map had been shown — just the margin, just the notation, nothing more — as a kind of reciprocal gesture: I'll show you I have something if you show me you understand why that matters. It had been a careful and precise social transaction and he respected it and found it clarifying. He was not in Amber Valley, where trust was ambient. He was somewhere that required trust to be built, specifically, over time, in the particular way you built anything worth having.

He would come back.

He walked down toward the river.

---

He carved on the docks that evening, feet over the water, working on the piece he'd been carrying since before the Confluence — the hardwood shape resolving itself for weeks, an abstract curve with something intentional to it that he hadn't yet named.

He'd been there longer than he meant to be. The light was going and his hands were cold and the detail he'd been working — an edge that wanted to be a curve and kept arriving as an angle — was not cooperating. He put the knife away. He put the wood in his pocket. He sat for a moment looking at the river in the way of someone who has decided to leave and is gathering the momentum to do it.

He almost left. He was halfway to standing when his hand found the piece of driftwood on the dock beside him — soft, water-worn, the kind that took a blade with almost no resistance — and the particular quality of its grain said *one more*, and he sat back down and started the symbol again, the inner orbit, quick and clean in the soft wood. A two-minute carving. An afterthought.

He was finishing it when he became aware of someone nearby.

Not close — a piling over, maybe six feet. He registered it without looking up. This end of the dock was the quiet end, past the loading bays, past the noise — the kind of spot the docks workers didn't use because there was nothing to load here and travelers didn't use because there was nothing to see. The people who came here came because they wanted the water without the waterfront, and in two weeks of evening carving Davan had noticed the same handful of faces: a fisherman who drank alone, an old woman who fed the river birds, the occasional couple wanting privacy. People at the edge of things.

He glanced up. A girl was standing at the dock's end — not sitting, standing, in the posture of someone who had come this far and was deciding whether to stay. Dark-haired, perhaps his age or a little younger, road-worn in the way he recognized from his own weeks of walking. She was looking at the water and then at the dock and then at him and then back at the water, the quick triangulation of someone assessing whether a space was safe enough to occupy. She stood like that for a moment that lasted long enough to be a decision being made.

Then she sat down. One piling over, feet above the water, eyes on the middle distance. She had a quality he'd learned to recognize in himself in mirrors: the alert watchfulness of someone who had been moving through uncertain country and hadn't entirely stopped, even sitting still.

Something else, too. Not a wrongness exactly — more like hearing a familiar song with a note missing, or standing near a fire that gave no heat. The warmth was expected and absent, and the absence was its own kind of presence. He looked at her face and the feeling sharpened slightly and he looked away.

She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the driftwood beside him.

He followed her gaze. He'd been carving the symbol again without noticing — he did this, the symbol appearing in whatever he was working on when his conscious attention was elsewhere, the way certain words appeared in his speech when he was tired. The inner orbit, clean and precise, in a piece of soft driftwood he'd picked up off the dock.

She was looking at it the way you look at something you've seen before and weren't expecting to see again.

For a moment he thought she was going to leave. She shifted her weight forward — the beginning of standing, the gathering motion of someone who has seen something she didn't expect and whose first instinct is to withdraw from it. He watched this happen without moving, without speaking, because anything he did right now would be wrong. She had to decide.

She settled back.

The feeling sharpened. He became aware, slowly, of the weight of the pine carving in his coat pocket — the small figure he'd made on the road north without deciding to, the one with the arm that wouldn't hang right. He had not looked at it closely since he'd made it. He didn't need to. The girl sitting six feet from him was holding her left arm the same way — close, guarded, tucked against her ribs with the specific care of someone who'd been carrying that posture so long it had stopped being a choice and become simply how she sat. His hands had carved that gesture weeks ago, working from something he hadn't understood, and now it was sitting beside him on a dock in the fading light. A sudden strong reluctance came over him — not because the carving was frightening, but because looking at it would mean acknowledging something his hands had known before the rest of him was ready. He wasn't ready.

He waited.

A long time. Long enough that the light moved and the dock's shadows shifted and a barge passed in the main channel and neither of them spoke or looked at each other. She was deciding something. He could feel the shape of the decision in the way her attention kept returning to the driftwood symbol and leaving it and returning — the rhythm of someone testing whether a risk was worth taking.

Then she reached into her coat and took out a small metal disc. Her hand hesitated — he saw it, the briefest pause at the coat's edge, the last moment before a thing done couldn't be undone. She didn't offer it to him — she angled it toward the fading light so the edge caught it, and on the edge of the disc the symbol was there, clean and small and identical to what he'd carved.

He looked at it.

He looked at her.

Neither of them said anything.

Somewhere further down the dock, at the edge of his peripheral vision, a figure was leaning against a mooring post — a woman, older, leaning in a way that said she'd chosen that spot deliberately and had no interest in being seen. He had the sense she'd been there longer than he'd realized. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the river.

He turned back to the girl with the disc.

"I'm Davan," he said.

She looked at him for a moment, weighing whether a name was something she was ready to exchange.

"Maren," she said.

They sat in the growing dark and said nothing for a while, and the river moved below them, and the first moon came up over the eastern ridge, and the woman down the dock remained precisely as still as she'd been.

He did not take the pine carving out of his pocket.

## Chapter Thirteen — *[Aldric]*
### What the Restricted Collection Restricts

---

Brennan's letter arrived on a Thursday, tucked between a supply invoice and a pastoral circular from the New Plymouth office, which was either a coincidence or a demonstration that someone handling correspondence had a sense of irony. Aldric found it at his desk in the late afternoon, identified it by the handwriting on the outside — Brennan had written in the same cramped leftward script since seminary, apparently on the principle that a man's hand was formed young and not worth revising — and set it aside until he had finished the supply invoice and the pastoral circular and the three other items requiring his attention first.

This took him forty minutes. He was aware, during those forty minutes, of the letter sitting at the corner of his desk with the patient weight of something that had traveled a long way to arrive.

He opened it.

---

Brennan was a scholar of old languages and what he called *material culture*, by which he meant things — objects, structures, residues of how people had actually lived rather than how they'd described themselves as living. He had spent thirty years in Greenford's university district accumulating a reputation for being rigorously careful and mildly inconvenient to the official accounts of various things. He and Aldric had maintained a correspondence for twenty years that served, for both of them, as a kind of intellectual weather station — the place you sent an observation that didn't fit the current theory and waited to see what came back.

Aldric had sent his observation. Brennan had sent back a storm.

The letter was eight pages. Brennan wrote the way he talked, densely and without apology, with the assumption that the reader was keeping up and could be expected to do some of the work. Aldric read it twice before he allowed himself to think about any individual part of it.

The first four pages were what he'd asked for: the Fathers' Boats tradition, its variants, its geographical distribution, its age. What Brennan had found went considerably deeper than the two pages in the Confluence archive. The tradition was old — older than the standard Chrysalis theology by a margin suggesting it predated the religion that had absorbed it. In the oldest versions, the boats were not metaphorical. They were specific: four of them, arriving from elsewhere, three of which remained. The fourth was described variously as fallen, broken, gone into the ground.

Aldric set the page down and pressed his fingers to the bridge of his nose. He was hearing, with a clarity that felt almost physical, the voice of every child in every temple yard he'd ever walked through: *four fires in the sky, one came down to die, three stayed up to cry.* A skipping rhyme. Considered by the Temple to be harmless folk tradition. He had considered it harmless himself, for thirty years, the way you consider wallpaper harmless — it was simply there, too familiar to examine. The children had been singing the count the theology had quietly revised, and no one had noticed because no one listens to what children are singing.

He picked the page back up. In several versions, the inhabitants of the fourth boat were the source of the Chrysalis — not as a gift, but as a kind of residue. Something that had been in them, and was now in the world, and had spread.

Something that had been in them.

Aldric put the letter down. He pinched the desk lamp out of habit — still burning, had been burning for an hour, he pinched it anyway and sat for a moment in the dimmer light of the room's remaining lamp before he caught himself and let it go and relit the first lamp with a taper.

He read the next four pages.

---

Brennan had a source he hadn't named in prior correspondence, a contact in the New Plymouth archive who had, under circumstances Brennan declined to specify, seen portions of the Restricted Collection. Not the full collection. A fraction of it. But a specific fraction: the material relating to what Brennan called, in the careful language of a man who knew his letters were read by people other than their intended recipient, *the old cartography*.

The old cartography, as Brennan described it, showed Gaius from an angle that conventional Gaian cartography did not use. Not a plan view, not a coastal survey, not the road-and-river maps that governed daily life. The angle from above. Not from a hill or a tower. From considerably further above than that.

The maps showed features no ground-level survey would capture — the shape of the continent from outside the continent, the curve of the coastline as something continuous rather than a series of connected local surveys. And in several versions, in the borders and margins, a notation recurring across multiple maps in what appeared to be multiple hands and multiple time periods, as if different people with different purposes had found occasion to write the same thing.

The notation used a symbol Brennan had reproduced in the margin of his letter. Aldric looked at it.

The inner orbit. The symbol from the temple doorway, isolated from the Chrysalis surround, the thing that wasn't quite a spiral.

Beside it, in each map, a number. Always one of three: three, four, or one.

Brennan's interpretation — offered carefully, prefaced with the appropriate scholarly qualifications about the limits of inference from fragmentary sources — was that the notation was a count. Of something in a fixed position relative to the world. Something that could be counted from the position from which the maps had been made.

Three, four, or one.

*Three moons,* Aldric thought. *Four moons before. One came down.*

He sat at his desk for a very long time. Then he read on.

---

Brennan's contact had seen something else in the New Plymouth restricted materials. He had written it carefully, in his oblique way, tucking it among the cartographic discussion as though it were a footnote rather than what it clearly was: another piece of a pattern.

*Among the catalogued objects from what the archive's internal notation calls the Founding period,* Brennan wrote, *my source identified three distinct categories of material. The first corresponds to what you described finding — containers of the same unidentified gray material, sealed, apparently inert. The archive lists several of these, all recovered from the eastern highlands over the past two centuries. The second category is objects of navigation or display — instruments whose purpose is not entirely clear, but whose construction matches the containers in material and apparent age. Something in your description of the glass face in the sealed room suggests it may belong to this category, though I would want to examine it before saying so with confidence.*

*The third category is more difficult. The archive notation refers to it obliquely, using the phrase "the Voice" in some records and "instrument of authority" in others. From what my source was able to gather, this is a weapon — or something that functions as one. It appears only once in any historical account with enough specificity to be confirmed: the Stilling of the Brace, three hundred years ago, when the bloodline suppressed the Brace uprising and left a mark on the palace steps that has never been explained and that people with the Chrysalis change cannot look at directly. The legend attributes this to a weapon the bloodline used and then secured. The archive materials suggest the weapon was not manufactured by the bloodline, but recovered, like the containers — a Founding-era artifact they had learned to operate.*

*The significant detail — and this is where I would ask you to proceed carefully, because I am reasoning from fragments and fragments can mislead — is the qualification attached to all three categories. The annotation "for unchanged hands" appears in the margin of the relevant catalog page, in a hand that is not the archivist's. It is the kind of note that gets written when someone is recording not just what a thing is, but what it requires.*

*If the containers, the navigation objects, the weapon, and whatever else belongs to this collection were all made for the same hands — and if "unchanged" means what I think it means in this context — then you are looking at a collection built for and operated by people who carry no Chrysalis change. The bloodline's claim to be the only such people is the foundation of their authority. And the weapon at the Stilling of the Brace is the most visible proof they have ever offered of that authority's physical reality.*

*I think the container you found, the glass you found, and the weapon secured in the palace vaults may all be from the same original collection. I think someone assembled these objects for a specific purpose, before the Chrysalis, and that the bloodline has spent three hundred years presenting their ability to use what they found as evidence of their singular nature. I do not know what this means for anything. But it seems to me that you should know it.*

Aldric set the page down.

He was a careful man, and careful men did not leap from fragments to conclusions. He noted that Brennan had offered the same caution himself. But a pattern was a pattern, and three categories of artifacts with the same annotation, in the same archive, pointing toward the same history — that was not a fragment. That was a shape.

He read the final page.

---

*I have to tell you something that is going to sound like the product of an old man's anxiety,* Brennan wrote, *but I want you to take it seriously. I have been asking, carefully, about the Restricted Collection for six months. In the last two months I have been aware that someone has been asking about me asking. Not a scholar. Not a librarian. Someone with access to the kind of information about my movements requiring either a very well-connected patron or a formal arrangement with the bloodline's domestic office.*

*I am telling you this because you asked me to look, and I looked, and I found more than either of us expected, and I think you should know that this territory has people in it who are watching for exactly this kind of looking.*

*I am also telling you that I think you should come to Greenford. Not by letter. Some of what I have cannot be written down. There is a man here, a surveyor who worked the eastern highlands forty years ago and has not discussed what he found there since, and I think he will talk to you in a way he won't talk to me, because you are a Keeper and I am merely inconvenient.*

*Come carefully. Tell no one you are coming, including in correspondence.*

*I remain, as always, your poorly-advised friend,*
*B.*

---

Aldric folded the letter and sat with it in his hands and thought about what it meant.

He was a careful man. He had been careful for thirty years, and carefulness had served him well, and he had not reached his current position — a major waypoint temple, a Keeper's rank, the respect of his peers and superiors — by acting precipitously on incomplete information. He believed in process. He believed in working through the appropriate channels, even when the appropriate channels were slow, because the channels existed for reasons and the reasons generally remained valid even when individual situations made them inconvenient.

He believed all of this and he was aware, sitting at his desk in the late afternoon with Brennan's letter in his hands, that he was reasoning around it in the way he'd been reasoning around the sealed room and the face and the symbol above the doorway. Making careful loops. Not arriving.

He thought about Prioress Crane.

She had been at the temple for five weeks. She had finished her review of the eastern archive catalog and had moved to the financial records, a normal thing for a doctrinal review to do, requiring her to spend several afternoons in the records room two floors above the undercroft. During those afternoons, Aldric had found reasons to be present in the eastern corridor, the one with the stairs, without being obvious about it. He was not sure he'd been successful at the not-obvious part.

She had, three days ago, asked him a question about the history of the temple's eastern wing. A casual question, architecturally framed, the kind arising naturally from reviewing records of building work. He had answered it accurately and completely, providing the date of the eastern expansion and the name of the Keeper who had commissioned it and the surviving records of the construction. He had not mentioned the undercroft. She had not asked about the undercroft.

The question had not been about the undercroft.

He had spent three days wondering why she hadn't asked about the undercroft.

He took out his journal and found the page with the note he'd made weeks ago: *Restricted Collection, NP Archive, shelf 7-eastern. Access: Bloodline approval required.* He looked at it. He thought about what Brennan had written: *someone with access to the kind of information about my movements requiring either a very well-connected patron or a formal arrangement with the bloodline's domestic office.*

The church and the bloodline were separate. This was doctrine, history, the settled arrangement of the world. The church administered the spiritual life of Gaius; the bloodline administered its temporal governance; the two collaborated on matters of mutual concern and otherwise respected their respective territories. This was what Aldric had understood his entire career, and it was, he was slowly understanding, a description of a relationship that had been true once and had not been true for some time.

The Restricted Collection required bloodline approval for access.

The Prioress's review had been initiated by the New Plymouth office.

He thought about the face in the sealed room below him. He thought about the fourth shape around the blue-green world. He thought about a notation on old maps counting something from above, and about the annotation in someone else's hand: *for unchanged hands.* He thought about a weapon used once at the Stilling of the Brace, leaving a mark on the palace steps that people with the Chrysalis cannot look at directly.

He thought: I have been reasoning around this for two months because to arrive at the conclusion is to be in a situation with no institutional remedy. The institution is part of the situation.

He sat with this.

He thought about Crane.

Not as an obstacle — he had been thinking of her that way for weeks, the careful adversary navigating the same corridors, and it had been easy, too easy, to reduce her to a function. But she was not a function. She was a Prioress who had spent her career in service to the same principles he had served, a woman who had risen to her position through the same combination of competence and dedication and willingness to do unglamorous work that had brought him to his. She believed in the institution. He believed in the institution. They disagreed, if they disagreed at all, only about what the institution was for.

He could tell her.

The thought arrived with a clarity that surprised him, because it was not new — it had been sitting at the edge of his thinking for weeks, the obvious correct action he had been declining to take. He could walk to her rooms tonight. He could sit across from her with the tea she always had warming and say: *There is a sealed room beneath the east wing, and in it are objects that predate the temple, and one of them shows me things I cannot explain, and I have been lying to you about all of this, and I am asking for your help.*

She would listen. She would be thorough. She would file the appropriate reports through the appropriate channels, because she was a woman who believed in channels, and the reports would travel upward through the system — regional office, New Plymouth archive, the High Keeper's desk — and they would arrive, eventually, at the same place all such reports arrived: a decision made by people with the authority to decide, in consultation with the bloodline's historical commission, what to do with objects recovered from sealed rooms in old temples.

*The Restricted Collection, NP Archive, shelf 7-eastern. Access: Bloodline approval required.*

The question he had been carrying — the question Sella had pressed into his wrist with her translucent grip, the question the children had been singing in the temple yard — would be contained. Not answered. Contained.

He had served the institution for thirty years. He trusted it the way he trusted a well-built wall — completely, until you found the sealed room behind it.

He did not pinch the lamp. He held his thumb and forefinger still above his knee, pressed together, finding nothing, and he looked at them and uncurled them deliberately and laid his hand flat on the desk.

He took out a clean sheet and began to write a letter to his second, Brother Cael, explaining that he would be traveling to Greenford for a scholarly consultation and expected to be absent for two weeks, that Cael should manage the daily operations in his absence, that the Prioress should be afforded every courtesy and any assistance she required. He wrote it in the pleasant administrative tone of a Keeper planning a routine journey, because that was the correct tone and because Cael was a good man and a reliable administrator and had no part in any of this, and Aldric wanted to keep it that way.

Then he sat and thought about whether there was anything in his rooms, in his desk, in the undercroft, that he needed to address before he left.

The face could not be moved. The sealed room could be re-sealed, but Holst knew it existed, and Crane was thorough, and a re-sealed room found by a thorough person was considerably more interesting than an unsealed room found before the finding. He thought about this and decided to leave it as it was. If she was going to find it, she would find it, and what she'd find was a room with artifacts she had no more context for than he'd had eight weeks ago.

He thought about Brennan's letter. He could not keep it in his rooms. He could not destroy it, both for the information it contained and because he was not, yet, the kind of man who destroyed correspondence, and he was not entirely sure he wanted to become that man. He would carry it.

He opened the journal to the current page and wrote, in his oblique shorthand:

*Going east to find out what I don't know. The not-knowing has become a different kind of problem than not-knowing usually is. I leave the room below as it is. I leave the light to face what comes. I leave with less certainty than I arrived with, which is the condition I was taught to seek and turns out to be considerably less comfortable in practice than in theory.*

He paused. Then he wrote:

*The symbol above the east door describes an orbit. I have counted four bodies in it. I am going to talk to a man who walked the eastern highlands forty years ago and found something he has not spoken about since. I am a Keeper of this temple and I have served in honesty for thirty years, and I am aware that what I am about to do requires me to define honesty more broadly than I have previously found necessary.*

*I believe the definition can bear the expansion.*

*I hope I am right about this.*

He closed the journal.

---

He left at first light on a Saturday, the beginning of his nominal discretionary period — two days per month that were his own, by the ancient custom of his order, so rarely used in his career that Cael looked faintly surprised when Aldric mentioned them. He told Cael the Greenford consultation had been long planned and he'd simply not mentioned it. Cael accepted this with the professional incuriosity of a man who had learned that Keepers occasionally had business that was Keepers' business.

He passed Prioress Crane in the eastern corridor on his way to the stable. She was walking toward the archive with a folder of documents under one arm, already in her working posture, the precise organized motion of a woman with a full day ahead of her.

"Keeper Voss," she said. Pleasantly.

"Prioress." He kept his pace even. "I'll be in Greenford until the middle of the month. Brother Cael has everything in hand."

"Of course." She looked at him with the expression she always wore, the one giving away nothing and suggesting, by its very completeness, that it was giving away nothing deliberately. "Safe travels."

"Thank you," he said.

He walked to the stable and readied his horse, old and amiable and with an excellent temperament for long roads, and he went east toward Greenford with Brennan's letter in his coat and the journal in his pack and the symbol from the doorway lintel in the back of his mind, orbiting something he couldn't see yet but was riding toward.

Behind him, at the end of the corridor, Prioress Crane watched him go.

He did not look back. He had always found it more useful to know which direction you were heading than what you were leaving.

The road east was cold and clear, and the moons were down, and the sky before him was the particular pale gray of early morning promising either a good traveling day or rain before noon, and couldn't yet be told which.

He rode into it.

## Chapter Fourteen — *[Maren]*
### Stillness as a Skill

---

Vane did not want her leaving the room.

She hadn't said this directly — she rarely said anything directly that she could say through arrangement instead — but the shape of it was clear from the first morning: the careful inventory of the room's exits, the window assessment, the specific instructions about the sound to make in the alley below before coming up. The room was defensible. The city was not. The former was preferable for as long as they could manage it.

Maren had pushed back on the second morning.

"I need to know the city," she'd said. "I can't run from somewhere I don't know."

Vane had looked at her for a long moment. "Two hours. Morning, when the docks are busy. Direct route to the market and back. Nothing requiring someone to look at you twice."

"That's not knowing the city."

"It's a start." Vane had said it with the specific finality meaning the conversation was over, and Maren had recognized the finality and decided the second morning was not the right moment to test it.

She had tested it on the fourth morning, returning an hour late by a different route, and Vane had been at the window when she came into the alley and the look on her face had been the kind that didn't need words. They had sat in the room for a while without speaking, and then Vane had said: "Tell me the route you took and everything you noticed," and Maren had told her, in full, and by the end of it the look had shifted — still watchful, but with something behind the watchfulness that wasn't quite satisfaction and wasn't quite relief and was probably both.

After that, two hours became three, and the direct route became Maren's own route, provided she could account for it afterward.

This was how Vane gave ground: not by conceding but by incorporating. You didn't win an argument with her. You demonstrated that your judgment had operational value and she updated accordingly.

---

The room itself was tolerable. Small — one bed, one table, one lamp, the window onto the alley — but clean, and the landlady Tova had provided an extra blanket without being asked, which suggested some experience with people who needed rooms and discretion simultaneously. The river smell came through the window at all hours. Maren had stopped noticing it by the third day.

They slept in shifts for the first two nights, until Vane had established to her own satisfaction that the room was not known to anyone who mattered. Then they slept normally — Vane in the way of someone trained to wake fast, Maren lying in the dark thinking until exhaustion made the decision for her.

She thought about the disc. She thought about what her uncle had told Ulla before the fever took him — the one thing her father had said the night he left: *Going to the hill. The disc will open it.* He'd said this once and then kept moving. He'd known about it — had known enough to go looking, had known what the key was. Had known, presumably, what it meant if someone was looking for him.

She thought about what Vane had said on the road: *they've been looking for you specifically, with your name, which means they know about the disc.* They knew about the disc and they'd known for long enough to have anticipated her coming north. Her father had known about the disc too, had gone east with it ten years ago, had presumably not opened whatever it opened or they wouldn't still be looking. Which meant either he hadn't found the hill, or he'd found it and couldn't open it without something else, or he'd opened it and what was inside hadn't given them what they needed.

Or he'd opened it and they'd caught him before he could do anything with it.

She didn't follow that thought to its end. Some thoughts were worth sitting with and some were only useful when you had more information, and this one was the latter.

She thought about the young man at the docks instead.

She had not mentioned him to Vane. Not quite protectiveness, not quite caution. Something more like not wanting to give something away before she understood its value. He'd had the symbol. He'd been carving it without knowing why, over and over, in the way she'd been carrying the disc without understanding it. Another person circling the same question from a different direction. That seemed significant. What to do with its significance was less clear.

---

On the fifth morning, Maren went out for her usual circuit — market, then a swing through the side streets near the Temple District before heading back. The market was busy, the foot traffic thick enough that she moved easily through it, just another face among dozens.

She was at a stall selling dried fish when she saw them.

Two men, working the market in the way that wasn't shopping. One at the far end of the row, the other closer. The kind of spacing you used when you were covering ground systematically, not looking for goods but for faces. Their clothes were unremarkable — that was the tell. Too unremarkable, too deliberately nothing, the studied plainness of men who wanted to be invisible in a specific way. She'd watched Vane move through a crowd long enough to know what that particular kind of not-being-there looked like from the outside.

The closer one stopped at a vegetable stall. She heard him ask a question. The vendor shook her head. He asked again, and Maren caught the shape of it — *young woman, dark-haired, traveling alone* — before she'd already turned, already moving, slipping between two women with loaded baskets and into the gap between stalls. A side street. Then another. She kept her pace even, kept her hands loose, didn't look back until she'd put three corners between herself and the market row.

Nothing behind her. She walked the long way back.

---

She came into the alley with the correct signal and came upstairs and stood in the middle of the room.

"Market row, eastern end," she said. "Two men, working in a spread pattern. Dressed plain, too plain. The closer one was asking a vendor — dark-haired young woman, traveling alone. He was asking north from the river end of the row, moving east. The other was at the far end of the row holding position. Both wearing gray-brown coats, belt knives not visible but the shoulder posture was wrong for unarmed." She paused. "The vendor he asked didn't react like she knew anything. He moved on."

Vane was looking at her with the attentive stillness she wore when she was processing.

"Which direction were they moving?" she asked.

"North from the river end when I left. Into the market. If they're systematic they'll work east and then north by noon."

"How far from the Merchant Quarter?"

"Four streets, maybe five." Maren sat down. "He's deploying in a grid."

"Yes," Vane said. She was quiet for a moment. Then: "Good work. That's well-observed."

Maren said nothing. She was thinking about the timing — how many days before a grid like that swept as far as this district.

---

Vane sat across the table and was quiet for a longer moment, and Maren recognized the quality of the quiet — thinking that had already been done and was now being sorted into the right order for presentation.

"I'm going to make a report to the dock constabulary," Vane said. "About the back room at Cade's supply house."

Maren looked at her. She knew what the back room at Cade's supply house was — Vane had mentioned it once, obliquely, as part of explaining the city's Bloom trade geography. "That's the Bloom supply operation."

"Yes."

"If you report it, the constabulary shuts it down."

"Or investigates. Which creates the same effect." Vane's voice was level, clinical. "Horst's agents can search a city where the constabulary is looking the other way. They can't search one where the constabulary is suddenly interested in the docks district. An investigation into the Bloom trade draws official attention to every transaction, every movement, every face in the area. It makes quiet operations expensive."

Maren turned this over. The logic was sound. Horst's men in their too-plain coats, working a systematic grid — they needed invisibility to do that. Put the constabulary in the docks district and the invisibility was gone.

"Why does the constabulary care about a report from you?"

"Because I know what's in the back room," Vane said. "And I know who uses it. And I know that the Bloom trade and the bloodline are connected." A pause — shorter than the ones she usually allowed herself. "They use bloodless people to produce it. I overheard this. If I make that connection visible to the constabulary, Horst has to manage a contamination problem instead of a search."

The practical geometry of it was clear. What Maren was looking at was something else.

The bark on Vane's arms, beneath her sleeves — Maren had caught a glimpse that morning when Vane had rolled them to wash, and the patches had spread visibly since the road, the edges angrier, the dark wood-grain texture climbing past the elbow on her right arm now. The supply tin in her pack that Maren had never seen her open, but had seen her check — twice a day, always with the same brief precise motion, the way you checked a wound you were monitoring for change. The episodes on the road, manageable then, had not stayed manageable. Maren had woken two nights ago to Vane sitting on the floor with her palms flat and her eyes unfocused, breathing in the counted-breath pattern she used when the world was doing something she hadn't asked it to do. It had lasted twenty minutes. When it passed, Vane had gotten up and gone back to the table and continued working the maps as if nothing had happened, and neither of them had spoken about it.

The Bloom was what kept those episodes short. Burning the supply operation meant no refill. It meant whatever remained in the tin — Maren didn't know how much, but from the frequency of the checking she suspected not much — was all Vane had between herself and whatever the full withdrawal looked like, and Maren had seen a preview of the full withdrawal in a roadside ditch and it had not looked survivable as a sustained condition.

"That's your supply," Maren said.

Vane's expression didn't change. "Yes."

"You're burning your own supply to pull them off me."

"I'm creating a tactical diversion using an available resource." She said it with complete evenness, the way she said most things, and did not say anything else. But her hands on the table were not quite still — the fine tremor Maren had first seen in the ditch, present now at the fingertips when Vane wasn't actively gripping something. It came and went. It was coming more than it went.

Maren looked at those hands. She thought about the tremor and the bark and the tin she'd watched Vane check and then stop checking, and the copper-floral smell that clung to the wafers, and what it meant that something with that smell was made from a person. She thought about the specific cost being laid on the table between them, factual and unarguable, and the way Vane was not going to let her say anything significant about it, and the way that refusal was itself a kind of statement about what the cost meant to her.

"All right," Maren said. She said it carefully, putting into two words everything she was not allowed to say about the rest of it.

Vane nodded once, already moving on. "We make the report tomorrow morning. After that we have two days before the constabulary presence becomes established enough to give us cover. We use those two days."

"For what?"

"Planning the route east." She pulled the maps across the table.

---

On the sixth day, Vane came back from her morning round earlier than usual.

Maren read it before she spoke — the quality of the entry, the way she set her pack down, the specific economy of her movements tightening when something required managing. And something else, newer: a hesitation at the threshold, brief, almost invisible, the way someone pauses when they're not certain the room is going to stay where it is. Vane had made the report to the constabulary two days ago. Maren had not asked about the tin since. She didn't need to. The checking had gone from twice a day to not at all, which meant it was either empty or Vane had decided to stop looking at what was left.

"Horst is in the city," Vane said.

She sat down and told Maren what she knew: the Merchant Quarter, a rented house, two men. Arrived two days ago by the river road. In the city himself, which meant the contract's timeline had become a concern at the level of someone who cared about timelines.

"He thinks I'm still running the search," Vane said. "He doesn't know I've walked away. As far as he's aware, I'm close."

"Are you? Close?"

Vane looked at her with the flat expression meaning the question had an obvious answer. "I'm in the same room as you."

Maren thought about the geometry: Horst in the Merchant Quarter, his men presumably ranging, the bloodline's existing search pattern converging on New Plymouth from the south. The room, defensible, four districts from the Merchant Quarter.

"How long before it stops being four districts?" she said.

"Days. Less if he's systematic." Vane's eyes went to the maps on the table, then back to Maren. "We need to move before he finds us. But we can't move until we know where we're moving toward." She paused. "The hill. Your father's letter says the disc opens it. We need more than that before we walk into open country with the bloodline behind us."

"How do we get more?"

Vane was quiet for a moment — the specific quiet meaning she'd been thinking about this before the conversation started and was deciding how to present it.

"There was someone at the dock the other night," she said. "When you were sitting at the water."

Maren went still.

"The young man. Carving." Vane said it carefully. "He had the symbol. The inner orbit — same form as the disc's edge. He's been carving it repeatedly, from what I could see. Not as decoration. The way someone works something out."

"You were watching."

"I was there." A pause. "I stayed further back than I might have. You seemed to be handling it."

Maren thought about the dock, the fading light, the symbol appearing in soft driftwood. She thought about the way his attention had moved — slowly, gathering, the way someone listened who was used to things telling him things in their own time. She thought about the carving he'd been working on, the hardwood piece, the abstract curve seeming like it had been trying to become something for a long time.

"He's not with the bloodline," she said. Not a question.

"No." Vane said it with the certainty of her nose behind it. "He's not from the city either. He came in from the south, same as you, probably within a week of when you arrived. He's been working the docks." She looked at Maren steadily. "He came to New Plymouth the same way you did — without a plan except north."

Maren sat with this.

"The symbol," she said. "He found it the same way I found it."

"That's what it looked like."

"Which means he has a piece we don't."

Vane said nothing, which was its own answer.

Maren thought about the disc in her pocket, her father's letter, the hill to the east. She thought about someone else moving north toward the same question by a different road, arriving at the same city, sitting on the same dock without knowing why. She thought about what it meant that two people were circling the same thing independently — whether it meant the thing was important enough to draw people toward it, or whether it meant something was directing them there.

She wasn't sure those were different possibilities.

"I should talk to him," she said.

"Yes," Vane said. "But not yet."

"Why not yet?"

"Because Horst arrived yesterday and you haven't looked at how that changes the dock as a location." She said it evenly. "Three nights ago it was a reasonable place to sit. Today it's exposed, high-traffic, difficult to exit quickly from. We find a better place first. Then you talk to him."

Maren looked at her. "You've already found the better place."

Something in Vane's face gave slightly, the way a locked thing gives when the right pressure is applied. "There's a cooperage on the river road, two streets west of the dock. It has a yard. The yard has a gate on the far side opening onto a service alley. The alley runs north." She paused. "The man who owns the cooperage doesn't ask questions if you buy something first."

Maren thought about the operational precision of this — the pre-surveyed exit, the cover purchase, the route already mapped. Then she thought about the young man at the dock, his slow careful attention, the symbol in the driftwood.

"How do I find him?" she said.

"He goes to the dock at the same time most evenings. I'll tell you when the pattern is safe to use again." Vane met her eyes. "Two more days. Maybe three. Then you talk to him."

It was not how Maren would have done it. She would have gone back to the dock tonight, found him, had the conversation. The urgency of having found a piece she didn't have felt like something that shouldn't wait.

She thought about Vane walking back toward her on the road south of Stillwater, the decision already made, the reasons given in the right order. She thought about the door in the night at Betta's farmstead, and the ditch-grass beside the road, and Horst now four districts away and narrowing.

"Two days," she said.

"Maybe three."

She turned to the window. The alley below was empty, the city going about its business in the middle distance, indifferent and enormous and full of things she didn't know yet.

Patience was a skill like any other. She was learning it late. Vane had been doing this for ten years and was still alive, and the patience she was asking for was the same patience she'd shown on the road south of Stillwater, and that had been the right call.

She sat down at the table.

"Tell me what you know about the city's eastern roads," she said. "If we're going to the hill, I want to understand what's between here and there."

Vane looked at her for a moment. Then she pulled the maps across the table.

The eastern route was rough. Vane traced it without sentiment: five or six days on foot through increasingly difficult country before you reached anything close to the hill country, the terrain rising into foothills that became genuinely wild before Irongate territory began. The eastern gate was watched, had been for weeks by Vane's assessment. The ford two miles west of it was the way out — less trafficked, no permanent post.

They worked until the lamp needed oil, and then Vane refilled it and they worked some more, and outside the city moved through its evening and the river smell came through the window and the moons rose one by one over the eastern roofline, and neither of them mentioned what they were preparing for except in the practical language of distances and road conditions and the eastern gate and the ford two miles west of it.

It was enough. For now, it was enough.

## Chapter Fifteen — *[Davan]*
### The Cooperage Basement

---

He went back to Edvar on the following Thursday, and the Thursday after that.

The second visit he brought a piece he'd been working on — not to show off, not as payment, but because he'd learned from watching his father that the best way to establish yourself with a craftsman was to bring your own work and let it speak without commentary. Edvar had looked at it for a long time without speaking, turning it in his hands the way he'd turned Davan's first carving, and then set it on the worktable and said: "You trained under someone."

"My father. He made furniture."

"The grain discipline is a furniture-maker's discipline." Edvar handed the piece back. "What do you know about pre-Chrysalis material culture?"

A test framed as a question. Davan told Edvar what he knew: the standard temple account of the Founding, the Fathers' Boats tradition from the river communities, the way the inner orbit symbol predated the Chrysalis iconography by a margin scholars acknowledged without examining closely. He told it plainly, without elaboration, and when he'd finished he stopped.

Edvar looked at him. "What do you think the symbol describes?"

"Something in orbit," Davan said. "Around something fixed. I think it's counting."

"Counting what?"

"I don't know yet."

Edvar nodded slowly, the nod of someone absorbing an answer that was right as far as it went. He didn't show Davan anything else that visit. But when Davan left, Edvar said: "Thursday after next. Come in the afternoon."

On the third Thursday, Edvar showed him two maps. Both old, both with the margin notation. Both with different numbers beside the symbol — one with three, one with four.

"Different dates?" Davan asked.

"Different sources," Edvar said. "Possibly different dates. I can't be certain." He looked at the maps. "The one with four is older. Significantly, I think. The one with three was made — I believe — after something changed."

"What changed?"

Edvar rolled the maps and returned them to their rack. "That," he said, "is the question I've spent twenty years on." He turned back to Davan. "I'm going to take you somewhere on Thursday evening. After you come here."

He spread the eastern highlands map flat one more time before rolling it, and Davan looked at it with fresh attention — the distance from New Plymouth's northern coast to the foothills, the river system as a kind of measuring line, the way the contour lines thickened toward Irongate country. Five or six days' hard travel, he thought. He'd estimated it from Edvar's first map and found he'd been right. The hill, wherever it sat in those eastern foothills, was five or six days from where he stood, and the path to it was increasingly rough, increasingly remote, increasingly the kind of country that asked something of you before it showed you anything. He filed this not as a fact but as a felt distance — the way a long walk home settled in your legs before your mind knew it.

---

The cooperage was two streets from the river, on a lane smelling of oak shavings and vinegar and the sweetness of new wood soaking in water. A working building, honest about what it was, the sign above the door plain and businesslike. Davan had walked past it three times without paying it attention, which he suspected was the point.

Pip met him at the corner. He'd said nothing specific about what they were doing — only that Edvar had decided, and that Davan should buy a hoop-set at the front counter before going through to the back. Davan bought the hoop-set, which he had no use for, and the cooper's apprentice took his coin and nodded him through a door at the back of the shop without looking at him directly, its own kind of communication.

Down a set of stairs. A low-ceilinged room — stone floor, damp at the edges where the wall met the ground, the air carrying the cooperage's vinegar-and-oak up from the shop above but mixing it with something older underneath, the mineral cold of a space that had been below ground level for a long time and had settled into its own climate. Two lamps on the table, not enough for the corners, so the room's edges dissolved into a dimness that made it feel smaller than it was. A table and seven chairs, five of them occupied. Pip behind him. Edvar already seated, which surprised him — he hadn't seen Edvar leave the shop.

He sat against the wall, his back to the cold stone, and felt the room's temperature settle through his coat. He was new here.

---

The woman who ran the meeting was perhaps fifty, with the aggressive precision of someone who had spent years being the most informed person in a room and had long since stopped apologizing for it. Her name was Renner. She'd been a temple archivist for twenty years before leaving — or being asked to leave, a distinction she seemed to consider a matter of framing rather than fact — after spending too long in the eastern catalog asking the wrong questions about the Restricted Collection.

She opened by challenging the cartographer across the table before anyone had fully settled.

"You've revised your date on the Irongate inscription again," she said. Not a question.

The cartographer — young, sharp-featured, ink stains on his right hand there probably since apprenticeship — looked up with the expression of someone expecting this. "The stratigraphy supports a pre-Chrysalis date. I've said that for two years."

"The stratigraphy supports ambiguity. That's different from support."

"Ambiguity that falls within the pre-Chrysalis window."

"Ambiguity that *could* fall within—"

"Renner." This from the tanner, a square-built man named Corvel with the deliberate speech of someone who had learned to measure words in public and hadn't fully released the habit in private. "We've had this argument."

"We've had it because it matters," Renner said, without heat. She had the quality of someone who had long ago separated argument from animosity and found the separation useful. She leaned forward as she spoke, both forearms on the table, and the nearer lamp caught the lines of her face in a way that made her look like someone who had been having arguments in low-ceilinged rooms for a very long time and intended to continue. "If the inscription is pre-Chrysalis, it suggests the symbol predates the Founding. If it's contemporary, it only suggests the symbol was absorbed into Chrysalis iconography early. Those are different claims."

"They both suggest the symbol existed before the current theology made it mean what it means," the cartographer said. He had not leaned forward. He sat with one hand flat on the table and the other holding a stylus he wasn't using, turning it between his fingers — the habit of someone who thought with objects in his hands.

"They suggest very different things about what the symbol was originally for."

Somewhere above them the cooperage floor creaked — a worker shifting weight, or the building settling, or both — and the sound travelled through the stone ceiling with the particular muffled quality of a room that existed underneath something else. Nobody looked up. They'd been meeting here long enough that the building's sounds were part of the room's furniture.

Davan sat against the wall and listened and tried to map the shape of what they were arguing about. He'd been in the room for three minutes and could already feel the texture of something running for a long time — an argument with layers, the kind where the surface disagreement was about dates and stratigraphy but underneath was something more fundamental. Whether the symbol was astronomical or theological. Whether the Chrysalis theology had absorbed something it understood or something it had simply found.

He kept his mouth shut and paid attention.

---

The woman who spoke next had not introduced herself. Younger than Renner by perhaps twenty years, with the particular watchfulness of someone who spent more time observing than speaking and had learned things from both. She had a quality Davan associated with people who worked with their hands — a directness of gaze, an assessment moving from the face to the hands and back. She'd come in after him and sat at the far end of the table, saying nothing for the first quarter-hour while Renner and the cartographer worked through their argument.

When she spoke, it was to Corvel. "You brought the container."

"I always bring the container," Corvel said. He produced it from his coat and set it on the table with a care that was habitual rather than dramatic — the practiced handling of something he'd carried for years without fully understanding, which was its own form of respect.

Smooth, gray, the length of a forearm, sealed, a material nobody had ever identified. In the lamplight it had no sheen, absorbed the light rather than reflecting it, which gave it the quality of a hole in the table's surface — present, solid, but somehow refusing to participate in the room's illumination.

The cartographer took it first, turned it twice with professional attention, passed it to Renner. She held it the way she held everything — with assessment rather than reverence, both hands, testing the weight. She ran her thumbnail along the seam and set it down and pushed it across to Edvar, who touched it briefly, nodded, and slid it on. When it reached Davan he held it and felt again what he'd felt at his first sight of it across the table — not quite emotion, not quite sensation, something in the register between them. A proximity. Something inside not inert in the way sealed objects usually were. His skin-light stirred faintly under his coat sleeves, responding to the proximity before he could suppress it. He turned slightly toward the wall.

He passed it to his left, to the younger woman at the far end, who took it with both hands and did not pass it on.

"The seam," she said — she was a glassblower, Davan would learn later, explaining both the hand-calluses and the quality of her assessment. "It's not a joint. There's no adhesive, no weld. It's more like the material itself closes."

"We've established that," Renner said.

"I know we've established it. I'm saying it again because it's relevant to whether the closure mechanism is mechanical or material." She turned the container in her hands. "If it's mechanical, there's a key or a sequence. If it's material — if the substance itself creates the seal — then it might respond to something the material recognizes rather than something that manipulates it physically."

"Such as."

"I don't know. Something that belongs to whoever made it, maybe. Or whoever it was made for."

A small silence fell. Not the silence of agreement — more the silence of people who have heard something that bumps against a question they've been not-quite-asking.

"The bloodline," the cartographer said.

"Or the bloodless," Renner said.

The words landed differently from what Davan had expected — not as a correction, but as a position in an ongoing disagreement. He looked at her. She was watching the container move around the table.

"The Restricted Collection footnotes," she said, in the tone of someone making an argument made before, "refer consistently to a bloodline capacity. The ability to interact with — " she paused, choosing words with the precision of someone who knew how imprecision had cost them before " — objects of the Founding period. Not a bloodless capacity. A bloodline capacity."

"Those are the same bloodline's footnotes," the glassblower said.

"Which is relevant but not disqualifying."

"It's extremely relevant. You're using their account of what they can do as evidence for what they can do."

"I'm using their account as evidence for what they believed they could do, which tells us something about the objects even if it tells us nothing reliable about the bloodline."

Renner set her hands flat on the table — deliberately, the gesture of someone about to lay something out — and looked at the container where it had come to rest in front of her. The nearer lamp guttered once in a draft from the stairwell and the shadows in the room shifted and resettled, and in that brief rearrangement of light the container seemed to darken further, as though it had drawn the flickering into itself.

"Consider what authority actually rests on. Not the genealogy. Not the doctrine. The physical reality." She paused. Around the table, the quality of attention changed — the cartographer's stylus went still, Corvel's hands came together on the table's edge, Edvar straightened slightly in the way of someone who has been waiting for a particular point to arrive. "The bloodline's authority has one moment of absolute demonstration, and it was three hundred years ago at the Stilling of the Brace. Every scholar in this room knows the story. A weapon used once — something from the Founding period, recovered and kept secured. It left a mark on the palace steps that people with the Chrysalis cannot look at directly. Full stop. That event is the capstone of the bloodline's claim." She looked around the table — face by face, a slow sweep that included Davan at the wall, acknowledging his presence without explaining it. "And that weapon, the container Corvel carries, the navigation instruments in the restricted archive — they may all be the same category of object. Made of the same material. Designed for the same hands."

"Unchanged hands," Corvel said quietly. He was looking at his own hands on the table as he said it.

"Yes. The bloodline's authority rests on being the only unchanged people — unchanged by the Chrysalis, unchanged by whatever spread from those who came on the fourth boat. They can operate the objects. No one else can." She looked at the glassblower. "That's the argument."

The glassblower — Asha — was quiet for a moment. She had the container in front of her still, one hand resting on it, and she did not take her hand away. The lamp threw her shadow long against the stone wall behind her.

Then: "The bloodless."

"What about them?"

"They're also unchanged," she said. "By definition. If the objects respond to hands unchanged by the Chrysalis — if that's the actual qualification, not bloodline descent, not genealogy, but absence of change — then the bloodless would qualify." She turned the container in her hands one more time — slowly, with the attention of someone who worked with material for a living and understood what it meant to hold something that refused to behave like anything she'd handled before — and set it down in the center of the table, where both lamps caught it equally and it absorbed them equally. "The bloodline's monopoly on operating these objects rests entirely on being the only unchanged people. But they're not. The bloodless are also unchanged."

The room was quiet.

Not the ordinary quiet of people thinking. Something heavier than that — the particular stillness of a group of people who have arrived, collectively, at a question they hadn't fully assembled before this moment, and who are now sitting with what it means. The cold from the stone floor rose through the soles of Davan's boots. The vinegar-oak smell of the cooperage settled around them like the room itself was listening.

Davan looked at the container on the table. He thought about the proximity-sense he'd felt when he held it — the warmth that wasn't heat, the reach of something inside toward something outside. He thought about the girl on the dock with her disc. He thought about the eastern foothills and the five or six days between here and whatever was there, and the hands that might or might not be able to open it.

He kept his mouth shut. He was new here. But he turned the question over, and it was warm, and it was real.

---

Afterward, in the lane outside, Pip fell into step beside him.

"Well?" Pip said.

"The container," Davan said. "Corvel's had it for how long?"

"Eight years. He brought it to the group two years ago."

"Has anything ever happened when someone touched it? Any kind of response?"

Pip looked at him sideways. "Not that anyone's mentioned. Why?"

Davan thought about the proximity-sense, the not-quite-warmth. He wasn't sure how to describe it without describing his mutation, and he wasn't ready for that with people he'd spent three hours beside. "Just a thought," he said.

Pip accepted this with the equanimity of someone who had learned to accept half-answers from people who'd eventually give him the rest. "What did you make of the argument? Renner and Asha."

"The glassblower's Asha?"

"Yes."

"I think they're both right about different parts of it." Davan turned this over. "Renner's right that the Restricted Collection tells us something about the objects even if it lies about the bloodline. Asha's right that the bloodline's account of their own abilities is the least reliable evidence you could use." He paused. "The thing I keep coming back to is the number changing. Four to three. Something happened. Whoever made the old map knew something had changed and recorded it in the margin as a count."

"That's Renner's position," Pip said. "The number is astronomical. Something changed in the sky."

"And the cartographer thinks it's theological? The count of something in the Chrysalis framework?"

"He thinks it could be either, and that treating it as definitely astronomical is overclaiming."

"He's wrong about that." Davan said it without thinking, and then considered whether he believed it, and found that he did. "It's astronomical. I've been watching the moons for months. The symbol is the same geometry as the path of the moons. It's not a coincidence."

Pip was quiet for a moment. "Renner will want to hear that. She's been arguing it for four years based on the maps. She'd appreciate someone arriving at it independently." He paused. "Though she'll also want to know why you're certain, and 'I've been watching the moons' is not going to satisfy her."

"I know." Davan looked at the lane, the lamplight, the shadow of the cooperage behind them. "I have something else. But I need to think about whether I'm ready to bring it."

Pip nodded slowly. He knew what to press and what to leave, and he left this.

"There's something else," Pip said after a moment. He stopped walking. "Edvar told me before you arrived tonight. Someone came to his shop last week. A woman. Alone, direct, not from the city. She asked about old maps of the eastern highlands."

Davan went still.

"He didn't show her anything. She wasn't someone he knew." Pip met his eyes. "He described her. Dark hair. Road-worn. Young." A pause. "She had something in her coat pocket she kept touching. He couldn't see what."

"He said she knew what she was asking," Pip said. "Not browsing. Already had a piece and was looking for the next one." Another pause. "She bought a map of the eastern roads north of the river fork. Paid with a Stillwater coin."

Davan said nothing.

"I think she's connected to whatever you're connected to."

Davan thought about the dock. The fading light. The disc angled to catch it, and the symbol on its edge identical to what he'd carved in driftwood. The way she'd held her arm — close, guarded, exactly the posture his hands had carved into the pine figure weeks before he'd met her. The carving in his coat pocket he'd been circling for weeks, the one with the arm that wouldn't hang right, that he still hadn't fully looked at since the dock because looking at it now meant reckoning with the fact that his hands had known something about her before the rest of him was ready.

He was done waiting to be ready.

"I don't want her address," he said.

Pip looked at him. "I wasn't going to offer it."

"Good." He thought for a moment. "She went to Edvar without anyone setting it up. She's running her own search. If I show up at her door, I'm everything she's been avoiding." He turned the carving over in his coat pocket, feeling the shape of it through the cloth. "Set something up through the cooperage. A time, a place, a gate she can leave through if she wants. And send her something she'll recognize."

"What?"

He took the pine carving out and looked at it in the lamplight for a long moment. The small figure, head tilted as if listening, one arm held close to the body in the guarded posture his hands had insisted on and he'd never been able to correct. He'd tried to fix the arm three times on the road north and it had come back each time, and now he knew why it had come back — because it was right, because somewhere ahead of him on the road a girl had been holding her arm exactly like this for three years, and his hands had found the shape of that gesture before the rest of him had any reason to know it existed.

He had no explanation for this. He was done looking for one.

He held it out to Pip.

"She has the disc," he said. "If she looks at this she'll either understand or she won't. Either way we'll know something useful."

Pip took it and looked at it and looked at Davan and looked at it again. He didn't ask. He was, in his way, as good at not-asking as Davan was at not-finishing thoughts, and for the same reasons: some things told you what they were when they were ready, and pressing only made them go quiet.

"Thursday," Pip said.

"Thursday," Davan agreed.

He walked back toward the flophouse with his hands in his empty pockets, and the emptiness of the one pocket felt right — not a loss but a release, like a breath let out after holding it. He thought about the argument he'd sat through, the positions staked out over years, the number changing from four to three — and the one that fell, the one nobody in the room had said out loud but everyone had been thinking around — and the thing in Corvel's container reaching toward him when he held it, and Asha's question sitting in the room's silence like something that had needed asking for three hundred years. He thought about unchanged hands and what they could open.

He didn't know yet. He was beginning to believe that not knowing yet was a condition, not a conclusion. You moved through it. The rest came.
