

**WHAT THE MOONS KEEP**

*Prologue: What Was Remembered*

Being Several Accounts of the First Coming,

as Collected, Contradicted, and Incompletely Preserved

*The following fragments were gathered from temple archives, traveling singers, condemned libraries, and the mouths of the very old. They do not agree with each other. They have never agreed with each other. This has not stopped anyone from dying over them.*

I.  FROM THE RECITATION OF FIRST LIGHT — GRAND TEMPLE OF THE CHRYSALIS, NEW PLYMOUTH

In the age before the Awakening there was only the hollow dark, and in the hollow dark there was nothing that breathed and nothing that changed and nothing that knew its own name.

And the Three Witnesses moved through the dark, and they saw the world below, and they said: here. Here is where it begins.

And the First Light came down from the Witnesses and touched the soil of Gaius, and where it touched, the Chrysalis waited. It had always waited. It was patient beyond the understanding of any living thing, because it was older than living things, older than the names for old.

The Founders arrived in the time of First Light. They were not yet themselves. They were seeds.

The Chrysalis entered them. The weak ones burned. The strong ones opened. The ones who opened became the first true people of Gaius, changed and awake and named by what they now carried inside them.

A small number would not open. These were not destroyed, for the Chrysalis is merciful. They were set apart, that they might perform the work that hands-which-change cannot perform. They tend the old things. They carry the old burdens. They serve.

The Three Witnesses remain above us still. They do not leave. They have never left. They watch our Bloomings and our dyings and our wars and they do not intervene, because they gave us everything we needed in the First Light and the rest is ours to do with.

This is the truth. It has always been the truth. To say otherwise is to invite the hollow dark back in.

*— Recited at every Blooming ceremony. First written down, approximately, seven hundred years ago.   Original oral composition: unknown.*

II.  AS TOLD BY NANA BRINE OF THE GREYWOOD VALLEY — TRANSCRIBED BY HER GRANDDAUGHTER, WILLA

She always started the same way. She'd bank the fire down low and say: the people didn't come from here. That's the important part that everyone forgets. The people came from somewhere else. She'd never say where. I asked her once and she said she didn't know, and nobody knew, and that not-knowing was a very old hole in the world that everyone had learned to step around.

She said they came in something. She couldn't say what. Something big. Something that moved through the dark the way fish move through water, she said, easy and purposeful and not concerned with the dark at all.

And then it stopped being easy. And then there was fire. And then there was Gaius.

She always made the landing sound like an accident. Not a punishment, not a miracle. An accident. I asked her if that made her sad, and she said: it made her think. Because accidents are no one's fault and everyone's problem, and there's something honest in that.

She said the people were very frightened. She said the world was not like wherever they'd come from. She said the ground itself got into them, the air got into them, the water got into them, and they were never the same after and most of them didn't mind because they didn't remember being different.

The ones who did remember different were the ones who didn't change. She felt sorry for them. She said they were like people who remember a dream so clearly it makes the waking world look thin.

I asked her about the moons.

She was quiet for a while. Then she said: your great-grandmother's grandmother's grandmother — some number of grandmothers back, she couldn't count them — said the moons were waiting for something. Not watching. Waiting.

I asked what they were waiting for.

She banked the fire back up and said it was time for bed.

*— Willa Brine, written down "so I wouldn't forget it wrong." Undated.*

III.  THE IRONGATE RECOUNTING — WARRIOR-CULT ORAL TRADITION, EASTERN MARCHES

Three gods fell. That is how it started.

The first god struck the earth and died and its body became the eastern hill country. Its blood went into the rock and from the rock into the rivers and from the rivers into the people. That is the Chrysalis. A god's blood, cut with river water and time.

The second, third, and fourth gods did not fall. They would not fall. They circled the sky where the first had fallen and they circle it still, and sometimes when the light is right you can see they are angry. You can feel it in your blood when they are full-bright.

The people who drank the god's blood changed. Good. Change is the only honest answer to a hard world.

Some people did not change. They were not gods. They were the servants the first god brought with it to do the carrying and the keeping. They are still servants. There is no shame in this. Someone has to carry things.

In the old songs they are called the Unblooded. The songs are not cruel about this. The Unblooded were useful. They could touch the god's old bones and weapons without burning. They kept the old things working until the old things stopped working anyway.

Then there was nothing for them to keep. So they became something else. What, the songs don't say.

*— Compiled from multiple singer-recitations in the eastern marches.   Estimated oral tradition depth: two to three thousand years at minimum.*

IV.  THE TIDEMARK TELLING — FISHER COMMUNITIES, COASTAL TRADITION

My father's father's people say it plain: the first ones came on ships.

Not like our ships. Ships that made our ships look like a child's toy boat pushed across a puddle with one finger. Ships that held hundreds of people. Ships that moved through the dark between the lights in the sky, because the people then knew how to sail that dark the way we know how to sail the gray water.

Four ships. One of them came down. Not a good landing. A bad one. The kind where you give up the ship to save the people, and even then you don't save all the people.

The other two ships — they didn't leave. Think about that. Every sailor knows what a ship does when she loses one of her own at sea. You don't leave. You stay. You circle. You call their name into the water until the water answers or it doesn't.

That's what they did. They circled. They called.

The answer, if there was one, none of us have heard.

This is why my family, when we lose someone to the water, we don't look down. We look up. And when all three lights are full-bright together, you can feel it in your chest if you're honest, that old patient grief that doesn't belong to you personally but is yours anyway because it's been handed down so many times that it's worn itself into the shape of a thing you were born knowing.

*— Spoken by a Tidemark harbor-master in his cups. Written down by a temple record-keeper   who included the note: "This man has had too much to drink. Also: I cannot stop thinking about what he said."*

V.  A CHILDREN'S COUNTING RHYME — WIDESPREAD, ORIGIN UNKNOWN

*Four fires in the sky,*

*one came down to die,*

*three stayed up to cry,*

*now you're born and so am I.*

*Skin like bark and eyes like glass,*

*roots go deep and years go fast,*

*but the ones who didn’t change*

*walk the old halls, strange and strange.*

*Don’t touch the dark, don’t touch the door,*

*don’t go asking what it’s for,*

*some things hum and some things burn*

*and the rest of it you'll never learn.*

*— Used in skipping games. Regional variants recorded across Thera with minor differences.   Considered by the Temple to be harmless folk tradition.*

VI.  FRAGMENT FROM A CONDEMNED TEXT — AUTHOR UNKNOWN, TITLE UNKNOWN, DATE UNKNOWN   RECOVERED FROM A PRIVATE COLLECTION, BINDING FIRE-DAMAGED

The honest version, as best I can reconstruct it from what the stories agree on when you strip out the parts that feel like someone's wish:

People arrived. From somewhere not here. They arrived badly. Something was lost. A great deal was lost. The world affected them in ways they had no words for, and by the time they had words for it the words were already the wrong shape for what had actually happened.

The ones who were not affected by the world had an advantage: they could still use the things they'd brought with them. Tools. Objects. Things that required hands that had not been changed by the world to operate.

This made them valuable. For a while. Then it made them powerful. Then the things they could operate began to fail, one by one, over centuries, and their power became only the power of claiming they still had it. And people believed them, because people had been believing them for so long that the belief itself had become the thing.

This is not complicated. This is how power works. You establish legitimacy in a moment of genuine usefulness, and then the usefulness fades, and what remains is the structure of the claim. It is not a conspiracy so much as a forgetting-to-correct-the-record, repeated across a thousand years until the record and the forgetting are the same thing.

The moons watch. They have always watched. I do not know what they see. I suspect what they see is people who have forgotten what they themselves were, telling other people confident and beautiful lies about it.

I do not think this is malicious. I think it is just what happens to a truth when it is left alone in the dark for long enough. It begins to glow. And everyone mistakes the glow for the original thing.

*— Remainder of text missing. Temple notation on the cover reads:   "Examined, condemned, destruction ordered." Below this, in different ink: "Why is this so hard to burn?"*

VII.  FROM THE PRIVATE DEVOTIONAL OF THE ROYAL HOUSE — NOT FOR DISTRIBUTION   RECITED AT SUCCESSION RITES. TEXT HELD ONLY WITHIN THE HOUSEHOLD.

We are the children of the Keeper. The first Keeper. She who held the keys to the fire and refused to let it die.

Before the Chrysalis. Before the people who changed. Before all of it. She was here, and she kept the fire, and the fire kept the people, and so we are here.

We do not change because we are the changers. We hold the old things because we are old things. We do not ask the question of what we came from because we carry what we came from in the metal and the light and the hum of the old weapon, which hums because it still remembers what it was made for and will not forget.

The changed ones are our people. We love them as you love anything that grows under your hand. We guide them. We protect them. We do not tell them everything, because a keeper does not give away the fire all at once. You give it in amounts that can be used.

The moons are the moons. They are ours. They are not for anyone else.

The hollow ones who are born outside our line are mistakes of nature, frightening to themselves, to be found and brought in or else handled. The Keeper does not leave the keys where anyone can pick them up. You tend the fire or you smother it, and there is no third option, and we have not survived ten thousand years by pretending otherwise.

This is not cruelty. This is tending. We have always tended.

Remember the first Keeper. Remember what she gave up. Remember what we carry for everyone who cannot carry it themselves, and be grateful for the weight, because the weight means you are still standing.

*— Passed down orally for most of its history. First written transcription within living memory.   Access restricted to blood members of the royal household.*

VIII.  UNDATED, UNSIGNED, FOUND SCRATCHED INTO THE BACK OF A WAYPOINT SHRINE EAST OF NEW PLYMOUTH

The moons are not moons.

Ask your blood what it remembers.

Then go east.

There is a hill shaped wrong.

# What the Moons Keep

## Chapter One — *[Maren]*
### The Ritual of the Second Skin

---

The herb woman's kitchen smelled of drying things — fennel and coltsfoot and the sharp ghost of whatever she'd pressed the week before, a scent Maren could never name but knew the way she knew her own hands. She was grinding dried bark into a paste that was supposed to help with joint-ache. She had been doing this since early morning and her arm was tired and she was thinking about nothing in particular, which was a skill she'd worked on for three years and was finally getting good at.

"You're too hard on it," Delia said, without looking up from the linen she was hemming. "The paste wants to be coaxed. Not beaten."

"I'm coaxing aggressively."

"That's just beating."

Maren eased off. Outside the window the valley was doing what it always did in early autumn — going gold-gray, the high grasses bending east under a wind that had traveled a long way to be cold by the time it arrived. Ashwick sat in a shallow bowl of plateau country, basalt ridges on three sides, the fourth open to a sky so wide it sometimes made Maren feel like the ground was tilting. She had grown up thinking this was normal. She still thought it was normal. It was just that she'd also learned to not look at the sky for too long on certain days.

Today was one of those days.

The Autumnal Rite was at sundown.

She put the mortar down and flexed her grinding hand and looked at the bandage on her left forearm. White linen, neatly wound, changed every third day because the deception had to be complete to be convincing and a clean bandage was one less question. Beneath the linen was not a wound. The wound was a story she'd told three years ago — a burning accident, a healer's visit, a scar that hadn't settled right, still sensitive to cold. People were incurious about other people's pain. She'd been counting on that and it had held.

Beneath the bandage, tonight, she would need to place the coal pouch.

She'd made it herself the first year. A small leather bag, flat, no bigger than her palm, filled with charcoal wrapped in wool-cloth so it held heat without smelling of smoke. She wore it close to the skin during the Rite, inside the bandage, against the forearm. If anyone touched her arm they felt warmth. If anyone looked at her face when the candles were lit and the changes were on display around her, they saw someone who was concentrating, which could mean anything.

It worked because nobody really looked. That was the first thing she'd understood: people at the Rite were looking inward, at their own changes, at the thing inside them that had opened when they were fourteen or fifteen and had been opening further every year since. They were not looking at a seventeen-year-old girl who smelled of fennel and kept her arm close to her side.

The second thing she'd understood: the Rite wasn't about proving anything. It was about belonging to the proving together. A hundred people in a field, feeling what they felt, and the particular comfort of not feeling it alone. She was never going to have that. She had the next best thing, which was knowing how to stand in the shape of it.

"Deacon's arriving at midday," Delia said. "From New Plymouth. He'll want feeding."

"I know. I put the salt-fish on last night."

Delia looked at her then — that particular look she'd been perfecting for three years alongside Maren's deception, the look that said *I am not going to say what I'm thinking about*. It was a warm look. It had a lot of weight underneath it. Maren had learned to receive it without flinching.

"He'll be stricter than Brother Aldous," Delia said. "They always are, when they come from the city."

"I'll be fine."

"I know you will." Another version of the look. "I'm just saying."

Maren went back to her grinding. She coaxed aggressively. The paste began to smell like something that might actually help a person's joints.

---

The deacon's name was Ferris. He was perhaps thirty-five, lean in the way of a man who traveled a great deal and ate when it was convenient rather than when he was hungry. He wore the Chrysalis mark tattooed on the backs of both hands — the spiraling symbol that priests got when they took their formal vows, meant to show the change-within-change, the Awakening that chose the devoted as surely as it chose everyone else. His own change, whatever it was, didn't show on the surface. A lot of the serious ones were like that. The mutations that went inward were supposed to be the deepest.

He ate the salt-fish without comment and asked about the village's yield and whether there'd been any trouble on the eastern road. He was pleasant. Maren watched him the way she watched everything she wasn't sure about, which was from slightly to the side, attention present and apparent interest withheld.

He asked about her arm over the meal.

Not aggressively. Conversationally. He asked what had happened and she told the burning story and he nodded and said he hoped it had healed well and moved on. She ate another bite of bread. Under the table she pressed her thumbnail into the pad of her finger until the small pain steadied her.

Later she overheard him speaking with Elder Torvann in the yard, and she heard him say, in the tone of a man making an administrative observation: "The girl. The herb woman's girl. She was here for the last Rite?"

She couldn't hear Torvann's answer. She went back inside and finished the salt-fish and washed the plate and told herself that a man from New Plymouth would ask questions about everything as a matter of professional habit. She told herself this was not specific. She had been telling herself things were not specific for three years. She was very good at it. She did not quite believe it today.

---

They lit the fires at sundown. Eight fires in a ring on the flat ground east of the village, and between the fires the people of Ashwick stood in their best clothes with their changes on display, and the deacon moved among them with the ceremonial candle and spoke the words of the Rite in the old formal accent that the city temples had preserved and the country temples had let soften into something more like ordinary speech.

Maren stood in her place in the ring. Coal pouch warm against her forearm. Bandage crisp and clean.

To her left: Bram the blacksmith, whose skin had been going gradually to a dark burnished copper for four years now, the change moving up his arms toward his shoulders, beautiful in firelight. He was proud of it in a quiet way that Maren found genuinely admirable. He'd never asked for a smith's change. He'd gotten one anyway. He'd built his work around it instead of the other way.

To her right: Jory, who was fifteen and newly opened and still adjusting. His eyes had gone reflective — not unpleasant, silver at the edges in low light, but they unsettled him in mirrors and he kept looking at the ground. His mother had her hand on his shoulder. He was shaking slightly in the way of someone trying hard not to.

Maren had watched people open their whole life. She knew what it looked like from the outside: strange and sacred and a little frightening and then, after, ordinary. A girl at the Rite two years back had grown a second set of small fingers partway through the ceremony, which had caused a great stir and much weeping of joy. The fingers were useful now. She sewed with them.

It was real. Maren had never once doubted that it was real. She had doubted a great many other things. She had doubted the stories about why it happened and what it meant and whether the three moons were witnesses or something else entirely. She had her private doubts about Deacon Ferris. She did not doubt the changes, because she had seen them happen to people she knew and they had not been pretending.

She just was not among them.

She never had been.

The deacon's candle moved through the ring. Each person held their hands out or showed their change or simply stood in the warmth of what they carried, and the deacon touched the candle briefly to their joined hands and said the words and moved on. The ritual had a rhythm to it. Maren knew the rhythm. She settled into it the way she settled into any performance: spine straight, face composed, attention on the specific moment rather than the whole shape of what she was doing.

He reached Bram. The words. The candle.

He reached Jory. Longer here — a first Rite, the boy shaking — and something the deacon said made Jory look up from the ground, and his reflective eyes caught the firelight, and his mother made a soft sound.

He reached Maren.

She held out her hands, the bandaged left arm extended, the warmth of the coal pouch radiating up through the linen the way it always did. She met his eyes because looking away was the wrong thing to do. She had learned this young: when you are hiding something, you look at people. Looking away is the thing that looks like hiding.

He looked at her hands.

He looked at her face.

He looked at her hands again, and this time the quality of the looking was different. Not the general pastoral attention of a priest moving through a ceremony. Something precise. Something that had already decided to look and was now simply confirming.

He placed the ceremonial candle very close to her arm.

Not touching. Close. The flame guttered in the wind and threw a little heat and that heat, added to the coal pouch's warmth, was one thing too many — the pouch shifted when she didn't expect it, and the small weight of it moved wrong against her forearm, and she felt it before she saw it, the slight change in how the linen sat, and she knew before he did.

The pouch slid.

The linen caught on itself and the small leather bag dropped, visible for just a moment at the cuff of her sleeve, before she got her arm down and her opposite hand over it.

Just a moment. A breath.

The deacon looked at her cuff. Then at her face.

She looked back at him. She had nothing to offer him. Her face was doing whatever it was doing and she had no instructions for it, no prepared expression, because she had not prepared for this because she had been doing this for three years without incident and she had let herself stop preparing.

The silence lasted long enough to be a different kind of word.

Then he said the words of the Rite in the normal voice, touched the candle near her hands, and moved on to Bram's cousin on her left.

Maren stood in the ring until it was over. She sang the responses. She bowed her head at the correct moments. Her hands were steady. She was very cold.

---

She did not go to sleep.

She lay in her pallet in the room off Delia's kitchen and listened to the house. She heard Delia bank the fire. She heard the footsteps of the deacon's allotted guest-room, overhead, pacing. She heard the wind off the plateau pushing at the shutters the way it always did this time of year, testing them with an impersonal thoroughness, not malicious, just thorough.

She heard, an hour after the house had settled, the low sound of voices in the kitchen.

She moved to the wall. The old construction of Ashwick's buildings was good for this: the interior walls were wood panel over stone and sound moved through the gap between them if you pressed your ear to exactly the right spot. She had found the spot at thirteen, before the deception, when she'd been a child doing what children do with walls. She had used it many times since.

Delia's voice first. Low and careful, the voice she used when she was frightened but was managing the fear with her hands, doing something while she spoke. The clatter of a cup.

The deacon's voice in response. She caught fragments: "...not certain, but the weight on the arm, the way it..." and then something she couldn't hear, and then her own name.

Delia said something sharp. Maren had never heard her speak that way. It lasted only a moment before she modulated back to careful.

The deacon said: "...bloodless."

The wind pushed at the shutters.

He said, or she thought he said — the wall, the wind, she could not be fully certain: "The bloodline has asked to be told."

Delia said nothing for a moment. Then she said something very quiet that Maren couldn't make out at all.

Then Delia cried. Not loudly. The way she cried when she thought she was alone, which was muffled and controlled and private, the grief of someone who had learned to take up as little space as possible with their pain.

Maren sat against the wall for a long time after the voices stopped.

*Bloodless.* She had heard the word before, always in the context of a bad thing, a sad thing, said with the slightly lowered voice people used for the afflicted. She had always understood it meant what she was. She had not, until now, understood that someone was supposed to be told.

She thought about her father.

He had left on a night like this one, windy, late autumn. She had been twelve. She'd woken to find him gone and her mother sitting at the kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood and her face doing something Maren didn't have a name for yet. She'd been told, after, that he'd left. That people sometimes did. Her mother had never said more than this, and when she died two winters later there was no one left to ask.

She had told herself for five years that he had abandoned them. She'd built a whole private architecture of bitterness around it, well-maintained, load-bearing. It had been useful. It had kept her from a different and harder question.

What she remembered of him was small and specific: lamp oil and the sharper bite of something metallic, and his hands by firelight, turning some small bright thing with the patience of a man for whom patience was a tool like any other. A gear, maybe, or a catch-pin — something that wanted coaxing into place, something that would either work or wouldn't and required a kind of faith in the trying. She could not remember what the bright thing was. She remembered the turning.

He had also been bloodless. She had always known this and had not let herself think about what it meant. He had also never shown the change. He had also, when she was young, been careful in ways she hadn't understood as careful at the time. The way he sat at the Rite. The way he held his arm.

He had left on a windy night. He had left without waking her.

She sat against the wall and she let the different question in.

---

She got up and began to pack. She did not take very long. Three years of knowing this might happen had made her efficient about it in the abstract, and she found that in practice her hands knew what her mind had planned.

The coin purse. The small knife Delia had given her for cutting herbs, which was good steel and balanced well. Two changes of clothes, rolled tight. The dried herbs she'd been collecting since summer in a leather wrap — things useful on a road, things she knew. Her mother's comb, which she had kept for reasons she'd never examined.

She stood at the kitchen shelf for a moment. Delia kept her money in an old crock behind the salt-box. There was more there than Maren had in her purse. She thought about it. She picked up the coin purse and found, under it, the small worn pouch she used to carry the charcoal, emptied now, just leather. She put it in her pocket. It smelled like Delia's kitchen, like drying things, like fennel and coltsfoot and the sharp ghost of last week's pressing.

She left the crock where it was.

She went out through the back, through the kitchen garden and the low gate, onto the path that ran north along the ridge. Three moons were up — all of them, which happened sometimes, their combined light enough to make shadows. The plateau stretched out pale and enormous in that light, the basalt ridges black and jagged against a sky that went on and on.

She walked north.

She did not look at the moons for long. She had always been vaguely unsettled by them without knowing why, and she did not have time for vague things. She kept her eyes on the path and walked at the pace that could be maintained indefinitely, the long ground-eating step she'd developed doing Delia's herb rounds, and she thought about what she knew and what she was going to need to do with it.

She knew: the deacon had seen. She knew: the bloodline was to be told. She did not know what that meant, not precisely, except that Delia had cried in the way of someone who could not prevent a thing, and Delia was not a woman who cried at things that could be prevented.

She knew her father had been bloodless. She knew he had left quickly and without waking her. She knew he had never come back and no one had heard from him, which she had filed for five years under abandonment and was now filing under something else, something without a name yet, that sat in her chest like a coal that had gone from warm to hot.

The path curved east at the top of the ridge. She kept north, off the path, through the high grass. The wind was in her face. The grass bent around her like it was trying to direct her somewhere.

She thought: I have been hollow my whole life and it has never mattered before. Something has decided it matters now.

She thought: I want to know why.

She walked. The moons moved in their slow courses. The plateau fell away behind her and the scrubland opened up, and somewhere ahead, past the basalt country and the long descent, was a road.
