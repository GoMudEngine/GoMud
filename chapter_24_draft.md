## Chapter Twenty-Four — *Davan*
### The Country Changes

---

The fold was colder at first light than it had been in the night — the
way stone cold went, getting further into itself in the small hours and
taking until the sun cleared the rise to admit it was morning. Davan
woke before anyone but Vane. Vane was up already, a shape against the
dry-stone wall where she had been on second watch, her hands inside her
cuffs the way she had held them at the fire.

She did not look at him when his eyes opened. She was making a short
efficient motion at her wrist with her other hand — tucking a sleeve up
over a bit of something that was nobody's business but hers — and she
finished the motion before she registered him. The sleeve came down.
Her hands went back inside the cuffs.

Something had come off her skin and settled on the cuff. Pale. A flake.
Smaller than a thumbnail. He saw it and looked past it and did not know
he had seen it until later, when the shape of it was already in his
carving hand.

He rolled his blanket. He stood up. His shoulders knew the shape of
sleep on cold ground and complained of it without making much of the
complaint.

Across the fold, Aldric was crouched by the ash of the fire, testing
with a fingertip whether the coals would come back under a handful of
dry grass. They would. He had put grass ready the night before. Davan
watched him work and thought, not for the first time since the basement,
that there was a man who knew how to be useful without being in the way.

---

They broke camp in the old-motion manner of a party that had not been
one long enough to have bad habits yet.

Edvar harnessed the horses, one shoulder into the off-side leather
because his left was stiff from the bench the day before. Aldric rolled
Maren's tarp with her while she stowed the fire stones. Vane walked the
perimeter of the fold once, then once again on a line a hundred paces
further out. Davan stowed the water-skins. He put his hand against the
wheel-hub as he passed the cart, because the touch told him whether the
iron was warm or cold, and cold was the right answer at this hour.

He caught himself at it. He did not name what the catching felt like.
It was too early in the day to be naming anything.

The country east of the fold began as upland pasture and did not stay
upland pasture for long. By the time the cart was moving it was
already a different country.

---

He walked at Edvar's off-wheel for the first hour.

They had fallen into the same loose line as the day before. Vane
ahead, on foot. Aldric and the bay just behind her, the horse bearing
nothing but tack and the small sealed letter-case. The cart in the
middle, Edvar on the bench. Maren on the off-side; Davan on the near
side at the wheel.

The road climbed gently. Stone broke through the grass in bigger
patches now. The walls along the field margins went from field-clearing
walls, wide and rubble-filled, to older walls — narrower, tighter-fitted,
built by hands that had cared about the fit and had not been in a hurry.

He ran a finger along one of them at a gate as they passed. The stones
were cold. The cold went into his fingertip and did not travel. Old
stone did not give warmth back. It took warmth away slowly and politely
and handed it down into the earth.

He walked on.

Maybe it was —

He did not finish the thought. It was too early. The shape had been
under his fingertip for the length of a touch, no longer.

---

By mid-morning the pasture was behind them.

The country opened out into the beginning of the eastern highlands.
Not yet heath. An upland of coarse tussock and bracken gone copper with
the season, grass combed in long pale sheets by the wind, rock breaking
the surface in slow irregular swells — bedrock, not scattered boulders,
the land's own bones coming up through the thin soil that covered them.

And the contour lines thickening.

Not in any map-sense. He had not looked at a map this morning. He meant
it in his body. The rises were closer together, and the ground under
his boots was not field-ground any more — it was the long muscled
shoulder of a country shaped by something larger than weather.

The wind came off the ridge ahead and brought no city in it. No fish.
No coal-smoke. Only dry grass, cold rock, and far underneath all of it
a note he did not know how to place — not a smell, exactly. A register.

His skin at the wrists did the slow thing it did when he had not named
what his skin was doing. The collar of his shirt had opened with the
stride, and the skin between the collar and his jaw had a greenish
cast. Not the green-gold of the night in Edvar's back room. A quieter
shade. Close to the skin. Not reaching for anything.

He did not cover it. Covering made it worse. He walked on at the pace
of the cart.

---

At the first short stop — the road bending around the foot of a tor
and opening a long sightline east for Vane to stand in — Davan went to
Maren.

The cart had been between them most of the morning. He had not minded.
There was a working rhythm to their not being in each other's pockets
that had been established in New Plymouth, and he had no interest in
breaking it. He came over now because she was holding her hand inside
her coat again, and he had been watching, without meaning to, the way
the hand went in and out of the coat across the morning.

She turned her face toward him as he stopped at her elbow. Her eyes
went to his collar, then back to the road.

"Warmer?" he said.

"Twice. Once before dawn. Once an hour ago."

"Same as yesterday, then. At the pulsing."

"A bit more. Warmer when it pulses."

He nodded. They stood a moment.

"There's something —" Davan said.

He let the word sit. Maren did not ask him to finish it. She was
reading his collar for him, because he was evidently not able to read
it for himself, and she nodded once, acknowledging what she saw, and
did not comment.

"Later," he said.

"All right."

He went back to the off-wheel. Her hand went back into the coat. The
cart began to roll. Vane came down off the tor and took her place at
the head of the line, and the country opened east, and he walked into
it with his skin doing its slow held green and the note underneath.

---

It was not a human note.

He had, for nine months now, been reading emotional residue in rooms —
the leavings of bodies that had felt something, the way a room could
smell of anger after the angry person had gone, the way the back bench
of a chapel held grief soaked into the wood. This was not that. This
was older. This was not human at all.

The road was old. The wall-stones along the road were older. The
bedrock under the wall-stones was older than either. What he was
reading was older than the bedrock — not in a geological sense, because
geological sense was not something his body had any instrument for, but
in a register sense. The way his proximity-sense had worked in the
cooperage with Corvel's container, the way it had worked in Edvar's
back room with the face — only bigger now, diffused across the ground,
and *under his feet.*

Near noon, he stopped at the verge and put his palm flat on an exposed
slab of rock. The rock was cold. The cold went into his palm the way
the stone at the wall had. But beneath the cold of the surface, not
quite reaching him and not quite failing to, was — the faintest —
something the stone did not have on its own account. A carrying. The
rock was carrying a thing that was not the rock.

He took his hand away. He walked on.

He did not tell Maren. He would tell her. He was circling. There was a
kind of saying that came from the circling, when the circling had gone
around enough times that the sentence came out clean, and he was not
there yet.

The skin at his wrists stayed green. Low. Steady.

---

They stopped at midday at a place Edvar had picked — a stone outcrop
by the verge that served as a table for travelers, a worn groove in its
top from generations of packs set down on it. A stream went past below.
Vane walked down, drank from her cupped hand, and walked back up
without turning her back to the road.

Edvar got down. Aldric unbuckled the bay's brow-band and let her ease
her head. Maren set her pack at the foot of the stone. She looked at
Davan across it, and she looked at Aldric, and something in her
decision-making went quiet in the way he had learned to recognize from
the cooperage basement.

She reached into the inside pocket of her coat. She took out the disc.
She set it on the stone.

The stone was light gray, pocked from weather, and the disc against it
was the dark it was — the dark that had taken the lamplight in Edvar's
back room and kept it, the dark that had stopped the lamplight at the
cooperage table. In daylight the effect was different. Under lamps the
disc had looked like an absence inside a ring of light. Out here it
looked like a window cut into the stone into a place the daylight did
not reach.

Aldric went still.

Davan had seen him go still in the basement — the hand that had not
closed the short distance, the hand that had stopped itself a handspan
away. This was the same stillness. It was the stillness of a man whose
body had just been shown the thing his body had travelled a thousand
miles to come near, and whose body was choosing not to move because
moving was not the answer yet.

Maren did not ask. She had asked once, two nights ago. She did not ask
twice. She went down to the stream to fill a skin.

Vane, at the upper edge of the outcrop, saw this arrangement and did
not comment. She moved thirty paces further up the road, took her
stand at a boulder, and watched east. Edvar took a piece of bread
out of the cart and broke it, and handed half to Davan without looking
at him, and sat on the cart step with his own half and ate slowly.

Aldric crossed to the stone.

He did not kneel. He leaned. His hands went flat against the top of
the stone on either side of the disc, not touching it, the way a man's
hands went flat against a lectern, and he looked at the disc at the
exact distance a man looked at the page on a lectern, and he looked at
it for a long time.

Davan had finished his bread before Aldric spoke.

---

"The inner orbit symbol," Aldric said.

His voice was level. It had the precise level of a voice that had held
a sentence for a very long time and had decided, now, to let it out in
the shape it had been kept in. Davan heard the shape of it — long
careful clauses, institutional vocabulary — and underneath the shape he
heard the other thing. A second voice under the first. A stumbling in
the places where the institutional words wanted to give way.

"I have been counting," Aldric said, "what it describes. For months."

He did not take his eyes off the disc. Edvar stopped chewing. Maren
was just coming back up the path from the stream, the skin heavy in
her hand, and she saw the shape of the moment and stopped at the edge
of it and did not step in.

"The moons," Aldric said. "Three in the sky. The fourth on the disc.
Four arcs. Three stations and a — " the word caught. He let the
catching stand. " — and a descent." He said the word like a man who
had known for weeks it was the right word and had only just now said
it aloud. "The fourth arc drifts off. I had taken it for damage in the
engraving. I do not take it for damage now."

"It was meant to drift," Davan said quietly.

"Yes."

"Drawn that way on purpose. The symbol is *before.* Before the —"

"Before the descent."

"Yes."

Aldric closed his eyes briefly.

When he opened them he was looking at Maren, not at the disc, and his
hands had lifted from the stone and folded themselves at his waist in
the small involuntary gesture of a man whose hands had remembered, for
a second, that they had been trained to fold. The folding looked
awkward on him in plain clothes. He saw that it looked awkward, and he
did not unfold, because unfolding would have been another thing.

"I have a thing to tell you," he said. "All of you. That I have been
holding." He glanced at Edvar. "Some of it Marrick knows. Some I have
not said."

Maren set the waterskin on the stone and sat on her pack. Edvar leaned
against the cart wheel with his bread in his hand. Vane did not come
in from her boulder. She did not need to. A voice pitched at the stone
would reach her, and her eyes would stay east.

Aldric spoke.

---

"There is a chamber," he said, "in the undercroft of the Temple of
Confluence. I found it eight weeks before I rode for Greenford."

He said *eight weeks* like a man giving testimony. A scholar gave a
duration that way; a priest gave a confession that way; and Aldric was
giving it both ways at once.

"Sealed for — I do not know how long. Long. The stone around the door
was cut before the temple above it. The door opened on a small room.
And in the room were five objects."

He paused.

"Four containers. Smooth. Dark. The same dark as the disc. The same
dark as —" he inclined his head minimally toward Edvar, and Edvar
nodded minimally back, and the acknowledgment moved between them in
the quiet shorthand of men who had spoken some of this already. "— as
other objects the group has seen. They stood against the wall in a row
like — " he paused again. "Like patient urns."

The phrase had lived in his head for months. He had never said it aloud.
Saying it now was itself a small crossing he did not take back.

"And the fifth object was a face."

Maren's hand, at her knee, did the small involuntary reaching he had
seen her hand do in the cooperage — a half-inch of motion and then
stillness. She brought the hand back onto her thigh.

"Flat," Aldric said. "Dark. The size of a large serving tray. A
surface that answered to a hand laid upon it. I laid my hand upon it
eight weeks ago and it — " the liturgical word came up into his mouth
before he could catch it, and he let it come because it was the word
he had, and because they would hear the other word under it " — it
*showed me.*"

He was speaking to all of them now.

"An arrangement of bodies. Four of them. Around a world — blue, green,
with cloud. A star at the center burning. The three near bodies each
with their mass. And the fourth —"

He stopped. He was looking at Davan now. He had heard something, or
seen something, that had turned him toward Davan at the word *fourth,*
and his pause was the pause of a man who had not expected another voice
to be ready.

Davan's skin at the throat was green-gold.

Not pulsing. Sustained. The color it had gone to in Edvar's back room
over the face with the lamp burning down by half. He had not felt it
arrive. It had arrived while Aldric spoke, at the count of the three,
as if his body had been running the count with Aldric and had reached
the fourth at the same beat.

He did not cover it.

"Edvar has one too," he heard himself say.

It came out flat. It came out plain. He had been circling it all
morning under his ribs, and the circling had come around.

Aldric's expression did not change very much. It adjusted slowly, the
way a man's expression adjusted when the new information was the
information he had half-expected without letting himself know he was
expecting it.

"A face."

"A face."

"The same kind."

"The same kind."

Aldric breathed out, once, very carefully.

"Show me what yours showed," he said to Davan, and then with a small
correcting turn of the head to Edvar. "If you have a way. I know the
faces do not travel easily."

"Mine is in the trunk," Edvar said. "The trunk is in the cart. I
brought the trunk."

---

They did not open the trunk at the outcrop.

Vane came in from her boulder and said, quietly, without heat, "We are
on a road. It is midday. People use this road."

Aldric nodded. Edvar nodded. Maren nodded. The trunk would be opened
at camp. The business of the road came back. The horses were watered.
The cart moved off. Aldric mounted the bay for the first time all
morning — not as a claim on the head of the line, but because Edvar
had said, over the cart wheel, that the afternoon was long, and
Aldric's knees were older than his pride. Aldric had laughed once, a
thin surprised laugh, and had mounted.

They rode and walked east into the afternoon.

---

The trunk was opened at camp.

Camp was a cart-stand, a place used by carters through the highlands
for centuries, set into the lee of a stand of ash. The ash trees were
bare. Their branches were north-bent, the way the hawthorns at the
fold had been. The country had a way of telling you which way the wind
lived.

Vane walked the perimeter. Twice. Wider each time. She came in from
the second circuit and did not say anything, and her not saying
anything meant the same thing as the morning: no one on the back, long
sightline, clear.

Edvar brought the trunk down from the cart bed with Davan's help.
Aldric held a lamp. Maren sat cross-legged on a blanket against the
cart wheel, her coat over her knees, her hand inside her coat where
the disc had gone back at noon and had stayed.

Edvar turned the key on the cord inside his shirt.

The face came out of the oiled canvas the way it had come out in the
back room — flat, dark, light going in and not coming out. Aldric made
a small sound, the sound a man made when his breath caught on a word
and let it go without the word coming.

The green-gold Davan had carried through the afternoon had faded into
low blue by the time they reached camp, the way a color faded when the
body had held a thing too long. Now, with the face on the canvas in
the lamp-light, it came back. Throat. Collarbones. Wrists.

Edvar set the face on the flat of the blanket and looked at Aldric.

"You want to," Edvar said.

"No," Aldric said. "I want Davan to. This is his."

Davan knelt and put his fingers on the surface. The surface lit, cold
and pale and precise, spreading from his fingertips, and the image
assembled itself in the small circle of lamplight and cold pre-crash
glow — the blue-green world, the three near shapes with their masses,
and the fourth.

Smaller. Further. Distant.

The distance of the fourth was the thing his body had gone to both
times he had seen it — the far-away quality of a point that was almost
a shape, something barely resolved, whole at the limit of what the
face could render.

Aldric looked at it for a long time.

"Mine showed four," Aldric said at last. "And a burning point. And the
world."

"Yes."

"The near three — these three."

"These three."

"And the fourth." He did not finish the sentence. He looked up at
Davan. "Yours shows it —"

"— smaller. Further. Distant." Davan said the words as he had said
them to Edvar. "That is what I saw."

Aldric nodded.

He did not say what his face had shown him of the fourth. Davan did
not ask. The asking was not for tonight. The asking would come —
tomorrow, the day after, at some hour the group could do the asking —
but tonight the two accounts were on the blanket together and the
accounts were enough. Two faces. Two renderings. The fourth shape was
present in both, and the how of it could keep until there was light to
see it by.

What landed in him, instead, was the other thing.

---

"Brennan's letter," Aldric said. "There was a phrase. *For unchanged
hands.*"

Maren did not move. She was listening.

"The surveyor," Aldric said. "Reth. Who walked a formation in the
eastern highlands forty years ago — a metallic formation, four hundred
meters across, no grain or seam, manufactured. With a recess at its
southeastern end. A recess shaped like a coin. With the inner orbit
symbol inside it."

He had delivered that sentence in the cooperage. Davan had heard it
then. It did different work now. On a road east, under a lamp, over
two faces laid on a blanket, the sentence became what it had not been
in the basement: *architecture.*

"And Brennan's survey," Aldric said, "of the ground immediately east
of the Temple of Confluence. There is a quality to the bedrock there
that he — " Aldric reached for a scholar's word and visibly chose
another one. "That he has been afraid of. For years. Metallic
signatures at depth where metallic signatures should not be. Not the
formation Reth walked. A smaller — a scatter. Under the temple. Under
the lands adjacent. As though something —"

He stopped. He looked at Davan. He had heard himself approaching a
sentence a cartographer had been unable to finish for twenty years,
and the hesitation — Davan read it — was not theological. Courtesy,
rather.

Davan finished it quietly.

"As though something came apart in the coming down," he said. "And a
piece of it went where it went. And another piece of it — pieces of it
— went elsewhere."

"Yes."

"The temple is built on a piece of it."

"Above a piece of it. But yes."

The silence after that was the silence at the cooperage table after
the disc pulsed, and also not. At the cooperage the silence had been
six faces catching one new thing. Here there were four. At the edge of
the lamp-circle, where the ring of light failed and the ash-bark
began, something moved in the dark — Vane, circling — and the silence
extended to the dark and the dark extended to the silence and the
country underneath it all carried its carried thing in its cold slow
register and said nothing because it had not been asked a question.

Davan's skin at the collarbones stayed green-gold. He did not try to
make it do anything else. The country carried what it carried, and his
body carried what his body carried, and for a long moment the two of
them were simply the same body.

---

Edvar shut the trunk eventually. Slowly, one knee at a time, the old
negotiation. The face had been out for perhaps twenty minutes. Enough.
The pale glow bled from the canvas seam for a while after, which was
why Edvar wrapped the canvas before the wood — the way a man wrapped
a candle when he carried it through a draft.

Aldric sat back on his heels. Then he stood and pinched something at
his coat that was not a lamp wick and let the fingers fall. He caught
the fingers at the fall, this time. He set them flat against his thigh.
His face did the small work of a man being honest with his hand about
what it had been about to do.

Maren did not comment.

Vane came into the light and sat at the edge of the blanket. She did
not look at the trunk. She looked at her hands inside her cuffs.

"You'll want watches," she said. "I'll take first. Aldric second.
Davan third."

"The dead hour," Aldric said.

"I'll take the dead hour. I am awake in the dead hour."

Nobody argued with her. Maren looked at Davan. Davan looked back. The
look was not an exchange. It was an acknowledgment. There was no
sentence any of them had yet that would do the day's work in summary,
and they did not reach for the sentence. For now the watch was set,
and the face was in the trunk, and the trunk was in the cart, and the
cart was in the lee of the ash.

---

He did not sleep for an hour.

He lay on his back with his coat over him and the hawthorn piece from
the night before in his hand, and he took the knife out of his belt
and carved.

He did not try to see what he was carving. He let his hands work. He
had been doing this on the road north; he had been doing it in the
docks at lunch; he had been doing it in Edvar's front room in
quarter-hours between conversations. His hands would find the shape
they found, and then he would look down.

Tonight the shape the hands found was a piece of ground.

Not a shape he had carved before. Not the symbol — the inner orbit
symbol had come out of his hands so many times in the last year he
could have carved it in the dark and had. This was a different thing.
A low slow rise. A shoulder of earth. The top of a thing you would not
know was a thing unless you had walked on it. A hill that the woodgrain
said was hollow under it — he had gone against the grain with his
knife-cut at the top, so the fibre ran straight down inside the shape,
and the straight-down of the grain made the top of the rise look like
it had something beneath it that was not the rise.

He looked at it in his palm.

He had carved a buried thing.

He had carved it without deciding to, and he had carved it with the
note under the country working in his wrists all day. The piece of
hawthorn was not the hill east of New Plymouth — the hill was five
days and he was on day two — but the shape of what his body had been
reading along the road was in his hand now, small, four-fingers long,
the wood a pale hawthorn-yellow in the lamp-edge light.

He set it on the blanket beside him.

The green-gold at his collarbones dimmed, at last, to the low steady
blue of a body at rest. Across the camp Vane was upright at first
watch, her shape against the ash. Maren was already asleep, one hand
inside her coat. Edvar had his mouth open in the way of men his age
asleep and did not care who saw. Aldric was in his blanket with his
eyes closed and was not yet asleep, his hand palm-down on his thigh,
the way he had laid it after the disc on the stone.

Davan closed his hand around the carved piece. The hawthorn was cold
on one side where the air had reached it and warm on the other side
where his palm had been. He kept his palm against the cold side until
the cold side was not cold any more, and he held it there past the
warming — past the point where holding did anything — because the
country under the camp was cold in a way that a palm could not warm,
and the hawthorn in his hand was a small thing, and the smallness of
it was what he had to give.

He did not finish the thought. He held the hawthorn piece against his
ribs under the coat, and the wood went on taking the warmth out of him
the way old stone took it out of a hand at a gate, slowly and
politely, and after a while his hand was on the carved thing and the
carved thing was against his heart and the country was under all of
it and did not ask anything of him at all.
